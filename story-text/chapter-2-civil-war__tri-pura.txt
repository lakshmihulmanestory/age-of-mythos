Agartala. The Ujjayanta Palace's east administrative wing. The first Wednesday of June. Eleven a.m. The inter-city water-rights committee's third quarterly meeting of the year.

The three city-chairs are silent for about fifteen seconds. The resolution is technically correct. The remaining five pages set out the technical specifications. Tripura-Asura reads the proposal in approximately ninety minutes. He approves it without amendments. The sabotage is scheduled for nine p.m. on the second Saturday of June. Week six of the chapter. Twenty-eight days after the water-rights committee meeting.

The second-monsoon-week storm reaches central Tripura at five-thirty-seven p.m. on the second Saturday of June. Wind-speeds at the gust-peak are recorded at one hundred and forty-three kilometres per hour by the Agartala Meteorological Station's primary anemometer. Rainfall in the first three hours is two hundred and eighty-seven millimetres — the highest three-hour rainfall recorded in central Tripura in the past forty-one years.

The self-repair algorithms are triggered across all three cities at six-fifteen p.m. by the storm's wind-and-rain stress-loads. The algorithms operate normally for the first two hours and forty-three minutes — redistributing structural load, sealing minor leaks, retracting and re-extending the Ujjayanta Palace's eastern-bridge spans to match the changing wind-vectors.

At eight-fifty-eight p.m. the signal-injection begins. Tri-Yantra-Bheda's operations-cell injects the reverse-stress signal at three simultaneous insertion-points: the Agartala Lake Rudrasagar pipeline's Sub-Pump-Three (the same Sub-Pump-Three whose anomalous readings Apurba Saha had reported to the Bangladesh-side intermediary twenty-eight days earlier), the Udaipur central-market water-cistern's primary inflow-valve, the Dharmanagar rubber-warehouse's industrial water-cooling intake. The signal propagates through the kingdom's pressure-monitoring sensor-network in approximately eleven seconds.

The self-repair algorithms begin operating in reverse at nine p.m. The Ujjayanta Palace's eastern-bridge spans retract instead of extending. The Bhubaneswari Temple's outer-courtyard walls release their self-anchoring locks instead of tightening them. The Dharmanagar rubber-warehouse's structural-cross-bracing extends instead of contracting under storm-load. The water-system back-pressures into the storm drains and floods the foundations.

The collapses begin at nine-oh-three p.m. They occur in seventeen-minute sequence across the three cities:

9:03 p.m. — Agartala central-Ujjayanta-Palace administrative-block primary support-truss fails. The administrative-block (where Tripurari is at the engineering-research-cell weekly review-session) collapses inward.

9:09 p.m. — Udaipur central-market jute-trade godowns collapse southward as the temple's outer-courtyard wall's self-anchoring lock release propagates through the adjacent civic-cistern structure.

9:14 p.m. — Dharmanagar rubber-warehouse central facility collapses outward as its cross-bracing extends past structural limits, taking down the Border-Trade Consortium's adjacent trade-office building and the Refugee-Resettlement office.

9:20 p.m. — Agartala Bharat-Yantra engineering institute's eastern wing collapses inward as the load-redistribution from the Ujjayanta-Palace collapse propagates through the institute's shared structural-support system.

The seventeen-minute window covers the four primary collapse-events. The secondary collapses — partial wall-failures, adjacent-structure damage, water-system flooding — continue for the next nine hours under the ongoing storm. The crossbow will not be reassembled in his lifetime; he will repair it when its components age, will teach the assembly to apprentices at the hermitage, will display the assembled crossbow once a year at the Margin-hermitage's autumn engineering-festival for visiting students, but he will never fire it.

He carries one small water-skin, one bag of rice and dal, one wool blanket. He carries no money — the Margins do not use money. He carries no notebook — the Margins do not write down what does not need to be remembered.

Triputra is at the gate. He has been there since four a.m. He has not slept the previous night. He is in his new ceremonial cotton, white with the kingdom-council's senior-chair sash. He looks nineteen and tired and like he is trying to be more than nineteen.

Tripurari stops at the gate. He sets down the satchel. He places his right palm on Triputra's right shoulder.

He says, in plain Bengali: "The engineering you know is the kingdom's. The poetry you know is also the kingdom's. You will need them both. The engineering will rebuild the cities. The poetry will keep the rebuilding from substituting for the politics again. When you feel the engineering eating the politics, write a poem. When you feel the poetry hiding from the engineering, build a bridge. The two will hold each other. The two are what I did not have. You have them. You will be the kingdom's hope. You will not be the kingdom's hero. The kingdom does not need a hero in Volume One. The kingdom needs you. The kingdom has me too — from the Margins, not from the throne. The Sarus-pigeon network will carry our correspondence. I will write at every full moon. You will write back at every new moon. The correspondence will be the kingdom's permanent inter-Margin-and-Throne record. The record will be archived at the Bhubaneswari Temple's administrative wing under Anjali Debbarma's seal. The temple will outlast both of us. The correspondence will outlast the temple if it is written honestly enough. Write honestly. I will. Walk now. I have nineteen days' walking ahead of me."

He lifts the satchel. He turns. He walks through the western gate.

Triputra watches him for as long as he is visible. The walk west goes through the palace-garden's now-cleared western edge, past the cremation-site where the langur-clan was burned, past the Mahua-tree grove where the pigeon-and-stone-egg incident has occurred (Triputra has not yet been informed of the Maw sign; the wildlife-rehabilitation officer will report it to him at the council's afternoon session). Tripurari does not stop at the Mahua-tree. He walks past it at the standard one-point-three-metres-per-second pace.

By six a.m. he is past the Agartala western city-limit. He continues west.

Triputra returns to the kingdom-council's open-air session at one p.m. He chairs the afternoon's business. The session opens with the day's mutual-aid coordination report. The report has six items.

Item one: the cross-kingdom helicopter-and-ground-convoy rescue operations are formally concluded as of day twenty-three. Total survivors recovered: 1,124. Total confirmed deaths across all three cities: 2,387. Missing-and-presumed-dead: 387.

Item two: West Bengal's Bilash-Babu has formalised the structural-engineering compact for Agartala's rebuilding. The compact runs for ten years, renewable. The compact provides senior engineering staff, structural-survey equipment, and a rotating cohort of West Bengal engineering students who will spend six-month rotations at Agartala under the institute's mentorship.

Item three: Assam's Kamalavarna has formalised the Bhubaneswari Temple administration-compact for Udaipur. The compact provides priestly cross-training between Kamakhya and Bhubaneswari, mutual-recognition of ceremonial qualifications, and a rotating Kamakhya delegation that will support the Bhubaneswari temple-cycle during the rebuilding.

Item four: Mizoram's Hmangaihi-and-Lalruatpuia have formalised the Aizawl-Dharmanagar refugee-resettlement compact. The compact integrates the Chakma and Tripuri refugee-administration with the Mizoram State Civil Service's parallel programmes. The compact is the saga's first formal cross-kingdom refugee-administration-mutual-aid arrangement.

Item five: Manipur's Moirangthem has sent a formal NE-Federation observer-status invitation. Tripura's membership in the federation will be ratified at the Chapter Three Sanctuary convening. Triputra will attend the Sanctuary as Tripura's formal representative; Vyasa-of-the-Margins will not attend (the Margins do not participate in federation-level political bodies); the kingdom's pigeon-correspondence with Vyasa will be the kingdom's standing private-counsel channel through Chapter Three and Chapter Four.

Item six: the pigeon-and-stone-egg incident at the Mahua-tree grove. The wildlife-rehabilitation officer's report is read aloud. The council requests a forensic-analysis report from the Agartala District Wildlife Office within seventy-two hours. The stone egg is taken into evidence and preserved at the zoological-museum sub-collection.

Triputra concludes the afternoon session at five p.m. He returns to the palace's south-edge engineering-workshop (his standing workspace; the central-Agartala administrative-block is gone). He sits at his desk. He has been the kingdom's throne-holder for five hours. He is nineteen.

He picks up a pencil. He writes, in Bengali, in his engineering-margin handwriting, on the back of a structural-survey blueprint: "The bridge father did not build — the bridge between engineering and the people — I will build. I do not yet know how. I will start with one beam at a time. The beam is the council. The next beam is the poetry. The third beam is the correspondence. The fourth beam is the kingdom's admission that we are smaller now and that smaller is the kingdom's honest answer to having been too large before."

He sets down the pencil. He goes to find Bharati Das and Sumit Bhattacharjee for the evening engineering-recovery briefing.

Day twenty-five. Five-fifteen a.m. The Ujjayanta Palace garden's western Mahua tree.

The Mahua tree is approximately seventy years old. Its main branches were untouched by the central-Agartala collapse — the garden-wall fell south of the tree, the administrative-block collapsed east of the tree, the tree's western position kept it outside the primary collapse-zone. The wildlife-rehabilitation officer (a Tripuri woman named Sunita Reang, forty-six, eight-year senior officer at the Agartala District Wildlife Office) has been at the tree every dawn since day eleven, monitoring the surviving palace-grove fauna.

This morning a Green Imperial Pigeon — mature female, banded by the wildlife-office in 2022 as part of the kingdom-wide imperial-pigeon population-monitoring programme, identifier TR-IMP-1141 — arrives at the Mahua tree's lower-eastern branch. She has been at the same nest-site for six consecutive breeding-seasons. Her mate (TR-IMP-1142) was last sighted on day three of the chapter's recovery operations and has not returned; he is presumed dead in the eastern collapse-zone.

She lays an egg at five-eighteen a.m. The egg is not an egg. It is a smooth ovoid of polished green-grey serpentinite stone. Sunita Reang observes the laying from her hide at six metres distance. The stone is approximately the standard imperial-pigeon-egg dimensions — four-point-one centimetres long, three-point-two centimetres at the widest point — but weighs (by Sunita's subsequent weighing at the field-office scales) two hundred and eighty-seven grams. The standard imperial-pigeon egg weighs eighteen to twenty-two grams. The stone egg weighs approximately thirteen times the standard egg.

The nest's branch-anchoring fails under the stone's weight at five-twenty-one a.m. The nest falls. The stone egg lands on the garden-path below intact and uncracked. The fall is approximately four metres; the impact-velocity at the ground would be sufficient to crack an actual imperial-pigeon-egg into many pieces.

The mother pigeon returns to the empty branch at five-twenty-three a.m. She walks the length of the branch where the nest had been. She settles into the empty space. She begins to perform the incubation behaviour — ruffling her feathers, occasional warming-turns, brooding the absent nest. She continues this behaviour for the next fourteen days.

Sunita Reang observes from the same six-metre distance. She does not interfere. She files the incident-report with the kingdom-council's emergency-response cell at eight a.m. on day twenty-five; the report is filed in the standing-correspondence file with a copy to the wildlife-rehabilitation cell's senior officer. She returns to the hide each dawn and dusk for the next two weeks.

On day twenty-five (the first observation morning) the pigeon ruffles her feathers and turns the absent nest at five-thirty a.m., six-fifteen a.m., seven a.m. (standard imperial-pigeon morning incubation-rhythm). She does not eat or drink.

On day thirty (the sixth observation morning) Sunita Reang notes the pigeon's weight loss is visible; her chest-feathers are loose and her eyes are unfocused. The branch-position has not been abandoned. The incubation-behaviour continues at the standard rhythm.

On day thirty-five (the eleventh observation morning) Sunita Reang notes the pigeon's behaviour has become slower and less precise; she is still in the incubation-position; the turns are slower; the feather-ruffling is reduced. She is dying. Sunita does not approach. She has been instructed by the kingdom-council's wildlife-protocol to allow the bird's natural progression to continue.

On day thirty-nine (the fifteenth morning since the laying) Sunita Reang finds the pigeon dead on the branch. She is in the incubation-posture. The branch-position has not been abandoned. The body is intact; rigor has set at the standard four-hour post-mortem rate; the cause of death is starvation-and-dehydration consistent with fifteen-day no-food-no-water continuous brooding.

Sunita Reang collects the body. The body is preserved at the zoological-museum sub-collection in the incubation-posture, alongside the stone egg, in a single display-case logged as "Green Imperial Pigeon (TR-IMP-1141) + serpentinite ovoid, Ujjayanta Palace western Mahua-tree, post-incubation-death at fifteen days, brooding behaviour continuous and unbroken to death." The display-case is not on public display. Only senior kingdom-council members and authorised researchers may view it.

Triputra drafts the cross-kingdom Sarus-pigeon cascade-alert about the pigeon-and-stone-egg sign at three p.m. on day twenty-six (after Sunita Reang's initial report and the first day's observed brooding-behaviour have been confirmed). The alert is the chapter's second cross-kingdom alert by a new throne-holder (the first being Hmangaihi's two-headed-serow alert).

The alert reads, in plain Bengali:

Bonded-kingdom guardians. The Maw has experimented on Tripura's imperial-pigeon line. A Green Imperial Pigeon laid an unhatchable stone egg at the Ujjayanta Palace western Mahua-tree the morning after my father-figure's formal abdication. The egg is serpentinite, thirteen times the standard mass of an imperial-pigeon egg. The nest collapsed under the stone's weight. The stone landed intact on the garden-path below. The mother pigeon has returned to the empty nest-position and is performing the incubation behaviour on the absent nest. The behaviour is continuous. The pigeon is not eating or drinking. The wildlife-rehabilitation officer's assessment is that she will die in incubation-position within ten to fifteen days. The Maw has demonstrated the capacity to make a creature perform an action whose object has been removed — the saga's first stuck-behaviour experiment. The kingdom's reading: the engineer who has built an architecture continues to perform engineering when there is nothing left to engineer. The kingdom must learn the difference between perseverance and stuck-behaviour. Watch your own bonded species for stuck-behaviour patterns. Build shrines at the threshold of the experiment's site. Do not interfere. Name the experiment in writing within seventy-two hours.

By the morning of day twenty-eight every bonded-kingdom senior guardian in the saga has read the alert. Kanchenjunga at Rumtek replies with a single-line acknowledgement and an offer of a Beyul-Kshetra meditation-circle for Tripurari's nineteen-day walk west (the offer is declined; Tripurari has already left and the Margins do not accept formal cross-kingdom support). Udayagiri at Tawang sends a longer reply noting that the Roing Dawn-Shadow Temple's First-Herald-return room is now formally extended to include stuck-behaviour-recovery as a Temple-function. Hmangaihi at Aizawl sends the cross-NE-kingdom note: "Mizoram's two-headed serow named me; Tripura's pigeon brooded the absent. The two experiments are paired: the Maw names the new and mourns the gone in the same season. The Sanctuary will need to address both."

Krishnadevaraya-Bhrata at the buried-temple chamber in Karnataka sends the saga's second cross-region reply (after the two-headed serow cascade-reply): "The Bronze-Dust Child has not spoken across the chapter; the Bronze-Dust Child has also not stopped eating. The Karnataka Herald is neither stuck nor naming. The third position is presence-without-action. The three positions are now catalogued: presence-without-action (Karnataka), future-naming (Mizoram), stuck-behaviour (Tripura). The Maw is mapping the saga's response-space."

By chapter end the three Tripura cities have begun rebuilding under their respective cross-kingdom compacts. Agartala's central-block reconstruction has begun under Bilash-Babu's engineering compact (the new design will not use self-repair-wall technology; the kingdom-council has voted unanimously to prohibit self-repair-architecture in any kingdom-civic-infrastructure for the next twenty-five years). Udaipur's temple-outer-wall is being rebuilt under the Kamakhya-Bhubaneswari joint-rite. Dharmanagar's rubber-warehouse is being rebuilt with Mizoram-engineered structural-bracing that requires manual-only maintenance (the manual-maintenance protocol mirrors the Mizoram bamboo-bend bier's saffron-cord daily-tending).

Tripurari arrives at Vyasa-of-the-Margins' hermitage on day forty-three. He stops walking on day forty-four. He becomes the Margins' standing engineer-apprentice that evening. His Volume I role is now formally as a guru-figure; he will reappear in Chapter Three as the cross-kingdom standing voice on broken-bond integration; he will reappear in Chapter Four at the Kurukshetra Council as the formal Margin-guru.

The kingdom of Tri-Pura is smaller, fragmented, and rebuilding. It is no longer a kingdom that solves political problems with engineering substitutions. It is a kingdom that is learning to hold engineering and poetry in the same hand. The kingdom's nineteen-year-old throne-holder writes the first poem of his reign at midnight on day forty-five. The poem is fourteen lines, in Bengali, in plain unadorned register. The poem's first line, which will be the kingdom's permanent state-motto-after-the-collapse, reads: "The bridge falls; the bridge is rebuilt; the bridge between us is the rebuilding."

The pigeon's body and the stone egg remain at the zoological-museum sub-collection. The Mahua tree is unharmed. The branch where the nest had been is empty. A new imperial-pigeon pair has not yet claimed the branch by chapter end. The kingdom is patient.

The next sign comes within the week.
