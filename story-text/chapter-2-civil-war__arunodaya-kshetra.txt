Tawang. The first Tuesday of September. Five-forty a.m. The eastern ridge above the Tawang Monastery at three thousand four hundred metres altitude. The first eastern light is forming behind the Bhutan-border ranges; the sky is rose at the horizon, deepening to indigo at the zenith.

Udayagiri is at the ridge-stone. He is thirty-one. He is in the dawn-walker robe, knee-length, deep amber, with the gayal-hide sash at his waist. He carries the Uday-spear — the dawn-forged spear his line has carried for four hundred and seven years, re-forged each dawn in the first light's heat. The spear's shaft is hill-bamboo; the head is a thin double-edged copper-alloy blade; the binding is gayal-leather wrapped in twelve turns. He has been at the ridge-stone every dawn for two thousand seven hundred and forty-eight consecutive days.

The first-light arrives at five-forty-three a.m. He turns the spear's blade-edge to the east. The blade catches the first ray. The metal warms in his hand — the daily re-forging that the saga's gayal-bond performs at the moment-of-light. He greets the dawn aloud in Apatani-Adi-Galo — the three-tribe ceremonial trade-tongue that has been Arunodaya-Kshetra's greeting-language for six centuries.

He says, into the morning air: "The dawn cannot be stopped. The kingdom is greeted. The day is begun."

The three-tribe trade-tongue is heard, as it has been heard every morning for six centuries, by approximately four hundred dawn-walkers across the kingdom — one ridge-elder per tribal sub-village — performing the same greeting at the same minute at their own local first-light. The greetings overlap across the time-zone-staggered ridges; the kingdom-net of dawn-walkers covers the state in concentric expanding light-rings from east to west across about thirty-eight minutes (the time it takes the first-light to walk from the Roing eastern ridge at the Mishmi Hills to the Tawang western ridge at the Bhutan border).

Udayagiri completes his greeting at five-forty-five a.m. He returns down the ridge-trail to the monastery's administrative wing where the kingdom's morning-council convenes at six. The morning-council is a thirty-minute briefing with the senior administrative monk Sonam-Lama-Bumla (no relation to Sikkim's Sonam at Rumtek; the name Sonam is common across the Karmapa-and-Tawang lineages), the state-government district magistrate's representative, and the three regional dawn-walker captains (Lower Subansiri, Anjaw, and West Kameng).

At the morning-council Sonam-Lama-Bumla raises a single new agenda-item: "The Tawang night-cult has grown to eleven hundred members. The new membership in the past fortnight is three hundred and twelve. The growth-rate is accelerating, not stabilising. The fourteenth-month threshold the night-cult appears to have set has been crossed. We must address it at the kingdom-level. Today."

Udayagiri sets down the tea-cup he had just lifted to his mouth. He has been hearing about the night-cult for fourteen months. He has approached it the way he approaches every shadow that forms inside the kingdom: more dawn, more first-light walks, more institutional outreach. He has personally led six morning-walks for war-trauma survivors over the past year. The morning-walks have helped no one. The walkers have politely attended; some have cried at the first-light moment; none have returned.

The night-cult has been growing, in those fourteen months, by approximately fifteen new members per week. The eleven-hundred-member threshold means the cult is now larger than five of the kingdom's twenty-six recognised village-clusters.

He says, into the council chamber: "The Comforter. Has he been seen. Confirmed seen."

Sonam-Lama-Bumla says: "Three times in the last six weeks. The Sela Pass shadow-zone. Always in the late afternoon, two hours before sunset, when the pass-shadow is at maximum extent. Always speaking to the Galwan-veterans first — the 2020 survivors. Then the Tawang-clash bereaved, the 2022 families. Then the Ziro forest-fire burned, the 2024 survivors. The pattern is consistent. He is finding the wounds the kingdom's dawn-walks have not reached."

Udayagiri says: "Description. Voice. Build. Gait."

Sonam-Lama-Bumla says: "Forty-four. Tall. Soft-voiced. Speaks Hindi and Apatani-Adi-Galo and what one of the older clash-veterans described as 'cave-Tibetan' — the dialect spoken in the deep-cave-system the kingdom's northern border touches. Walks slowly. Sits with people. Listens for hours before speaking. When he speaks the listeners report a sense of being seen. The sense is the cult's primary recruitment-vehicle. They are not joining a doctrine. They are joining a man who finally heard them."

Udayagiri does not respond for thirty seconds.

He says: "Is he Asta-Asura."

Sonam-Lama-Bumla says: "The cave-Tibetan dialect is the indicator. So is the listening-pause. So is the height. The kingdom has not seen Asta-Asura since the cave-system battle nine years ago. The description matches."

Asta-Asura is, by the kingdom's oldest dawn-walker commentary tradition, Udayagiri's own shadow split off at the moment of his birth. He was born — that is, came into separate existence — in the first second of Udayagiri's first dawn-greeting at the Tawang ridge thirty-one years ago, when the infant Udayagiri's shadow cast eastward by the rising sun did not retract when the sun cleared the ridgeline. The shadow remained. It walked away. It grew. By the time Udayagiri was nine years old the shadow was a fully separate person, living in the deep-cave-system that runs beneath the Bhutan-Arunachal border, ten kilometres north of the Tawang valley.

The shadow had a face. The face was Udayagiri's face inverted — the same bone-structure, but the planes of light and dark reversed. Where Udayagiri's cheek caught the morning sun, Asta-Asura's cheek was in deeper shadow. Where Udayagiri's eye reflected the dawn, Asta-Asura's eye absorbed it.

In Chapter One Udayagiri had not fought Asta-Asura directly. The Ch 1 antagonist had been the elder sister Tamasi, taken from the family at age three by raiders from across the northern border, raised in the cave-system that had become Asta-Asura's territory. Tamasi's suffering and Asta-Asura's growth had occurred along the same cave-system but on different lineage-axes — she by human trauma, he by metaphysical detachment. At the Chapter One climax Tamasi had retreated deeper into the cave-system with the surviving cave-dwellers; Asta-Asura had vanished from sight altogether, his location unconfirmed for nine years.

He has now reappeared at the Tawang night-cult's recruitment-zone in the form of the Comforter. The reappearance is the chapter's first event.

Week two of the chapter. Tawang Sela-Pass shadow-zone — the wide afternoon-shadow that the four-thousand-one-hundred-metre Sela ridge casts down into the upper Tawang valley from about three p.m. to sunset. The shadow-zone is roughly four kilometres long and two kilometres wide. The temperature inside the shadow is approximately seven degrees Celsius cooler than the sunlit valley floor.

The night-cult meets at the centre of the shadow-zone at four p.m. on weekdays. The meeting-place is a low wooden platform — about eight metres by six — constructed by the cult members over the past year out of timber recovered from the 2024 Ziro forest-fire's burn-zone. The platform is the cult's only physical structure. It has no walls, no roof. The shadow is the only ceiling. The cult does not call the platform a temple; they call it the listening-floor.

Udayagiri walks to the listening-floor at four-thirty p.m. on the twelfth day of the chapter. He walks alone. He does not carry the Uday-spear (he has left it at the monastery; carrying it into the shadow-zone would be a hostile statement and he is not yet making one). He is in plain Adi-tribe wool with no insignia. He has dressed deliberately. He does not want to be seen as the kingdom's representative; he wants to be seen as a member of the kingdom who has questions.

The cult's outer ring — about three hundred members today, the weekday-low — recognises him at about eighty metres. The recognition propagates inward. By the time he reaches the listening-floor's edge there are perhaps forty cult-members in his immediate line-of-sight, all of them studying him with the careful absence-of-aggression that characterises every interaction inside the shadow-zone. The cult has explicit instructions from the Comforter: "If the dawn-walker comes, do not greet him as opposition. Greet him as a man who has not yet rested. Offer him shade. Do not insist on it."

Asta-Asura is on the listening-floor. He is in plain dark-grey homespun. He is sitting cross-legged at the floor's eastern edge with a Galwan-survivor named Tashi-Norbu (the name Tashi is common in Tawang; this Tashi is a 2020 Galwan veteran, fifty-one years old, missing his left arm below the elbow from the river-stone clash). Asta-Asura has been sitting with Tashi-Norbu for two hours. Tashi-Norbu has been talking for the last forty minutes. Asta-Asura has not spoken during those forty minutes. He has nodded twice.

Udayagiri stops at the listening-floor's western edge. He does not step onto the floor. He waits.

Asta-Asura looks up. He sees Udayagiri.

He says, in plain Hindi, in the Comforter's soft register: "Brother. You have come. Will you sit. There is room at the western edge."

The address is the kingdom's neutral kinship-form — bhai, brother, used between strangers across the tribal-line. It carries no doctrinal weight. It is also, in the cave-Tibetan tradition Asta-Asura learned from, the form used to address a shadow-twin.

Udayagiri does not sit. He stands at the western edge.

He says, in the same plain Hindi, at the same volume: "Comforter. I have come to talk. I will stand. The talking will be brief."

Asta-Asura nods. He gestures to Tashi-Norbu in a way that indicates: Continue when you are ready; this conversation is between brothers; you are welcome to listen or to step off the floor as you choose. Tashi-Norbu stays. He has been silent in his life for a long time; he wants to hear what the dawn-walker will say to the man who finally listened to him.

Udayagiri says: "What you are doing here is real. The grief is real. The shade you are offering is real. The cult members are real. I have come to ask three questions. Will you answer them."

Asta-Asura says: "Yes."

Udayagiri says: "First question. Are you Asta-Asura."

Asta-Asura says: "Yes."

Udayagiri says: "Second question. Are you here to destroy the dawn-walk."

Asta-Asura says: "No."

Udayagiri says: "Third question. What would you ask of the kingdom."

Asta-Asura is silent for about fifteen seconds. He looks at Tashi-Norbu beside him. He looks at the perhaps three hundred cult-members in the shadow-zone around them. He looks at the four-thousand-one-hundred-metre Sela ridge above them. He looks back at Udayagiri.

He says: "I would ask that the kingdom build the room the morning will agree to wait outside of. The room is one room. The morning is the kingdom's vocation. The room is the kingdom's mercy. Build the room and I will be its caretaker. The cult-name will not change — the members have earned the name. The room will be the cult's home and the kingdom's recognised institution. The shade will not be hidden any more. The shade will be administered."

Udayagiri does not respond for a long time.

He says, finally: "I will think."

He turns. He walks back across the shadow-zone toward the sunlit valley floor. The cult-members watch him leave. None of them speaks. Tashi-Norbu, after Udayagiri is about a hundred metres west of the listening-floor, says to Asta-Asura: "Comforter-la. Will he build the room."

Asta-Asura says: "He will. The brother does not yet know he will. He will know in about five weeks."

Week three. Above the cloud-line at four thousand two hundred metres on the Sela ridge's northern face. The hermit's shelter — a single stone hut three metres by two, no door (the wind does not enter at this altitude in this orientation), a single low entrance, a butter-lamp inside, a small stove, no other furniture. Baba Parashar has lived in the shelter for forty-one years.

Baba Parashar is sixty-eight. He is in faded saffron robes, wool socks, no shoes. He has not descended below the cloud-line since 1985. He speaks to the clouds. The clouds report the weather-patterns of the entire subcontinent to him as patterns of vapour-shape. He interprets the patterns as metaphors. His prophecies are weather-forecasts in metaphor and they are always accurate. He has been Udayagiri's mentor since Udayagiri was twelve. Udayagiri climbs to the shelter approximately twice a year. The climb takes three days from Tawang.

Udayagiri arrives at the shelter at dusk on the seventh day after the listening-floor conversation. He carries one wool blanket, one small bag of tsampa-and-tea, one small bottle of mustard-oil, no spear (he never carries the spear above the cloud-line; the daily re-forging requires the dawn-light, which the cloud-line interrupts). He has been thinking about Asta-Asura's offer for seven days. The thinking has produced no clarity. He has climbed for clarity.

Baba Parashar is at the shelter's entrance when Udayagiri arrives. He is looking northwest at a low cloud-bank that is forming over the Bhutan-border ranges. He does not turn at Udayagiri's arrival. He waves toward the stove.

He says, in Apatani-Adi-Galo: "Make the tea. Three cups. The third is for the cloud that arrives at midnight. The cloud is bringing news from the Lower Subansiri."

Udayagiri makes the tea. He boils the water on the stove with a small handful of pine-needle kindling. He pours three cups. He places the third at the shelter's entrance for the midnight cloud.

He sits beside Baba Parashar at the entrance.

He says: "Baba-la. The Comforter has asked the kingdom to build a room. The room would hold managed shade alongside the dawn. The cult would be its caretakers. The kingdom would recognise the cult institutionally. I have been thinking for seven days. I do not know whether to build the room or to refuse it."

Baba Parashar does not respond immediately. He watches the northwest cloud-bank for about three minutes. The cloud-bank is moving slowly south-east; the cloud's shape is a long thin ribbon with a slight curve at the western end.

He says, finally: "The cloud at the western end is curved because the wind at four thousand metres is pushing it down and the wind at three thousand metres is holding it up. The cloud is being shaped by two opposing winds. The cloud does not choose between them. The cloud holds both shapes at once and the result is the curve. The curve is the cloud's honest answer."

He pauses. He sips the tea.

He says: "Your kingdom is being shaped by two opposing winds. The first wind is the kingdom's six-century-old vocation of dawn-walking. The second wind is the eleven hundred unhealed members of the night-cult, plus the seventeen thousand five hundred war-trauma survivors and bereaved families and burned forest-survivors who have not joined the cult only because they do not yet know it exists. The first wind is the kingdom's vocation. The second wind is the kingdom's wound. You cannot push the second wind back to the cave-system; the wound is already inside the kingdom. You cannot push the first wind aside; the vocation is the kingdom's name. The honest answer is the curve."

Udayagiri says: "Build the room."

Baba Parashar says: "Build the room. But understand the cost. The dawn-tribes will not forgive you for three or four generations. The night-cult will follow you only as long as the Comforter follows the agreement. Both factions will believe you have surrendered to the other. The kingdom will not understand the curve as the honest answer for at least a decade. You will be the dawn-walker who built the first shadow. The history-books in your lifetime will record this as your shame. The history-books in your great-grandchildren's lifetime will record this as the kingdom's saving. You will not be alive for the second history-book. You will live with the first one."

Udayagiri sits with the tea-cup at his lips for about a minute without drinking.

He says: "Will you come down with me. Will you announce the room from the dawn-walker's side. The dawn-tribes will not accept it from me alone. They will accept it from you."

Baba Parashar does not respond for thirty seconds.

He says: "Yes. I will come down. I have been above the cloud-line for forty-one years. I am due to descend. The kingdom requires it. I will descend tomorrow at first light. I will walk to the Tawang valley pass with you. I will speak at the kingdom-council. I will say what I have said to you. The dawn-tribes will hear me. The night-cult will hear me. The kingdom will hear me."

He pauses. He looks at the northwest cloud-bank again. The cloud-bank has moved further south-east and the curve at the western end has deepened.

He says: "The cloud is telling me one more thing. Both factions will be carrying weapons at the pass tomorrow. The cloud cannot tell me which faction will fire first. The cloud can only tell me that whoever stands at the centre of the pass between the two factions will be the cloud-shape's curve. The curve does not survive being shaped by two opposing winds. The cloud knows this. The cloud is telling me anyway."

Udayagiri says: "Baba-la. Then I will stand at the centre. Not you."

Baba Parashar says: "No. You are the dawn-walker. The kingdom needs you alive on the other side of the curve. The curve needs a different body. The cloud has told me whose body. The cloud is honest. The cloud is also kind. The cloud has given me a forty-one-year warning. I have had enough warning. I will stand at the centre. You will stand at the dawn-tribe's line, ten paces from me. You will not move when the arrows arrive. You will let me be the curve. The dawn will be your teacher in this matter and the cloud will be mine."

The midnight cloud arrives. The third tea-cup is drunk by the cloud. Udayagiri does not see the drinking but the cup is empty at one a.m. when he checks it. The cloud's news from the Lower Subansiri is that the Lower Subansiri night-cult subsidiary has reached six hundred members; the news confirms the cult is no longer a Tawang-local phenomenon. The kingdom needs the room within the month.

Udayagiri sleeps three hours. They begin the descent at four a.m.

Week three, day three. The Tawang valley pass at three thousand three hundred metres altitude — the principal valley-crossing between the eastern dawn-tribe villages and the western Sela-Pass shadow-zone where the night-cult meets. The pass is the kingdom's natural geographical division between the two communities.

Baba Parashar has descended below the cloud-line for the first time in forty-one years. The descent has taken him eleven hours; he is at the pass by three p.m. He is exhausted but composed. His feet, unused to walking at altitudes below four thousand metres, are blistered. He has refused medical attention. He says he will rest after the council.

The kingdom-council has been convened on a Thursday-emergency basis. The eastern dawn-tribe representatives are arrayed on the pass's eastern edge — about a hundred dawn-walker captains and tribe-elders from the thirteen recognised sun-tribes. They are armed with traditional dawn-walker spears (long copper-headed, eastern-light forged); some carry modern rifles purchased through the state-government's tribal-defence allocation. The night-cult representatives are arrayed on the pass's western edge — about a hundred and twenty cult-members. They are not armed with traditional weapons (the cult forbids them) but a significant minority carry modern hunting-rifles. The rifles are not officially cult-sanctioned but the cult's senior delegates have not confiscated them.

The pass itself — the open ground between the two edges — is about sixty metres wide and a hundred and twenty metres long. It is empty.

Baba Parashar walks alone to the centre of the pass at three-fifteen p.m. Udayagiri stops at the eastern edge, ten paces from the dawn-tribe representatives' front rank. Sonam-Lama-Bumla stops beside Udayagiri. Tashi-Norbu the Galwan-veteran has come from the western edge and stops at the night-cult representatives' front rank.

Baba Parashar reaches the centre. He stops. He raises his right hand. The gesture is the dawn-walker's neutral-address gesture — palm forward, fingers spread, held at eye-level.

He speaks. He speaks in Apatani-Adi-Galo, then repeats each sentence in cave-Tibetan, then repeats each sentence in Hindi. The triple-language address takes about twelve minutes.

He says: "Kingdom of the rising sun. Children of the dawn-walk. Children of the listening-floor. I have come down from the cloud-line for the first time in forty-one years because the kingdom requires that I come down. The kingdom is being shaped by two opposing winds. The first wind is the dawn-vocation. The second wind is the kingdom's wound. The honest answer is the curve. The curve is the building of the room the morning will agree to wait outside of. The room will be built at Tawang in the eleventh week of this chapter. The night-cult will be its caretakers. The dawn-walkers will recognise it institutionally. The kingdom-council will register the cult as the Order of the Dawn-Shadow Temple. The Comforter will be the Temple's shade-master. Udayagiri will be the Temple's dawn-master. Tani-of-the-Veil —"

He pauses. He has not yet been introduced to Tani-of-the-Veil. He has been told her name by the cloud. He says it for the first time.

"Tani-of-the-Veil will be the Temple's translator between the two masters. The kingdom-council registers the Temple by my authority as the senior cloud-speaker of the kingdom. The registration is effective at the moment of this address's completion. The dawn-tribes will accept the registration because the cloud-speaker has authority over kingdom-institutional matters under the kingdom's six-century-old founding charter. The night-cult will accept the registration because the Comforter has agreed to the architecture in advance. The kingdom is now whole in the curve."

He lowers his right hand.

The eastern dawn-tribe's third-rank archer — an unnamed twenty-three-year-old dawn-walker who has been at the Tawang clash in 2022 and has lost his elder brother and his cousin in the same hour — raises his bow at three-twenty-seven p.m. He has not been authorised to fire. His bow-arm is shaking. The arrow is on the string.

The western night-cult's second-rank rifleman — an unnamed thirty-one-year-old Ziro forest-fire survivor whose grandmother died in the second-day burn-wave and whose face is half-scarred from the same fire — raises his rifle at three-twenty-seven p.m. He has not been authorised to fire. His finger is on the trigger. The rifle is unsafetied.

Neither faction's senior delegates see the raising. Neither faction's discipline-officer intervenes.

The dawn-walker fires the arrow at three-twenty-seven-thirty-eight p.m. The arrow travels approximately fifty-five metres at an initial velocity of fifty-four metres per second.

The rifleman fires the bullet at three-twenty-seven-forty-two p.m. The bullet travels approximately forty-eight metres at an initial velocity of seven hundred and forty metres per second.

The arrow reaches Baba Parashar's chest at three-twenty-seven-thirty-nine-point-one p.m. The arrowhead enters between the third and fourth ribs on the right side and pierces the right lung at a depth of about eleven centimetres.

The bullet reaches Baba Parashar's back at three-twenty-seven-forty-two-point-three p.m. The bullet enters between the seventh and eighth thoracic vertebrae and exits through the upper-left abdomen.

Baba Parashar is dead at three-twenty-eight-fourteen p.m. The death is approximately thirty-six seconds after the first arrow.

The pass is silent for about eighteen seconds. Then both senior delegate-lines move forward simultaneously to apprehend their own shooters. The dawn-walker is restrained at three-twenty-eight-forty p.m. by Sonam-Lama-Bumla and two dawn-tribe elders. The rifleman is restrained at three-twenty-eight-forty-five p.m. by the night-cult's discipline-officer Karma-Norbu (no relation to Tashi-Norbu).

Udayagiri walks to the centre of the pass at three-twenty-nine-twelve p.m. He kneels beside Baba Parashar's body. He does not weep. He places his right palm above his teacher's forehead in the dawn-walker's farewell gesture. He says, in plain Apatani-Adi-Galo, audibly to the eastern and western edges: "The curve has held. The cloud was honest. The cloud was kind. The forty-one-year warning was the cloud's last gift."

Asta-Asura walks to the centre of the pass at three-twenty-nine-thirty p.m. He arrives from the western edge unarmed. He kneels at the opposite side of the body from Udayagiri. He places his right palm above his own forehead and lowers it — the cave-Tibetan farewell gesture, mirror to the dawn-walker's.

The two men do not look at each other. They look at the body.

Asta-Asura says, in cave-Tibetan, then in Hindi: "Brother. The kingdom has begun the room with this man's body at its first cornerstone. We will build the room. We will name the man at the consecration. We will not let his name fade. The cloud was right. The curve was honest. The kingdom needed this man's body. I am not glad. I will help."

Udayagiri does not respond. He stays at the centre of the pass with the body until Sonam-Lama-Bumla and Karma-Norbu jointly arrange the stretcher and the carrying party. The body is carried up to the cloud-line that night for cremation in the traditional dawn-walker manner; Asta-Asura and three of his senior cult-delegates accompany the cremation party. The cremation is performed at first light the following morning by Udayagiri and Asta-Asura together — the first joint act across the faction-line. The ashes are divided equally; half are scattered above the cloud-line at the hermit's shelter; half are returned to the Tawang valley and interred at the future Dawn-Shadow Temple site as the cornerstone.

The two shooters are arrested by the Sikkim-Arunachal joint district-magistrate's order at six p.m. that evening. Their trials are referred to the Arunachal High Court under regular criminal jurisdiction. Both men plead guilty within eleven days. Both serve fifteen-year sentences. Neither faction publicly defends the shooter. The kingdom's common position, articulated by Sonam-Lama-Bumla at the post-pass press conference: "The two shooters are the kingdom's failure to heal in time. They are not the kingdom's representatives. They are the kingdom's unhealed."

Week four. Tawang valley. The morning after the cremation. Tani-of-the-Veil arrives at the monastery's administrative wing at five-thirty a.m.

She is twenty-nine. She is a Galo-tribe woman from the Aalo district originally; she had been at the 2022 Tawang clash front-line as a battlefield medic with the Indian Army's Arunachal Scouts; she had pulled seven wounded soldiers off the river-stone before being struck herself by a flying piece of granite and losing the sight in her right eye. She had been with the night-cult for thirteen months. She had served as Asta-Asura's primary interpreter at the listening-floor — she was the only cult-member fluent in all four of the cult's working languages (Apatani-Adi-Galo, cave-Tibetan, Hindi, and English).

She is in the night-cult's plain dark-grey homespun. She has a black wool veil draped across the right side of her face from temple to chin, covering the scarred right eye-socket and the surrounding tissue. The veil is held by a small silver clip at her right temple. The veil is the source of her cult-name: Tani-of-the-Veil. She has worn the veil since the cult's third month.

She walks into the monastery's administrative wing alone, unannounced, having travelled from the Sela-Pass shadow-zone overnight on foot. She has not been authorised by the cult to make the journey. The cult's senior delegates do not yet know she has come.

She finds Udayagiri at the morning-tea station. He is alone — the morning-council has been postponed by three hours in observance of mourning. He looks up.

She says, in plain Hindi, in a steady administrative-voice: "Dawn-Walker-la. I am Tani-of-the-Veil. The cloud-speaker named me yesterday at the pass. I have come to learn what the naming means."

Udayagiri sets down the tea-cup he has just lifted. He looks at her for about ten seconds. He sees the veil. He sees the steady voice. He sees the absence of cult-doctrine in the address.

He says: "Sit, sister. The tea is fresh."

She sits. She accepts the tea. She does not speak again until the tea-cup is empty.

She says, finally: "The naming means I am the translator. The cloud-speaker said the kingdom needs a translator between the dawn-master and the shade-master at the Temple. I am willing to be the translator if the kingdom is willing to receive me. The cult is willing to release me to the role if you formally request it of the Comforter; the request must come from your side because the cult's rule against unilateral defection requires that any cross-over be initiated by the receiving institution, not by the defector. The Comforter will agree to the release. He told me last night, before the cremation. He named the cloud's seeing as authority enough."

Udayagiri says: "Why you. Why this role."

She says: "Because I am the only cult-member who has lived in both worlds. I was at the Tawang clash, in the morning light, holding wounded men's arteries closed with my hands. I was at the listening-floor, in the afternoon shadow, holding wounded men's grief without trying to close it. The two practices are the same practice in two languages. The translator the kingdom needs is the one who has learned both languages by doing them. I am the only person currently alive in the kingdom who has done both."

He says: "The Tani in your name. The Adi-tribe creator-figure. Are you claiming descent from him."

She says: "The Tani in my name is from the cult. The Comforter named me Tani-of-the-Veil because the Adi-creator Tani was the first man to walk through the world before the world had been finished. The first man saw both the unfinished light and the unfinished dark. The veil is not the Adi-creator; the veil is the medic's veil — what I wear over the eye that the clash took. The two meanings are joined in the cult's naming-tradition. I have used the name for thirteen months. I will keep it. The kingdom may keep it."

He says: "The role is yours. I will make the formal request to the Comforter this afternoon. The request will be by Sarus-pigeon. The cremation has not yet officially ended; in-person travel between the factions is suspended for the seven-day mourning period; the pigeon is the correct channel. The Comforter will release you by tomorrow evening. The kingdom-council will register your appointment on the third day after the mourning. The Temple's consecration is set for the eleventh week. You will work between the cult's senior delegates and the monastery's administrative wing during the seven-week build-out. You will translate at every joint meeting. You will train successors. By the time of the Sanctuary convening in Chapter Three you will have trained two more translators — one from each faction. The kingdom will not depend on a single bridge."

She says: "Yes."

He says: "Sister. The veil. Will you ever lift it."

She says, after about a half-minute's pause: "The veil is the eye the kingdom took. I will not hide that the kingdom took it. I will also not insist that the kingdom keep looking at the absence. The veil is the room I have built around my own wound. It is not unlike the room you are about to build. I will lift the veil at the Temple's consecration. I will lift it once. The kingdom will see the eye-socket. The kingdom will know what the room is for. Then I will replace the veil and continue the work. The single lifting will be the kingdom's permanent record of the wound the Temple is the answer to."

He says: "Yes."

The eleven-week build-out of the Dawn-Shadow Temple begins on the eighth day after Baba Parashar's cremation. The site is a half-hectare plot adjacent to the Tawang Monastery's western boundary, donated jointly by the monastery's administrative wing and the night-cult's shadow-zone-platform-land-trust.

The architectural design is the cloud-shape's curve. The Temple is a single oval enclosed structure, forty-two metres long along the east-west axis, eighteen metres wide along the north-south axis. The eastern half of the oval is the dawn-room — open to the eastern sky, no roof, walls only at the southern and northern arcs, the dawn-walkers' greeting-stone at the eastern apex. The western half is the shade-room — fully roofed in dark slate, ventilated through small north-and-south slit-vents, the listening-floor's timber re-installed at the western apex (the timber the night-cult had built the original outdoor listening-floor from). The two rooms meet at a central transition-arch — a low stone arch about three metres wide, one metre seventy high, which a person can walk through but cannot run through. The arch is the cloud-curve made architectural.

The build-out employs approximately three hundred workers — one hundred and fifty drawn from the dawn-tribe villages, one hundred and fifty from the night-cult. The two work-crews are arranged in mixed pairs by Tani-of-the-Veil's assignment rota; she insists that no pair contain two members from the same faction. The pairs work side-by-side for the full eleven weeks. By the third week the pairs have begun eating together at the site's noon-meal. By the seventh week the pairs have begun walking to and from the site together at dawn and at dusk. By the eleventh week three of the pairs have requested the kingdom-council's permission to formalise civil-partnership arrangements; the council approves all three.

The kingdom-council's registration of the Order of the Dawn-Shadow Temple is filed on the fourteenth day after Baba Parashar's cremation. The registration document, drafted by Tani-of-the-Veil and counter-signed by Udayagiri, Asta-Asura, Sonam-Lama-Bumla, and the district-magistrate, runs to seven pages. The first page establishes the Order as the kingdom's seventeenth recognised religious institution. The second page sets out the dawn-master and shade-master arrangement. The third page sets out the translator's standing role. The fourth page sets out the eleven-week consecration schedule. The fifth page sets out the long-term cross-faction-pair work-rota. The sixth page sets out the Karna-shadow elder's name as the Temple's cornerstone — Baba Parashar of the Cloud-Line, forty-one years above the cloud-line, dawn-walker by tradition, cloud-speaker by vocation, killed at the Tawang valley pass at three-twenty-seven-thirty-nine p.m. on the third day of the third week of this chapter. The seventh page sets out the Temple's succession-procedure for the dawn-master, shade-master, and translator positions on the death or resignation of any incumbent.

The registration is published in the state-government gazette on the seventeenth day. The registration is countersigned by the Manipur, Assam, Nagaland, Meghalaya, and Sikkim NE-Federation registry on the twenty-third day (the cross-kingdom recognition that the new Temple is regionally legitimate, independent of any individual kingdom's ratification).

Week eleven, day three. The Tawang valley. The morning of the Dawn-Shadow Temple's consecration. First light at the eastern apex's greeting-stone is timed for five-forty-three a.m. (the same minute Udayagiri had greeted the dawn at the ridge-stone on the chapter's opening day, eleven weeks earlier; the consecration has been deliberately scheduled to fall on the same first-light minute).

The Temple is fully built. The eastern dawn-room is open to the sky. The western shade-room is fully roofed. The central transition-arch holds the cloud-shape's curve in its stone-line.

The crowd at the consecration is approximately twelve thousand people. The number is unprecedented for any religious event in Arunodaya-Kshetra in the past forty years. The crowd includes approximately three thousand dawn-tribe representatives from all thirteen sun-tribes, approximately one thousand one hundred night-cult members (the full membership, minus about a hundred who are still working at the site as last-minute caretakers), approximately three thousand kingdom-government civil servants and dignitaries, approximately five thousand kingdom-citizens drawn from villages across the state who have travelled to witness, and approximately seventy-five international observers (including the Manipur, Assam, Nagaland, Meghalaya, and Sikkim NE-Federation delegations and four cross-bloodline senior-guardians from the South and Central regions, sent by Krishnadevaraya-Bhrata and Dvadashashringa respectively).

Udayagiri arrives at the eastern apex at five-forty a.m. He is in the dawn-walker robe with the gayal-hide sash. He carries the Uday-spear. He has re-forged the spear that morning at the ridge-stone above the monastery (the daily re-forging continues; the consecration does not change his standing daily discipline).

Asta-Asura arrives at the western apex at five-forty a.m. He is in plain dark-grey homespun — the cult's working garment; he has declined the kingdom-council's offer of a formal shade-master's robe (the offer was made; he had said: "The robe is the kingdom's formal recognition. I am the kingdom's informal mercy. The homespun is the honest answer."). He carries no weapon and no implement. He holds a single small clay bowl of water drawn from the Tawang valley's western stream — the shade-master's consecration-bowl, an invention of the new Order. The bowl will be poured at the transition-arch at the moment of first-light.

Tani-of-the-Veil arrives at the central transition-arch at five-forty-one a.m. She is in plain wool, neutral grey, with the silver-clipped black veil at her right temple. She carries the seven-page registration document in a sealed scroll, with the kingdom's seal and the NE-Federation's seal both visible on the cover. She will read the cornerstone naming at the moment of first-light, in all three languages (Apatani-Adi-Galo, cave-Tibetan, Hindi), in the same sequence Baba Parashar had used at the valley pass eleven weeks earlier.

The first-light arrives at five-forty-three a.m. Udayagiri turns the spear's blade-edge to the east. The blade catches the first ray. The metal warms. Tani-of-the-Veil reads the cornerstone naming. The reading takes about four minutes per language; the full triple-language reading takes twelve minutes.

At the conclusion of the triple-language reading, Asta-Asura pours the clay bowl's water at the transition-arch's threshold. The water flows from west to east across the threshold-stone, then disperses into the gravel of the eastern dawn-room's floor. The dispersion is the cult's ritual symbol for shade meeting light without resistance.

Tani-of-the-Veil lifts the silver clip at her right temple. The black wool veil falls from her face onto her shoulder. The kingdom sees her right eye-socket — empty since 2022, the scarred tissue grey-pink, the bone-orbit visible at its edge. She holds the exposed face still for about fifteen seconds. The kingdom's witnesses include the twelve thousand assembled and the regional television-and-radio broadcasters whose camera-feeds carry the image to about thirty million regional and national viewers in real time. Then she replaces the veil, re-clips it at her right temple, and steps to the side of the arch.

At the moment Tani-of-the-Veil's veil falls and the kingdom sees her eye-socket, Udayagiri feels the gayal-bond pulse through his right pectoral.

The Stage III gayal-foreleg mark begins to form. It begins at the right collarbone in the standard gayal-bond position. It arcs down across the right pectoral as the bull-gayal's right forelimb, with the hoof-impression at the lower edge near the sternum. The mark forms in approximately fourteen seconds beneath the dawn-walker robe. It is not visible to the assembled crowd. Udayagiri feels the formation as a slow warm pressure across the pectoral — the warmth is the gayal-bond's recognition of the concession.

He does not show the moment on his face. He continues the consecration-ceremony per the registration document's schedule. The dawn-room's formal opening-greeting in Apatani-Adi-Galo at five-fifty-five a.m. The shade-room's formal opening-statement in cave-Tibetan at six-oh-five a.m. The transition-arch's combined-language blessing in Hindi at six-fifteen a.m. The crowd's formal entry into both rooms in mixed-faction pairs (organised by Tani-of-the-Veil's pre-arranged rota) at six-twenty a.m. through six-fifty a.m.

By seven a.m. the kingdom is inside the Temple. The dawn-room holds about six thousand people in the open air with the eastern sky above. The shade-room holds about six thousand people in the slate-roofed interior with the listening-floor's timber beneath. The transition-arch is open; the kingdom passes back and forth through it freely. The cloud-shape's curve is the kingdom's new architecture.

Six-fifty-seven a.m. on the same morning. Roing village in the lower Dibang valley, approximately two hundred and eighty kilometres east of Tawang, at the kingdom's eastern edge. The morning's first-light has reached Roing at five-oh-eight a.m. (the time-zone-staggered ridges' first-light walks east-to-west across the state across thirty-eight minutes). The village is going about its standard Tuesday morning. The Adi-tribe villagers are at the market-square, the schoolteacher's shop, the rice-storehouse, the river-bank.

A man walks into the village from the east at six-fifty-seven a.m. He is dressed in plain Adi-tribe homespun cotton shawl, undyed, no clan-pattern. He is barefoot. He carries nothing. The sun is at an eastern angle of approximately thirty-two degrees above the horizon — the standard mid-morning angle that casts every person, animal, and post-mounted lamp a long westward shadow of about one-point-six times the casting object's height. The man walks at a standard adult pace — about one-point-three metres per second.

He casts no shadow.

The villagers see the no-shadow at about the seventh metre of his progression. The first villager to see it is an eleven-year-old schoolboy named Tashi-Dorjee (the name Tashi is common across the Northeast; this Tashi is unrelated to the Sikkim Tashi-the-cousin and the Sikkim Tashi-Bhutia ranger and the Tawang Tashi-Norbu Galwan-veteran). The schoolboy stops walking. He stares at the dirt-path beneath the man's bare feet. There is no shadow. There is no foot-impression. There is no compression of the dirt.

The schoolboy says, in Adi: "The man has no shadow."

The next villager to see is a thirty-eight-year-old market-vendor named Mami-Bobo. She stops her selling. She stares. There is no shadow.

The next is a sixty-one-year-old elder named Aaba-Tani (the name Tani is the Adi-creator name; Aaba-Tani is "father-Tani" in the Adi honorific). He stops his morning rice-buying. He stares. There is no shadow.

The man walks the length of the village's main path. The path is about eight hundred metres long. He walks the full length at the steady standard pace. The walk takes eleven minutes and approximately fifteen seconds. During the walk approximately a hundred and thirty villagers see him — he passes their line-of-sight in the eleven-minute window. Each of them sees the no-shadow.

He continues out of the village at the western end. He walks toward the Dibang river. He does not reach the river within the eleven-minute observation-window (the river is about three kilometres west of the village's western edge). He continues at the steady pace beyond the village's line-of-sight. None of the villagers follows him.

Within ninety minutes of his passage, none of the witnesses can remember what he looked like.

They remember he was a man. They remember he was dressed in plain Adi homespun shawl. They remember he was barefoot. They remember he carried nothing. They remember he had no shadow. They remember the no-shadow. They cannot remember his face.

The eleven-year-old Tashi-Dorjee at school during the third hour says, when his teacher asks: "Was he Adi or Galo or Apatani." He answers: "I do not know. I cannot remember." The teacher says: "Was he young or old." He answers: "I do not know. I cannot remember." The teacher says: "Was he tall or short." He answers: "I cannot remember."

The thirty-eight-year-old market-vendor Mami-Bobo tries to draw the face that afternoon. She is a competent amateur-portraitist; she has drawn faces from memory before. She cannot draw this face. She draws three different attempts. None of the three faces resembles either of the others. She destroys all three at sunset.

The sixty-one-year-old Aaba-Tani sits with two other elders at the village-square at dusk and tries to compose a verbal description. The three of them cannot agree on a single feature. The man was tall or short or middle. He was young or old or middle. He was Adi or Galo or Apatani or non-tribal. The face has erased itself from their memory.

By the evening of the same day a Sarus-pigeon-letter reaches Tawang. The letter is from the Roing village-headman to Udayagiri. The letter is two paragraphs long. The first paragraph describes the no-shadow walk. The second paragraph describes the face-erasure. The closing line reads, in Adi: "Dawn-Master-la. The kingdom has been visited by a Herald of the Maw. The Maw has taken a face from the world's memory. We have not seen this before. The kingdom requires that you read the sign at the Temple consecration's evening assembly."

Udayagiri reads the letter at seven p.m. on the consecration day. He is at the Temple's eastern apex with Asta-Asura, Tani-of-the-Veil, Sonam-Lama-Bumla, and the assembled kingdom's evening-prayer attendance.

He reads the letter aloud in Apatani-Adi-Galo and in Hindi. The twelve thousand-still-attending crowd hears it. The NE-Federation delegates hear it. The Krishnadevaraya-Bhrata-and-Dvadashashringa observers hear it. The regional television-and-radio broadcasters carry it.

Udayagiri stands at the eastern apex of the Temple at seven-thirty p.m. on the consecration evening. The dawn-room is open to the deep-evening sky above; the first stars are showing. The shade-room is lit by two hundred and forty butter-lamps arranged along the slate-walls in two long rows. The transition-arch is open; the kingdom passes through it slowly.

He says, in plain Apatani-Adi-Galo, then in cave-Tibetan, then in Hindi:

"Kingdom of the rising sun. Order of the Dawn-Shadow Temple. The Maw has sent its First Herald to Roing. The Herald is a man without a shadow whose face the witnesses cannot remember. The Herald is the Maw's most direct manifestation in human form. The Herald is not killed. The Karnataka rule holds. The Karnataka Third Herald is named the Bronze-Dust Child by a ten-year-old girl. The Goa Second Herald is named Vasco-without-a-ship by a thirty-three-year-old woman at her front step. We do not yet know who will name the First Herald. We do know what we will not do. We will not raise a weapon against him. We will not pursue him to the river. We will not investigate his face. The face is the Maw's erasure; investigating the face is the trap. We will instead build the second Temple. The Second Dawn-Shadow Temple at Roing. The Temple will be the Roing village's answer to the no-shadow walk. The Temple will be the room the Herald will be invited to walk through, when he returns, and to sit in. The Herald will return. The Maw sends Heralds back. The Temple will be ready."

He pauses. He looks at Tani-of-the-Veil at the transition-arch.

He says: "Tani-of-the-Veil will travel to Roing within the fortnight. She will draft the second Temple's registration in consultation with the Roing village-headman and the Lower Dibang district magistrate. The second Temple's caretakers will be drawn from the Roing village itself — a dawn-master from the village's sun-tribe elders and a shade-master from the village's war-trauma-survivor population, with a translator drawn from the village's teacher-or-medic professional class. The Tawang Temple is the kingdom's pilot. The Roing Temple is the kingdom's first replication. The Maw has chosen Roing as the next site of its experiments. The kingdom will be there before the next experiment."

He pauses again.

He says: "The First Herald will be named when we name him. The naming-tradition the saga has established — from Karnataka and Goa — is that the Herald is named by the person who first recognises him as a Herald-and-not-as-a-threat. The eleven-year-old schoolboy Tashi-Dorjee at Roing is the first witness of the kingdom-side. The naming may come from him. The naming may come from someone else. The kingdom will not rush the naming. The naming is the kingdom's evidence that we have not forgotten the face. The kingdom will look at the face that has been erased and will replace the erasure with a name. The naming may take a week, a month, a year, a decade. The naming is the kingdom's patient work."

Three days after the Tawang consecration, a Sarus-pigeon arrives from the Lower Subansiri. The pigeon is from the Lower Subansiri night-cult subsidiary — the six-hundred-member sub-cult the cloud at the hermit's shelter had described to Baba Parashar at midnight eleven weeks earlier. The letter is from the sub-cult's senior delegate, an Apatani woman named Khoda-Jaya (the name Khoda is from the Apatani creator-line and means "the one who is brought forth").

The letter is short.

Dawn-Master-la. Comforter-la. Translator-la. The Lower Subansiri sub-cult requests admission to the new Order of the Dawn-Shadow Temple. The admission will be subject to the same architecture as the Tawang pilot — a dawn-master to be drawn from the Lower Subansiri Apatani-tribe elders, a shade-master to be drawn from our six-hundred-member sub-cult, a translator to be drawn from the Lower Subansiri medic-and-teacher community. The site we propose is the Hapoli town's eastern hillside — the same hillside the cloud-bank the cloud-speaker had read curves over each morning between four and six a.m. The site is one hectare. The Apatani-tribe-elder council has agreed to the donation provisionally subject to the kingdom-council's ratification. We expect to commence the build-out within the week of ratification.

Sub-cult, Lower Subansiri, — Khoda-Jaya.

The letter is read at the Temple's morning-council on the fourth day after the consecration. The kingdom-council ratifies the Lower Subansiri Temple by unanimous vote at the same morning-council. The third Temple is begun within the fortnight.

By the end of Chapter Two, three Dawn-Shadow Temples are in active construction across the kingdom: Tawang (consecrated; in full operational use), Roing (the foundations laid; the consecration scheduled for the third month of Chapter Three; built explicitly as the Herald-return room), Lower Subansiri (the foundations laid; the consecration scheduled for the fourth month of Chapter Three). The kingdom's pilot has become the kingdom's replication-program.

Two weeks after the Tawang consecration, a Sarus-pigeon arrives at the Temple from Imphal. The pigeon is the one Moirangthem has been using for the NE-Federation correspondence-network. The letter is from Moirangthem.

The letter reads:

Dawn-Master-la. The NE-Federation has read the Order of the Dawn-Shadow Temple's registration document and the cross-kingdom co-signing has been ratified by all five federation kingdoms. The Sanctuary in Chapter Three will be a six-kingdom assembly: Kamakhya (Kamalavarna), Imphal (Moirangthem, candidate), Shillong (U-Khrawkmen, regent), Kohima (Dzulevira, deferred-vote), Gangtok (Kanchenjunga). Your vote will complete the regional ratification. Will you give it.

Udayagiri writes the reply over two days. The reply is two paragraphs long.

NE Dominant Candidate. I will give my vote at the Sanctuary. The vote will be for you. Arunodaya-Kshetra has stabilised after the First Herald's passage at Roing but the stabilisation is not yet certain — the second Herald-visit will come, and the third, and we do not know when. The Sanctuary's convening will mark the threshold across which the NE Federation will need to coordinate on the Herald-and-Temple-replication question. My vote is for you because you will be the regional voice for that coordination and because your own kingdom's Loktak Mouth-sign has shown us that the Maw is initiating form-making and our Temples are the kingdom-architecture answer.

Sub-paragraph: Tani-of-the-Veil will attend the Sanctuary as my co-translator. She is twenty-nine and will be the Sanctuary's first medic-trained attendee and the first cross-faction-defector co-administrator in the kingdom's history. Asta-Asura will not attend — the cult's self-imposed restriction is that the Comforter does not travel outside the kingdom in his first three years as shade-master. He will remain at Tawang. Sonam-Lama-Bumla will attend as the kingdom's administrative voice.

Dawn-Master-la, — U.

He sends the letter by the same Sarus-pigeon at sunset. Moirangthem receives the letter at Imphal at noon the next day. He writes the one-line reply: The accommodation is arranged. The Sanctuary will receive all three. He sends the reply by the pigeon returning, at dusk.

Six weeks after the Tawang consecration. Roing village. The first foundations of the second Temple have been laid. The Roing village-headman's daughter — a thirteen-year-old girl named Yapum-Mihu — has been appointed the second Temple's translator-in-training under Tani-of-the-Veil's remote supervision. The second Temple's build-out is being managed by a thirty-four-year-old former Tawang-clash medic named Karme-Bobo who has moved from Tawang to Roing for the role.

The First Herald has not yet returned to Roing.

Karme-Bobo has been at the Temple-site at five a.m. each morning for the past three weeks, before dawn, walking the perimeter, reading the village-headman's incident-log from the previous twenty-four hours. The log has recorded no further no-shadow walks. The log has recorded an increase in the village-elder Aaba-Tani's morning-prayers and a small uptick in the village-children's nightmares (Yapum-Mihu records nine children mentioning "a man we cannot remember" in their morning play-conversations over the past week). The Herald is, in some sense, still in the village — the absence of his face is still being felt.

On the third Thursday after the foundations are laid, Yapum-Mihu the thirteen-year-old translator-in-training arrives at the Temple-site at five-fifteen a.m. She has been at the site every dawn since her appointment. She has been quiet during her training. She has not yet been heard to speak more than a few sentences at any one assembly.

She walks up to Karme-Bobo at the site's eastern apex. The first-light is forming behind the Mishmi Hills.

She says, in Adi: "Karme-la. I have thought of a name. The First Herald. I would like to name him. The naming may not be correct. The naming may be revised later. I would like to offer it."

Karme-Bobo says: "Yes, Mihu. What is the name."

She says: "Sahodara-Aapun. The brother-without-self. In Adi. The Adi-creator-line says that Tani — the first man — had a brother who walked beside him through the unfinished world but who did not have a self of his own. The brother held the unfinished part of the world together while Tani gave it form. The brother was forgotten when the world was finished. The Adi children's songs remember him without his face. The songs do not require a face. The name in the songs is Sahodara-Aapun. The Herald is the same. He is the brother who held the unfinished part. We forgot him when the kingdom was named. We are naming him now. The naming may not return him to us. The naming may only remind us that we forgot him. That is what the name will do. It will not retrieve the face. It will hold the place where the face was."

Karme-Bobo says, after a half-minute pause: "Yes. We will send the naming to Tawang today. We will register the name with the kingdom-council on the seventh day. We will read the name at every Temple's morning-greeting. We will hold the place where the face was. The Maw will know that the kingdom did not forget the forgetting."

She nods. She returns to her translator's desk at the Temple-site's preliminary administrative tent. Karme-Bobo writes the naming-letter to Tawang in the next hour. The Sarus-pigeon carries it westward at six-thirty a.m. Tani-of-the-Veil receives it at the Tawang Temple at noon. She walks it to Udayagiri at the eastern apex.

Udayagiri reads the letter. He reads it twice.

He says, to Tani-of-the-Veil: "The First Herald is named. By a thirteen-year-old translator-in-training at Roing. The name is Sahodara-Aapun. The brother-without-self. The kingdom will hold the place where the face was. The Maw will read the kingdom's answer."

He pauses. He looks at the eastern dawn-room's open sky. The sky has begun to cloud over — the first afternoon clouds are forming.

He says: "Baba-la would have said the cloud is honest. The cloud is also kind. The kingdom has built the room. The Herald has been named. The dawn-walker has conceded the shade. The Comforter has accepted the architecture. The translator has lifted the veil. The thirteen-year-old has remembered the brother-without-self. The cloud is curved. The curve has held. The chapter is closing."

He turns the Uday-spear's blade-edge to the east one more time. The afternoon-clouds have begun to gather but the eastern sky is still open at the apex. The blade catches a thin late-afternoon ray. The metal warms.

The kingdom's next sign comes within the week.

The Pilot's Closing Image

A forty-two-metre oval temple at Tawang, the eastern half open to the sky and the western half roofed in dark slate, joined by a low stone arch a person can walk through but cannot run through. A bull-gayal-foreleg mark on a dawn-walker's right pectoral beneath the amber robe. A twenty-nine-year-old translator with a black wool veil clipped at her right temple, the veil lifted once for fifteen seconds at the consecration and then replaced. A forty-four-year-old shade-master in plain dark-grey homespun pouring a clay bowl's water across a threshold-stone from west to east. A sixty-eight-year-old hermit-prophet's ashes scattered above the cloud-line at the hermit's shelter and interred at the Temple's cornerstone in equal halves. A thirteen-year-old Adi translator-in-training at Roing reading the morning-greeting in the Adi creator-line's children's song that remembers the brother-without-self without his face. A man without a shadow walking the eight-hundred-metre length of a village main path at six-fifty-seven a.m., his face erased from the memory of every witness, his name now Sahodara-Aapun in the kingdom's permanent record — the kingdom holding the place where the face was.
