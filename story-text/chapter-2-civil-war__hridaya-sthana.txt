Bhopal. The state capital. The Twelve-Court Assembly Hall — a circular chamber under a dome of carved sandstone, with twelve doors set into the walls at exact thirty-degree intervals. Each door leads to one of the twelve court-chambers. The judges enter through their own doors at 9:00 a.m. each working day, take their seats around the central circle, and the assembly opens.

Dvadashashringa is forty-six. He sits in the central position — the thirteenth seat, which he has never thought of as the thirteenth seat because in his system there are exactly twelve judges and one observer-king. His twelve-pointed crown sits on a teakwood stand beside him. He carries the Dwadash twelve-pronged ceremonial staff diagonally across his lap. The staff is six feet long, of central Indian ironwood, each prong tipped in solar-gold.

He has been the king of Hridaya-Sthana for twenty-two months. He has reigned without significant scandal. The twelve courts have ruled on three thousand four hundred eleven cases. The Bhopal newspapers, which take pleasure in criticising every state government, have written three editorials in twenty-two months — one of which was actually complimentary, an unusual outcome.

This morning is an unusual morning. The chief justice of Court X (Disaster Relief) is about to read a memorandum.

The memorandum is short. The southwest monsoon over the Vindhya plateau has failed by forty-eight percent against the ten-year average. Three districts — Damoh, Chhatarpur, and Tikamgarh — are reporting groundwater drops of more than two metres. Rice and millet plantings are losing seedlings at a rate that suggests a complete harvest loss by November. The first refugees from the worst-hit village clusters have begun arriving in Sagar and Bhopal — about three thousand people so far. The state-revenue board projects fifteen to twenty thousand refugees within four weeks if the situation does not stabilise.

Dvadashashringa listens. He says: "Court X, please initiate emergency-relief deliberations. Court III, please prepare a contingency funding analysis. Court XII, please open a cross-border grain-procurement file."

Three court-chiefs nod.

He does not yet know — he could not yet know — that he has just made a small organisational decision that will kill forty thousand people.

Three weeks later. The famine has worsened. Refugees in Bhopal are now twelve thousand. Court X is in week three of its disaster-relief deliberations — the disaster-relief statute's 2007 narrow-scope problem has emerged exactly as Court X's chief justice feared it would. Court III is in week three of arguing about whether to draw from the contingency reserve or pass an emergency revenue measure. Court XII is in week three of a 1981 cross-border-trade memorandum dispute.

Four other courts have, in the meantime, taken up some aspect of the famine. Court I (the new refugee camps' land-status), Court VII (refugee-as-temporary versus refugee-as-permanent classification), Court VI (whether temple grain-stores can be opened), and Court XI (Kanha's barasingha herds, who are also starving).

At the start of the fourth week, a senior dharmic counsellor presents himself at the assembly hall's ceremonial entrance. He has been invited by three of the chief justices to provide neutral arbitration. His name is Adharma-Kendra. He is fifty-three. He wears a plain ochre dhoti and shawl. He carries no staff and no entourage. He is welcomed.

Dvadashashringa recognises him. He stands. He says, evenly: "Uncle. You have come to the wrong meeting."

Adharma-Kendra smiles. The smile is genuine. He says: "Nephew. I have come to the meeting your wisest judges asked me to come to. They wrote to me. I have their letters in my satchel. I am not here to vote. I am here to abstain. There is a difference."

He turns to the assembly. He bows to each of the twelve courts in turn — the formal twelve-fold bow that has not been performed in this hall since the system's founding. The eleven judges who do not know what Dvadashashringa knows are deeply impressed. The one judge who has read his Chapter-One file — Aranya-Devi, Court XI, the forest-and-wildlife chief — is not impressed; but Aranya-Devi has been overruled on her own court's narrower question already this morning and is not in a position to overrule a procedural welcome.

Adharma-Kendra is admitted as a neutral senior counsellor with abstain-only voting privileges across all twelve courts.

Within forty-eight hours every court has cited him in support of continued deliberation. Within a week every court has slowed by another fifteen percent. The argument is always the same — the senior counsellor advises caution; let us deliberate more fully. Adharma-Kendra has not voted once. He has not needed to.

By the end of October the refugees in Bhopal number twenty-eight thousand. Court X is in week six of disaster-relief deliberations. The first famine-deaths are reported from a small village called Kunwariya in the Damoh district — eleven adults and four children in a single week.

Dvadashashringa reads the Kunwariya report on a Friday morning. He calls a private meeting with the chief justices of Courts X, III, and XII. He says: "What is the legal minimum decision-time required to authorise an emergency food convoy?"

Court X's chief justice (a careful woman of sixty-two) says: "Court X cannot authorise. The disaster-relief statute is scope-narrow. Court X can only authorise emergency-shelter and emergency-medical."

Court III's chief justice (a sixty-eight-year-old former finance secretary): "Court III cannot draw from contingency without Court X authorisation, which is scope-narrow as above. An emergency revenue measure requires bicameral assent — minimum three-week procedural cycle. Currently in week three of bicameral consultation."

Court XII (a thoughtful fifty-eight-year-old former diplomat): "Court XII cannot purchase grain abroad without contingency-reserve activation or revenue-measure passage. Currently in 1981 memorandum dispute, week six."

Dvadashashringa says: "What if I invoke royal prerogative?"

The three chief justices look at each other.

Court X says: "Maharaj. You founded this system to prevent royal prerogative. The founding charter forbids prerogative-invocation except in war."

"Is this not war?"

Court III, after a long pause: "It is a famine."

"Forty people are dead this week."

Court XII: "Famine, Maharaj. Not war."

He looks at them. They are not wrong. They are not even slightly wrong by the system he built. They are the best judges in central India. They are paralysed because his system is what made them so.

He says, finally: "Continue your deliberations."

He returns to the central observer-king seat. He does not sleep that night.

The Kunwariya report is the first one Sutrakara reads end-to-end. She is twelve. She has been sitting in the gallery of the Twelve-Court Assembly Hall since she was eight, mostly drawing diagrams of judges' speech-patterns in the margin of her grandfather's old arithmetic textbook. Her mother is dead (six years ago, an aneurysm). Her father is a king. She has been raised by court tutors and the gallery's sixty-year-old usher Mrs Bhattacharji, who lets her read official documents because Mrs Bhattacharji believes children should know the truth.

Sutrakara reads the Kunwariya report. She turns the page. She finds the village-by-village statistical annex Court X's clerk has compiled but not yet circulated to the chief justices. She reads the annex. She is twelve and she has been doing differential mathematics since she was nine. She runs the death-rate-curve forward against the deliberation-timeline.

The number she gets is forty thousand dead by the time the courts conclude. She runs it again. She gets the same number. She runs it a third time with conservative assumptions. She gets thirty-eight thousand.

She writes her calculation in clean ink on the back of three blank case-folder pages. She brings the pages to her father's study that evening.

He is preparing arguments for the next morning's court session. He looks at the pages. He says: "Beta. This is excellent arithmetic. The courts will reach their conclusions. The numbers cannot drive the dharma."

She says: "Pita-shri, the numbers are the dharma. Forty thousand people are the dharma. The courts are how we make sure no one person can be wrong. The dharma is something else."

He says, gently: "It is bedtime."

She says, more quietly: "I will speak to the chief justice tomorrow."

He nods. He thinks she means she will request an audience. He returns to his arguments.

The next morning she does not request an audience. She walks into the assembly hall during open session, climbs the gallery steps to the gallery's lowest railing, and asks Mrs Bhattacharji for the microphone-relay. Mrs Bhattacharji — who has been waiting for some child to do something like this for thirty years — hands her the microphone-relay.

She speaks. Her voice carries into the chamber. The twelve courts pause.

"Senior judges, I have run the famine-mortality curve against the deliberation-timeline. The current curve shows thirty-eight thousand to forty-four thousand dead by the time the courts conclude on their current procedural pace. I have brought death certificates from forty-seven villages. The certificates are below. Please look at them."

She drops the pile of certificates over the railing. They fall into the chamber. The certificates are real — she has collected them through Court X's clerk, who has been quietly grateful for someone to give them to.

Court XI's chief justice — Aranya-Devi — rises. She says: "I move that this assembly consider the young lady's evidence and accelerate deliberations accordingly."

Adharma-Kendra rises. He speaks softly. He says: "Senior judges. I am not voting today — I do not vote — but I will offer one observation. The numbers cannot dictate dharma. The courts must reach their natural conclusions. To act on raw numbers is to abandon dharmic process for utilitarian panic. I move — with humility, since I have no vote — that the assembly thank the young lady for her diligence and continue the deliberations."

The motion is taken. Aranya-Devi votes against. Adharma-Kendra abstains. The other ten chief justices vote for. The motion passes ten-to-one with one abstention.

Sutrakara puts the microphone-relay back in Mrs Bhattacharji's hand. She walks out of the gallery without saying a further word.

Dvadashashringa finds her in the corridor outside the gallery. She is crying with her back to a sandstone column. Her face is wet but she is not making a sound — she is twelve and she has been raised in court galleries; she knows how to cry quietly.

He kneels in front of her. He does not touch her.

She says: "Pita-shri, the courts will not save them. The courts cannot save them. They will all be dead by the time the courts agree."

He says: "The courts are how we know what is right."

She wipes her face with the back of her wrist. She looks at him in the way only a daughter can look at a father in a corridor.

She says: "No. The courts are how we make sure no one person can be wrong. The dharma is something else. The dharma is what gets done. The courts do not do anything. The courts decide whether something done is correct. If nothing is done there is nothing for the courts to decide whether is correct. The courts have made themselves unnecessary."

He does not answer for a long time.

She says, more softly: "I read the Bhimbetka paintings with Aaji last summer. Thirty thousand years of people. The paintings never show twelve judges deciding. The paintings show one person who saw the danger and acted. The dharma is in the acting."

He stands up. He looks down the corridor. The hall's twelve doors are at exact thirty-degree intervals. The corridor he is standing in is at one of those intervals. He has been standing in this corridor for twenty-two months without noticing it.

He says, finally: "Go home, beta. I will come for dinner. There is a thing I need to walk to the Narmada about."

She nods. She walks home.

He walks to the Narmada at dusk.

The Narmada river runs west across central India — the only major Indian river that runs east-to-west, against the geography. Dvadashashringa has always thought of this as a small but useful daily reminder that what looks like the necessary direction is not always the direction. He has not paid attention to the reminder.

He sits on the riverbank at the Bhojeshwar Temple stone-platform. The temple itself — an unfinished eleventh-century Shiva temple, one of central India's great medieval ruins — sits behind him. He watches the river.

The water ripples in a pattern that is not the current. He has been at this riverbank for six years. He has never seen this pattern. The pattern is a face.

The face is Sage Vidyaranya. The spirit-guru of Karnataka. The one who appears in moving water from the Tungabhadra and now — for the third time in three months — from the Ganga and now from the Narmada. The cross-kingdom visits are themselves the message.

Dvadashashringa reads the ripples.

Cousin. The Yudhishthira who matters is not the one who answered every question. The Yudhishthira who matters is the one who let his brother burn his palace down because the palace had become a cage.

Dvadashashringa says, aloud, to the water: "If I dissolve the courts I violate my own founding principle."

If you do not dissolve the courts you violate the lives the courts are killing. There is no dharma without one of those violations. The dharma is the one you choose.

"My uncle will say I have abandoned dharmic process."

Your uncle has been abandoning dharmic process for forty years by staying perfectly still. You will know it tomorrow because the man hunted south from Lucknow is coming to your court.

He looks up sharply. He says: "Drona-of-the-Plain?"

Drona-of-the-Plain. He has the proof.

The face is gone. The river is just the river. The barasingha-mark on his chest pulses once, low.

He walks home. Sutrakara is waiting at the dinner table with bhutta-roti and mahua-pickle and the day's second arithmetic-notebook. He eats. He talks to her. He does not tell her what he is going to do in the morning.

He arrives at the side-gate at three a.m. He is sixty-seven years old. He has been on the road for nineteen days — travelling by train, by bus, by farmer's truck, by foot for the last forty kilometres because he saw Adharma-Kendra's men at the Bhopal railway station. His grey hair is uncut. His travel-coat is dust-coloured.

He carries a leather satchel. The satchel contains: (1) photographs of Adharma-Kendra meeting with Adharma of Banaras in a Mumbai hotel six weeks before the Karnataka throne-stone destruction; (2) bank transfer records showing the same Singapore-routed account funded both the Karnataka Restoration Charter publication and the Mormugao port consortium's lobby budget; (3) a memorandum — written in Adharma-Kendra's own hand — outlining a coordination strategy across the Bloodline-B fault line. The memorandum is the proof.

Dvadashashringa receives him in the small east study. He reads the memorandum three times. He says: "How did you obtain this."

Drona-of-the-Plain says: "A junior secretary in Adharma-Kendra's Mumbai office is my late wife's cousin's son. He has been worried for some time. I asked. He gave."

"Will you testify."

"I owe forty years of straightening. I will testify."

Dvadashashringa pours two cups of mahua-tea from the silver pot. He says: "The assembly meets at nine. I will dissolve the twelve courts before lunch. You will testify in the recess. Stay in the small east study until I send for you."

Drona-of-the-Plain says: "Maharaj. I am sorry I waited this long."

"You did not wait. You came when you could. Drink the tea."

The Twelve-Court Assembly Hall opens at nine sharp. The twelve chief justices take their seats. Adharma-Kendra takes his neutral-counsellor seat. Sutrakara is in the gallery again — Mrs Bhattacharji is in her usual spot. Aranya-Devi (Court XI, the forest-and-wildlife chief) makes eye contact with Sutrakara and nods once.

Dvadashashringa enters carrying the Dwadash twelve-pronged staff in both hands — not diagonally across his lap as he usually carries it; held in both hands across his body. He is wearing the twelve-pointed crown.

He climbs to the central observer-king seat. He does not sit. He stands in front of the seat. He removes the twelve-pointed crown and places it on the teakwood stand. He turns. He faces the twelve courts.

He says: "I founded the twelve-court system at the start of my reign because I believed twelve sides of every question were better than one side. I was right then. I am wrong now. The court system was designed for the normal questions of land, marriage, taxation, succession, water-rights, religious sites, caste honour, refugee status, commerce, disaster scope, wildlife, and cross-border trade. It was not designed for a famine. The famine has killed two thousand four hundred people while we have argued whether refugee-status is temporary or permanent. The dharma is not in the deliberation. The dharma is in the feeding. I cannot dissolve the famine. I can dissolve the courts."

He lifts the Dwadash staff. He swings it once in an arc above his head — the ceremonial preparation-arc. The twelve solar-gold prong-tips catch the sandstone-filtered morning light.

He strikes the floor of the assembly chamber.

The first strike rings off the sandstone. Court I, Land & Inheritance, dissolves.

He strikes again. Court II, Marriage & Family.

Strike. Court III, Tax & Revenue.

Strike. Court IV, Commerce & Hoarding.

Strike. Court V, Tribal & Adivasi.

Strike. Court VI, Religious Sites.

Strike. Court VII, Citizenship & Refugee.

Strike. Court VIII, Inter-State Water.

On the eighth strike the Dwadash staff cracks. The crack runs the length of the shaft — he feels it through his hands more than he hears it.

He does not stop.

Strike. Court IX, Caste & Honour.

Strike. Court X, Disaster Relief. (The chief justice of Court X, the careful woman of sixty-two, weeps openly.)

Strike. Court XI, Forest & Wildlife. (Aranya-Devi sits straight in her seat with both hands on her armrests.)

On the eleventh strike the staff splits into two pieces in his right hand. The lower piece falls. He picks it up with his left hand. He continues.

Strike. Court XII, Cross-Border Trade.

On the twelfth strike the lower piece breaks in his left hand. The Dwadash twelve-pronged staff is now in three pieces on the sandstone floor of the assembly chamber. Twelve solar-gold prong-tips are scattered across the stone.

Dvadashashringa says: "The dharma is not in my staff. The dharma is in the act of breaking it."

The hall is silent. The chief justices are stunned. Adharma-Kendra rises slowly. He keeps his voice gentle.

"Nephew. You have just destroyed three thousand years of dharmic governance because a child showed you a number."

"I have destroyed twelve courts that could not decide whether to feed forty-seven villages. The dharma was in the feeding. The dharma was not in the courts."

"You will be judged for this."

"I am the judge. I am dissolving that too. From this hour the kingdom of Hridaya-Sthana operates under emergency single-court governance. The single judge is me. The single advisor is my daughter Sutrakara. The administrative deputies are: Aranya-Devi of former Court XI as deputy chief justice; the chief justices of former Courts III, X, and XII as regional governors of central, northern, and southern Madhya Pradesh respectively. The other eight courts' chief justices may submit resignations or take administrative postings at their discretion. The famine-relief convoys leave Bhopal in forty-eight hours."

Adharma-Kendra opens his mouth. Dvadashashringa raises his right hand.

"I am not finished. I have one more matter. The neutral senior counsellor admitted to this assembly in week three has been engineering the deliberation paralysis on purpose. I have the proof."

He turns his head slightly. Drona-of-the-Plain steps out from behind the central pillar where he has been waiting for thirty-one minutes. He carries the leather satchel.

He walks to the centre of the chamber. He stands beside Dvadashashringa.

He says, with the same plain steadiness he used at the Imambara: "Honourable judges. My name is Drona-of-the-Plain. I am the senior dharmic scholar of three Bloodline-B kingdoms. I owe Adharma-Kendra a forty-year debt for the saving of my maternal uncle's life. I have, until last month, allowed that debt to tilt my counsel in his favour. Last month at the Lucknow Bara Imambara I gave testimony that reversed the tilt. He has hunted me south since. I am here this morning because the evidence in this satchel proves what the Maharaj has just said. I will display the evidence."

He opens the satchel. He places the photographs on the assembly's evidence-table. He places the bank records beside them. He places the handwritten memorandum on top.

He says: "The handwriting on this memorandum is Adharma-Kendra's. Three of the eleven of you have ruled on cases where his handwriting was the central exhibit. You will recognise it."

The three chief justices stand up to look at the memorandum. They look. They sit down slowly.

Court IX's chief justice — an older man named Patwardhan-ji who has known Adharma-Kendra socially for thirty years — says, with visible difficulty: "It is his hand."

Adharma-Kendra is no longer in his seat. The senior-counsellor's seat is empty. The side-door of the chamber — the one Adharma-Kendra entered through three weeks ago when the assembly welcomed him — is slightly ajar.

He is gone.

Aranya-Devi sends three Court XI staff after him within ninety seconds. They do not catch him. The Bhopal police are alerted by 11:00. By 14:00 he has been sighted in the Bhopal old quarter near the Taj-ul-Masajid mosque. By 18:00 he has not been sighted again. He surfaces three days later in a small village outside Sehore and disappears. He survives the chapter intact.

Dvadashashringa stands at the centre of the assembly chamber with the broken Dwadash staff at his feet. The eleven chief justices, ashamed, sit in their seats. The hall is silent for a long minute. Sutrakara is in the gallery with both hands gripping the railing.

He feels the barasingha-mark pulse through his chest. The half-body Stage III stroke forms — the antler-curve crossing from sternum to shoulder, the foreleg extending across the ribcage. But the mark forms cracked. There is a visible scar through the centre of the antler-curve — a single line of skin where the bond's ink does not settle. The mark records, in the bond's own writing, what the dissolution cost.

He looks down at the cracked mark. He says quietly, to himself: "So we will carry this."

The Indian Paradise Flycatcher — MP's sacred bird, with its two impossibly long tail feathers — flies in through the assembly's open ceiling vent and lands on Sutrakara's shoulder in the gallery. The bird's tail-feathers trail down past her knees. The bird sings once. The Stage I hoofprint — the daughter's own first mark — will form on her upper back over the next week. She is not yet a hero. She is the kingdom's witness.

The famine-relief convoys leave Bhopal forty-six hours after the dissolution. The first convoy reaches Damoh-Kunwariya the following Monday with twelve trucks of rice and chickpea-flour. Local volunteers distribute. The Kunwariya headman — whose name is Lakhan Singh, sixty-one — weeps when the first sack of rice is opened.

Over the next eight weeks the death-count rises to forty-seven thousand before slowing. The single emergency-court resolves a hundred and fourteen cases in those eight weeks — more than the twelve-court system resolved in the preceding six. Aranya-Devi is appointed deputy chief justice on the third day and is by week six effectively running daily operations. Vidura-Babu of UP sends two of his best Awadhi administrators on day three of the dissolution; they arrive in Bhopal with a written note from Gangaputra (delivered by Sarus Crane carrier — the network is back in operation).

The note says: Brother. I heard you broke the staff. I heard you broke the courts. I broke a crown last month. We seem to be in the breaking business. Vidura-Babu sends his two best administrators. Send word when you need more. Tell Sutrakara her cousin Yamunadatta will write to her when his calligraphy improves. — G.

Dvadashashringa reads the note. He laughs for the first time in nine weeks. He passes the note to Sutrakara. She reads it. She does not laugh, but she smiles. She begins drafting a reply that night. The reply takes her four days because her calligraphy is already excellent and she wants to make sure Yamunadatta's is up to the standard.

Two weeks after the dissolution. Kanha National Park, north-east of Mandla. A herd of sixty barasingha — one of the larger surviving herds in the park — is reported by a junior forest-guard as standing motionless in a clearing south of the Sonf meadow.

The guard reports it on Monday morning. By Tuesday afternoon it has not moved. By Wednesday it has not moved. By Sunday it has not moved.

The herd is unhurt. The animals are not in visible distress — their breathing is normal, their eyes track moving objects, they are not dehydrated. They are simply not doing what grazing animals do. They are facing east. They are standing.

By the seventh day the news has reached Bhopal. Aranya-Devi sends a telegram to Dvadashashringa: Maharaj. Court XI matter that no longer exists. Herd not behaving. Will you visit?

He goes alone. He drives the eight hours from Bhopal himself, in an old Mahindra he keeps because his father kept the same model. He arrives at the Sonf meadow on the herd's ninth motionless day.

The herd is exactly as the guard reported. Sixty animals, antlers raised, facing east. The morning mist is lifting off the meadow. The animals stand in the mist as if they were carved.

Dvadashashringa walks to the centre of the herd. The animals do not flinch. He has the barasingha-bond — the only person in the kingdom strong enough in the bond to read what they are doing.

He reads it.

The herd has been caught in the gap between knowing-the-question and answering-it. They sensed the famine. They sensed the courts arguing. They began to ask, in the herd's collective sense, what to do. The asking did not arrive at an answer because the kingdom's answering-mechanism — the twelve courts — had broken. They have been frozen in the middle of asking for eleven days.

The Void Maw lives in that gap. The Maw is what arrives when the asking and the answering separate too far. The herd has not been taken; they have been paused.

On the twelfth day, late in the afternoon, one of the barasingha lowers her head and begins to graze. The others follow within ten minutes. None of them remembers the eleven days. The pause-time is a blank in the herd's behavioural memory — visible through the bond as an empty interval.

Dvadashashringa orders a small shrine built at the southern edge of the clearing. Sandstone and packed earth. A single barasingha-antler carved in low relief on the eastern face — the direction the herd had been facing. The Kanha forest guards become the shrine's keepers. The shrine remains.

A month after the herd returned to grazing. Bhimbetka — the rock-shelter site forty kilometres south of Bhopal, where central Indian people painted on stone for thirty-five thousand years.

Dvadashashringa takes Sutrakara to Bhimbetka on her thirteenth birthday. They climb up to Shelter III — the one with the famous hunter-and-bull painting, the one their family has visited four times since Sutrakara was six.

They sit on the stone bench inside Shelter III. The painting is on the wall in front of them — a single hunter, painted in red ochre, facing a single bull, painted in white. The hunter holds a spear. The bull's horns are forward. The painting is eight thousand years old by the conservative dating, twelve thousand by the unconservative.

He says: "You were right. The painting shows one person acting."

She looks at it. She has looked at it eight or ten times in her life and she has thought about it carefully every time.

She says: "Pita-shri. The painting also shows the bull. The bull is the famine. The hunter is you. The hunter is alone but the painting was made by a tribe. The tribe is what remembers. The tribe is what painted. The painting is older than the hunter and older than the bull. The painting is what makes the act dharmic instead of just an act."

He looks at her. She is thirteen as of this morning. The Indian Paradise Flycatcher — the one that landed on her shoulder at the assembly dissolution, the one with the impossibly long tail feathers — has followed them to Bhimbetka. The bird is perched on the stone-shelter's ledge above the painting. The bird does not sing. The bird listens.

He puts his arm around her. She leans into him. She has been twelve and she is now thirteen and she has been correct about the hunter and the bull and the tribe in the same paragraph.

He says: "I will carry the broken staff into every Council session. I will not have it repaired."

"Good."

"You will sit beside me at every Council session."

"I know."

"You will be twelve again in three more sessions and then you will be thirteen again."

She smiles. The wordplay is one of theirs — old, comfortable, mathematician's wordplay about how she will always have been twelve in the same way her father will always have been forty-six in the moment of dissolution. The mark on his chest will always be cracked. The painting will always be eight thousand years old in the conservative dating.

The bird sings — one short note, the call of the Indian Paradise Flycatcher that birders write down as tszip-twee. The note is very small in the size of Bhimbetka's rock-shelters. The note is very large in the size of the chapter.

Twelve thousand kilometres away — or at any rate considerably south — in Kanha National Park, sixty barasingha graze.

The Pilot's Closing Image

A father and a thirteen-year-old daughter sitting on a stone bench inside an eight-thousand-year-old rock shelter. A red-ochre hunter facing a white bull on the wall. An Indian Paradise Flycatcher on the stone-shelter ledge above the painting, its two impossibly long tail-feathers trailing past the rock's edge. The barasingha-mark cracked on the father's chest. The Dwadash staff in three pieces wrapped in saffron cloth at his feet. The tribe that painted the painting gone for eight thousand years but visible in every red-ochre line. The hunter is alone but the painting was made by a tribe. The tribe is what remembers. The tribe is what painted. The painting is what makes the act dharmic instead of just an act.
