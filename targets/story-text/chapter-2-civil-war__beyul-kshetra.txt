Rumtek Monastery, Gangtok district, twenty-four kilometres south-east of the city. The first week of October. The inner sanctum is a windowless stone chamber three metres by three metres at the back of the main prayer hall. Two butter-lamps burn at the north and south walls. A single low altar of black slate runs along the east wall. On the altar, in a square-cut wooden housing the size of a child's hand, sits the Chintamani — the wish-fulfilling jewel of Avalokitesvara, the Beyul-Kshetra's primary relic, the Hidden Land's heart-stone, the thing that has remained inside this small chamber without a single hour's absence since 1959.

Kanchenjunga is at the altar at four-fifteen a.m. He is forty-one. He has been senior Beyul-guardian at Rumtek for nine years. He is in the dark-red guardian-robe with the seven-fold sash of the Karmapa lineage. His head is shaved. His hands are calloused. He has been in this chamber every dawn for three thousand two hundred and eighty-seven consecutive days.

He sees the Chintamani in its housing. He places his right palm on the altar-edge in the morning's first gesture of recognition. He does not touch the jewel. He has never touched it. No guardian has touched it in sixty-seven years.

He turns and leaves the chamber. The day's schedule is the same as every other day's. Morning prayers at four-thirty. The refugee-blessing line at six. The Tuesday community-kitchen at ten. The Wednesday camp-rotation at the south camp at one. The Thursday and Friday camp-rotations at the central and west camps. The administrative council with the Sikkim state government at five on alternating weeks.

At noon today the monastery will hold a Special Refugee-Blessing Ceremony for newly-arrived refugees from the autumn Tibetan-border surge. Two hundred and twelve refugees are scheduled to receive the blessing in three rotating batches. The Chintamani will be brought into the main prayer hall for the ceremony — not exposed, but carried in its housing on a small ceremonial palanquin from the inner sanctum to the central altar where it will sit visible (in housing) throughout the three-hour ceremony.

This is the procedure. It has been the procedure for sixty-seven years. The Chintamani is brought out four times a year for major blessing-ceremonies. The autumn refugee-blessing is one of those four times.

Twelve thousand. That is the count of refugees currently registered in the three south-Sikkim camps as of the morning of October the first. Approximately two thousand from the autumn Tibetan-border surge alone. Approximately five thousand long-standing Tibetan refugees from the 1959 wave and its descendants. Approximately two thousand from the 2008 Lhasa-unrest wave. Approximately one thousand from various Bhutan-border irregularities. Approximately one thousand identified as "ethnic-mixed" — refugees whose paperwork is unclear and who have therefore been registered as Beyul-Kshetra ward-residents under the monastery's nine-century-old compassion-charter.

The monastery has absorbed every one of them. The compassion-charter, originally written by Padmasambhava's commentators in the eighth century and re-ratified by the Karmapa lineage in 1962, admits no refugee-rejection on any ground. Every applicant is welcomed. Every applicant's family is welcomed if they arrive. Every applicant's political enemy is welcomed too. The Beyul-Kshetra has no enemies. The Beyul-Kshetra is the absence of the question of enemies.

Kanchenjunga has worked within the charter for nine years. He has welcomed two refugee Chinese-state-intelligence agents (identified by central Indian intelligence three months after arrival, then welcomed again as protected ward-residents). He has welcomed one former-Tibetan-monastery-corrupt-treasurer (who confessed to the corruption on his second week and was welcomed exactly as he had been welcomed the first week). He has welcomed a former-state-government Sikkim official accused of land-grab against the Hidden Land's own forest-boundary (welcomed; reabsorbed; placed in the camp-administration cell where his expertise on Sikkim land-records made him the camp's most effective ration-clerk).

He has welcomed all of them. The charter requires it. The charter is not a guideline. The charter is the kingdom.

In the spring of 2024, two and a half years before the chapter's opening, a man in his forties arrived at the south refugee camp with a small backpack and a thirty-page Tibetan-language autobiography written in pencil in a school-exercise book. He gave his name as Krodha-Sherpa. He said he had been born in Pelliang Village in the Tibetan-occupied zone and that his entire family had been killed in a Chinese border-clearance operation in 2018. He had walked out across the Cho La pass alone in the winter of 2022. He had spent the intervening two years in Lhasa-region Buddhist monasteries doing manual labour and waiting for a route to the Indian side.

He was welcomed. The intake clerk — a junior monk named Lobsang — logged him in the camp-administration database at intake-number 9,847. He was assigned to barrack 17-C, bed 3. His ration-card was issued the same afternoon. His autobiography was filed in the camp-archive in box 142 along with eleven hundred other recently-arrived testimonials.

He was organised. He was articulate. He spoke Tibetan-fluent, Hindi-fluent, Nepali-fluent, Sikkimese-Bhutia-fluent. He read Sanskrit and English. He was not a monk, but he had a monk's vocabulary. Within his first three months at the south camp he had started a Tibetan-language literacy class for refugee children whose schooling had been interrupted by the border crossing. Within his first six months he had organised a mutual-aid council across barracks 17 and 18, eight hundred refugees pooling ration-card supplements to support the most dependent among them. Within his first year he was running a weekly assembly for the south-camp's twelve hundred adult-male refugees, called the Compassion-Reciprocity Council, that met every Sunday evening in barrack 17-C's common room.

The other refugees began to call him the Refugee Father. The phrase began informally and became official by the end of his second year. By the autumn of 2026 — the chapter's opening — the Compassion-Reciprocity Council had grown to forty-seven hundred active members across all three south-Sikkim camps. The Refugee Father's Sunday assemblies were attended by more refugees than the monastery's own bi-weekly camp-administration meetings.

Kanchenjunga had visited the Compassion-Reciprocity Council twice. The first visit, eight months earlier, he had sat at the back of the common room and listened to the Refugee Father explain the council's ration-supplement allocation procedures. He had been impressed. The procedures were more efficient than the monastery's. The Refugee Father's administrative grasp was first-rate.

The second visit, three months earlier, he had stayed for the Refugee Father's closing-prayer. The closing-prayer was a Tibetan-language compassion-prayer attributed to the Sixth Karmapa, recited in the modern unision style. Kanchenjunga had joined the recitation. The Refugee Father had stood at the front and led it without faltering.

Kanchenjunga had walked back to the monastery that evening reassured. The kingdom's compassion-charter was producing its expected fruit. The most administratively-capable refugee in the camps was using his capability to multiply the camp's self-care. The Beyul-Kshetra was working.

He had not asked about the backpack. He had not asked about the autobiography. He had not asked the intake clerks for Krodha-Sherpa's pre-2022 history. He had not, in nine years of guardianship, ever asked any refugee about his pre-arrival history. The charter forbade it.

The Special Refugee-Blessing Ceremony begins at noon on the chapter's opening day — the first Sunday of October. Two hundred and twelve new refugees are arranged in three rotating batches of seventy-one, seventy, and seventy-one. The Chintamani is brought into the main prayer hall in its ceremonial palanquin at eleven-forty-five a.m. and placed on the central altar. The housing is opened to half-visibility — the jewel's side is exposed to the assembled refugees but not its top or its underside (the orthodox half-disclosure that has been the ceremonial standard since 1959).

The hall holds about a thousand people. Two hundred and twelve refugees in the batch-area at the front. Six hundred and ninety long-standing camp residents and monastery monks at the rear. Approximately a hundred state-government and visiting-observer guests in a side gallery on the north wall. The Refugee Father is in the front row of the rear seating, beside the senior Compassion-Reciprocity Council delegates.

Kanchenjunga performs the blessing in the standard procedure. Each refugee approaches the altar in turn, bows three times, receives the Chintamani-touched compassion-water from the senior monk's ladle on the right of the altar, drinks it, returns to the seating area. The procedure takes about thirty-five seconds per refugee. Seventy-one refugees per batch. Three batches. The ceremony is scheduled to end at three p.m.

At one-fourteen p.m., during the second batch, between refugee forty-three and refugee forty-four, the hall's south-wall butter-lamps flicker. The flicker lasts about two seconds. Both lamps. Simultaneously. The hall's ventilation does not normally produce flickers of this kind in the south-wall lamps; the prevailing draft at noon enters from the north door. Several of the monastery's senior monks notice the flicker; none stops the ceremony; the flicker is logged later as "atmospheric."

At one-forty-seven p.m. the second batch concludes. The hall enters its standard fifteen-minute interval for the seating-rotation. Two senior monks remain at the central altar. The Chintamani is still in its half-opened housing. The Refugee Father stands and walks slowly toward the south-wall butter-lamp — the one that had flickered — ostensibly to adjust the wick. He bows to the senior monk at the south side of the altar in passing. The senior monk — Lobsang, the same junior monk who had logged Krodha-Sherpa's arrival two and a half years earlier — bows back. The Refugee Father continues to the lamp. He adjusts the wick. The lamp resumes its normal burn.

At one-fifty-three p.m. the third batch begins. The procedure continues.

At three p.m. the ceremony concludes. The Chintamani is returned to its housing, the housing is sealed, the palanquin is carried back to the inner sanctum by the standard four-monk procession with Kanchenjunga at the head.

Kanchenjunga opens the housing in the inner sanctum to perform the closing-of-day verification. He has performed this verification three thousand two hundred and eighty-seven consecutive times.

The housing is empty.

Forty-seven days. The Chintamani is missing from the inner sanctum for forty-seven days. The monastery has not announced the theft. The state-government has not been informed. Two of the senior monastery monks have been informed. Lobsang the intake-clerk-and-senior-blessing-attendant is one. The senior administrative monk, Sonam, is the other. No one else.

Kanchenjunga has decided to keep the absence private for as long as possible. The decision is not deception; it is triage. If the Chintamani's absence is announced to the refugee population, the camps will riot. Twelve thousand refugees will read the absence as the end of their welcome. They will conclude that the monastery has lost the relic that anchored their compassion-charter. They will leave the camps in panic. Half of them will die in unprepared border-crossings. The math is concrete. He has done it.

The decision is to keep the absence quiet and search the monastery, the camps, the state-government compound, the surrounding villages, in concentric rings, beginning with the closest. Lobsang and Sonam have searched the monastery building-by-building for nine days. They have found nothing. They have searched the south, central, and west camps barrack-by-barrack for another twelve days. They have found nothing. They have searched the surrounding villages with the cooperation of the Sikkim state-government chief secretary (who has been informed under strict confidentiality) for sixteen days. They have found nothing.

At forty-six days, just before dawn on the forty-seventh day, a girl arrives at the monastery's east gate.

She is sixteen years old. She has walked the four kilometres from the south camp on foot. She is wearing the standard camp-issue brown sweater and dark trousers. Her hair is uncovered. She carries no bundle, no bag, no paper. She has come empty-handed.

She is Tibetan. Her name is Pema-Chöden. She has been at the south camp for three months — arrived in the autumn surge, intake-number 11,438. Her parents and her older brother were killed in the same border-crossing she survived. Her brother was fifteen. He had been trying to help their mother across the third river-crossing on the western route. He had been shot by a Chinese border-patrol unit at the moment of helping her up. Their mother had been shot in the same burst. Their father had been shot at the same crossing on the way back trying to recover their bodies. Pema-Chöden had been on the eastern bank with their younger cousin Tashi. The two of them had walked the remaining sixty-four kilometres to the Sikkim border on foot over twelve days.

She had taken her brother's name as a middle-name on her camp-intake form. The form had her listed as Pema-Wangchuk-Chöden. Wangchuk was her brother's name. She had explained the addition to Lobsang at intake by saying: "He should keep arriving in my paperwork. He did not make it past the river. He should arrive everywhere I arrive."

She had been a quiet camp-resident for three months. She had not attended the Compassion-Reciprocity Council's Sunday assemblies. She had not attended the Tibetan-language literacy class. She had attended the monastery's twice-weekly meditation hour and sat at the back. The senior monastery monks knew her by sight. Kanchenjunga had recognised her at the blessing-ceremony forty-seven days earlier — she had been at the front of the second batch, refugee number twenty-three of the second batch. She had received the compassion-water and bowed in the standard procedure and returned to her seat.

She stands at the monastery's east gate at five-twelve a.m. The gate-keeper monk asks her the standard pre-dawn entry question. She answers: "I need to speak with Kanchenjunga-la. Alone. Now. Before the dawn-prayer."

The gate-keeper hesitates. The dawn-prayer is at five-thirty. Kanchenjunga is in the inner sanctum.

She says: "The Refugee Father is Krodha. I know because my brother went with him last week and came back wrong."

The gate-keeper opens the gate.

Kanchenjunga is at the inner sanctum altar when Lobsang brings her in. He is in his dawn-prayer position — right palm at the altar-edge, the absence of the Chintamani in front of him as it has been every dawn for forty-seven days. He has not slept the previous night. He has not slept properly in two weeks.

He turns when Lobsang opens the door. He sees the sixteen-year-old refugee girl at the entrance. He registers her face from the blessing-ceremony forty-seven days earlier. He registers that she is empty-handed. He registers that she has come at five-twelve a.m. unannounced.

He says, in his even guardian-voice: "Sit, daughter. The altar is your seat. Lobsang, the second tea."

She does not sit. She walks past the altar to the south wall and stops with her back to it. She faces him directly across the altar. The Chintamani's empty housing is between them.

She says, in Tibetan: "My brother Tashi — my cousin, not the one who died, the younger one, my mother's sister's son — went with the Refugee Father last week. The Refugee Father has been running a special evening sub-council for the new autumn boys for the past three months. The sub-council is at barrack 17-C, late, after the regular Compassion-Reciprocity Council closes. The new autumn boys are invited individually. The invitation is private. The boys who attend are told not to discuss it with the elders or the other refugees. Tashi attended for the first time twelve days ago. He came back to our barrack at three a.m. He did not speak for two days. On the third day he spoke. What he said was not Tashi."

Kanchenjunga says: "What did he say."

She says: "He said the Compassion-Charter was a Sikkimese lie. He said the Beyul was a Tibetan-fraud monastery built by collaborators with the 1959 expulsion. He said the senior monks were the inheritors of the lie. He said the Refugee Father was the only Tibetan in the kingdom who still knew what the original Beyul had been before the Karmapa lineage corrupted it in 1962. He said the Refugee Father had a plan to restore the original Beyul by removing the senior monks one by one over the next five years. He said the plan had begun last month when the Chintamani had been 'reclaimed for the original sanctum.' He said the Chintamani was now in the original sanctum and the senior monks did not know where the original sanctum was. He said the original sanctum was 'at the south camp, under the dawn-prayer-wheel.' He said I must come to the next sub-council on Tuesday or I would be considered a collaborator with the senior monks."

She pauses. She is breathing steadily. She has rehearsed the recitation.

She says: "He said this in the voice the Refugee Father had taught him. The voice was not Tashi's voice. Tashi's voice was higher and slower. The voice that spoke through Tashi was lower and faster, with the Refugee Father's exact cadence. I sat across the table from my own cousin and listened to the Refugee Father's voice come out of his mouth. He could not see me from the inside any more."

Kanchenjunga says: "How do you know the Refugee Father is Krodha."

She says: "In 2018, when I was eight, my mother took me to Pelliang Village to visit a great-uncle. We stayed for nine days. There was a man in the village who had been the village-school's deputy headmaster. He had been removed from the position the year before because three of the village children had developed a strange silence after their tutoring with him. The village had been arguing about whether to expel him for two years. He was forty at that time. He had a small backpack he carried everywhere. He had a thirty-page autobiography he had written about himself which he showed to my mother on the third evening. The autobiography was about his childhood in a different village in a different region. My mother read three pages of it and told me afterwards that the autobiography was the most carefully-constructed lie she had ever read. She told me to remember his face. She made me draw it from memory the next morning. I drew it. The drawing is in my barrack-locker. The man in the village was Krodha. The Refugee Father is the same man. The face is the same."

She pauses. Kanchenjunga has not moved.

She says: "My mother is dead. My father is dead. My older brother is dead. My younger cousin is in the Refugee Father's sub-council and is not himself any more. I have come to you because no other senior monk would believe a sixteen-year-old refugee girl. You will believe me because the Chintamani is missing from the housing on the altar between us and you have not announced it to anyone outside three people and I am the fourth person to know in this monastery. I know the Chintamani is missing because the housing's wooden joints make a different sound when the chamber is closed empty than when the jewel is inside. I have heard the chamber closed full at the blessing-ceremony forty-seven days ago and I have heard the chamber closed empty when the senior monk Sonam carried the palanquin past my barrack at the south camp three nights ago when he was searching for the jewel. The empty sound is higher. Anyone who has been listening would know."

Kanchenjunga sits down on the stone bench against the east wall. He has not been told a more devastating thing in his life. He is sitting because his knees have signalled that they are not going to support his weight any longer.

He says: "The dawn-prayer-wheel at the south camp. The one beside barrack 17-C. The large one. The black-iron one. The pilgrim-wheel installed by the Karmapa lineage in 1967."

She says: "Yes."

He says: "The wheel is hollow. The Chintamani would fit in the upper chamber. The wheel is turned every dawn by approximately two hundred camp-residents. A jewel placed in the upper chamber would not be discoverable by the wheel-turners because the turning would not open the chamber. Only a specifically-shaped key opens the upper chamber. The key was discontinued from monastery use in 1989. Three keys still exist. One is in the monastery archive. The other two are in Lhasa and Dharamsala."

She says: "The Refugee Father showed Tashi a key on the second night of the sub-council. The key was the same shape as the discontinued key. He told Tashi the key had been 'recovered from the original sanctum's sealed cupboard.'"

Kanchenjunga is silent for forty seconds. Lobsang stands at the doorway. He has not breathed audibly during the entire recitation. He breathes now.

Kanchenjunga says: "Will you walk with me to the south camp at noon."

She says: "Yes."

He says: "The Refugee Father will be at the south camp's administration office at noon. The Sunday weekly council meeting begins at one. He will see us walk in. He will know what we are."

She says: "Yes."

He says: "The Compassion-Reciprocity Council members at the south camp will outnumber my monastery escort by approximately forty to one."

She says: "The Compassion-Reciprocity Council members are not all loyal to him. About half will go with him when you draw the boundary. The other half will stay."

He says: "How do you know."

She says: "The boys in his sub-council are loyal. The men who are running the older mutual-aid networks are not. My barrack-elder is one of those men. He has been concerned about the sub-council for six weeks. He has been waiting for someone like me to come and say what he could not say. He will speak when I speak. Three of the other barrack-elders will follow him."

Kanchenjunga looks at her for a long time.

He says: "Daughter, do you know that you have just become the Beyul-Kshetra's first refugee to hold what amounts to administrative office in its nine-century history."

She says: "Guardian-la, I have not asked for office. I have asked you to draw the boundary. The boundary is your work, not mine. My work is to walk beside you to the wheel. After that we will see what the kingdom requires."

He says: "The kingdom is going to require both. Sit down. Drink the second tea. Lobsang, bring the breakfast. We will walk to the south camp at eleven."

She sits down at the altar's edge. She drinks the second tea. The dawn-prayer is missed for the first time in three thousand two hundred and eighty-eight days. The senior monks at the main prayer hall hold the dawn-prayer without Kanchenjunga, in his absence, by his standing instruction (silent in the inner sanctum until he returns) issued through Lobsang at the doorway at five-thirty-three a.m.

The Beyul-Kshetra has taken its first action under conditions of internal infiltration. The first action is breakfast.

Eleven a.m. The monastery's east-gate path. The four-kilometre walk to the south camp passes the central tea-grove, the small village of Yang-Lhakhang, and the foothills approach where the path opens onto the south-camp plateau.

Kanchenjunga walks at the front. He is in the full guardian-robe, the seven-fold sash, the prayer-beads. He carries the Karmapa-line walking-staff he has not used in nine years — a yew-wood shaft one and a half metres long with a copper-cap at the top and a small bronze prayer-wheel at the lower-third. The staff is the senior guardian's formal walking-implement, used only for cross-kingdom processions and council-summons. He has summoned it from the monastery's ceremonial cupboard at nine a.m.

Pema-Chöden walks at his left shoulder, half a pace behind. She has been given a clean monastery ward-robe to wear over the camp-sweater — a knee-length deep-red over-robe that marks her as a guest of the senior guardian. The over-robe is not a refugee garment. It is the garment a state-government dignitary wears when received at the monastery for tea. She wears it because Kanchenjunga has insisted. The garment is its own boundary — the first boundary of the day, drawn between her and the camp she will return to in two hours' time.

Lobsang and Sonam walk at the rear, with four junior monks behind them. The state-government chief-secretary's deputy walks beside Sonam — the chief-secretary has been informed by Kanchenjunga at eight a.m. and has sent his deputy in a Sikkim-state-administration plain-clothes capacity. The deputy is unarmed. He has a satellite-phone in his sleeve-pocket and a single sealed envelope (the state-government's formal exile-of-foreign-national authorisation, pre-signed) in his inside-pocket.

The procession is not announced. The four kilometres are walked in approximately one hour and ten minutes — Kanchenjunga's normal walking pace. Pema-Chöden matches his pace. The other monks adjust to her pace as well.

They arrive at the south-camp's administration-perimeter at twelve-fifteen p.m. The administration-office is the central building of the camp — a single-storey concrete structure with a tin roof at the south-camp's central plaza. The dawn-prayer-wheel is forty metres east of the administration office, beside barrack 17-C, in the camp's eastern plaza.

The Refugee Father is at the administration-office's front step. He has been informed by his sub-council messengers (the runners are nine, twelve, and fourteen years old, refugee boys from barracks 14, 17, and 22) of the procession's approach from the second-kilometre mark onward. He has had forty-five minutes to prepare.

He is in his usual plain dark-red Pelliang-style tunic and dark trousers. He has on his wrist the small wooden prayer-bead bracelet that has marked him in the camp since 2024. He has on his face the calm reassuring expression that the camp has known him by for two and a half years.

About one thousand four hundred refugees have gathered in the central plaza by twelve-fifteen p.m. The Compassion-Reciprocity Council's senior delegates — about forty men — are arrayed in a semi-circle behind the Refugee Father. The mid-rank delegates and the sub-council boys — about a hundred and fifty — are arrayed behind the senior delegates. The general camp-population — the remaining twelve hundred — is arrayed in concentric rings further out.

The atmosphere is not yet hostile. The Refugee Father has framed the procession in advance to the senior delegates as "the senior guardian's autumn welfare-visit, accompanied by a young refugee girl whose case has come to the Compassion-Charter's special attention." The framing is plausible. The senior delegates are watching with respectful uncertainty.

Kanchenjunga stops six metres from the Refugee Father. Pema-Chöden stops one metre behind Kanchenjunga's left shoulder.

The Refugee Father bows in the Pelliang-style. The bow is technically perfect. The senior delegates bow in solidarity. Approximately three hundred of the mid-rank delegates and sub-council boys bow as well. About a thousand of the general camp-population bow, in scattered uneven groups.

Kanchenjunga does not bow. He plants the Karmapa-line walking-staff at his right side. He lifts his right palm.

He says, in Tibetan, in the senior-guardian formal-address voice that the camp has heard him use exactly four times in nine years (at the major blessing-ceremonies):

"I have come at noon to draw the first boundary of my guardianship. I have welcomed every refugee for nine years. I will continue to welcome every refugee who is here as a refugee. The Compassion-Charter is unchanged for them. The Beyul-Kshetra remains open. But I will not continue to welcome one of you. The one I will not welcome is the man standing in front of me. His name is Krodha. He arrived at this camp in 2024 under a forged Tibetan identity. He has organised a sub-council that has been recruiting boys to a doctrine of monastery-replacement and senior-monk-removal over the past three months. He has stolen the Chintamani from the inner sanctum at the autumn blessing-ceremony forty-seven days ago. He has hidden it in the upper chamber of the dawn-prayer-wheel beside barrack 17-C. He will leave the Beyul-Kshetra by sunset. He will cross the Lachen pass into the Bhutan-Tibet border region by sunset of the third day. The refugees who choose to follow him are also welcome to leave with him — the boundary is the man, not the followers, and any follower who wishes to remain in the Beyul-Kshetra without him is welcome to remain. The followers who choose to leave with him will not be barred from leaving and will be permitted to take their personal effects and a one-month ration allocation. The state-government has authorised the formal exile under the cross-border emergency-residence clause. The deputy chief-secretary will issue the exile-order to the Refugee Father in person at the conclusion of this address. The Chintamani will be recovered from the dawn-prayer-wheel by the camp's newly-appointed Beyul-Kshetra co-administrator, Pema-Chöden of the autumn surge, who will turn the wheel three full revolutions and open the upper chamber with the recovered Karmapa-line key. The Compassion-Charter is now amended for the first time in nine centuries: a refugee who has violated the inner sanctum is no longer welcome. The amendment is limited. The amendment is necessary. The amendment is mine to draw and I am drawing it."

The address takes seven minutes. The translation into the camp's ambient mix of Tibetan-Hindi-Sikkimese is held in the listeners' own minds; Kanchenjunga's Tibetan is the original, and the bilingual listeners translate for the non-Tibetan-speakers in real-time, whispering into neighbours' ears.

The Refugee Father's face changes through the seven minutes. The change is in stages.

At minute one (the announcement of the boundary), the face shows the calm reassuring expression with an added quarter-smile — the smile of a man who recognises a rhetorical move and is preparing his counter-rhetoric.

At minute two (the naming of the forged identity), the smile narrows. The calm-reassuring is replaced by a thin focused listening.

At minute three (the description of the sub-council), the focused listening contracts. The skin around the eyes tightens. The thin lips fold inward.

At minute four (the accusation of the Chintamani theft, with specific location), the face is no longer composed. The Refugee Father's right hand — the one holding the wooden prayer-bead bracelet — trembles for the first time. The senior delegates behind him register the tremble. Their faces begin to change.

At minute five (the offered exit, the freedom for followers to choose), the Refugee Father's face composes itself again — deliberately. He is recovering. He is preparing to speak.

At minute six (the announcement of Pema-Chöden as co-administrator), the Refugee Father turns his head ten degrees to the right. He looks at Pema-Chöden directly for the first time. The look lasts about two seconds. In that two seconds his face becomes the face of the village deputy headmaster from Pelliang in 2018 that Pema-Chöden drew from memory the next morning. The mask slips for two seconds, then resets.

At minute seven (the closing "the amendment is mine to draw and I am drawing it"), the Refugee Father makes the choice that decides the rest of his Chapter Three arc. He chooses to leave.

He bows. The bow is again Pelliang-style. The bow is shallower than the opening bow. It is the bow of a man who has accepted a defeat and is signalling, to those who will follow him, that they should also accept it for the time being.

He says, in his even camp-voice: "Senior guardian. I accept the boundary. I will leave by sunset. Those who choose to follow me will be welcome to follow. Those who choose to remain will be welcome to remain."

The Refugee Father's precise restraint is its own danger-flag. The senior delegates feel it. About half of them step back from him in the next thirty seconds — one half-pace, then another, then a third. The other half do not move.

By twelve-twenty-five p.m. the central plaza has bifurcated. The Refugee Father is at the south side of the plaza with about seven hundred refugees clustered around him. Kanchenjunga is at the north side of the plaza with about seven hundred refugees clustered around him, with Pema-Chöden at his left shoulder, with the four monastery monks behind, with the deputy chief-secretary at his right side reading from the pre-signed exile-order.

The split is forty-eight to fifty-two. The Refugee Father takes the slightly-smaller half.

At one p.m. Pema-Chöden walks alone to the dawn-prayer-wheel.

The wheel is two metres in height, one and a half metres in diameter, black-iron with the standard Karmapa-line copper-banding. It sits in a stone enclosure beside barrack 17-C. About a hundred refugees are still at the wheel from the morning-prayer rotation, but the dense crowd has dispersed. The eastern plaza is quieter than the central plaza had been.

She has the recovered key in her right hand. Lobsang has retrieved it from the monastery archive at twelve-thirty p.m. and has run the four kilometres to the south-camp eastern plaza in forty-five minutes. The key is six centimetres long, brass, with a triangular bit and a copper-band ferrule.

She walks up to the wheel. She places her left palm on the iron drum at the wheel's east face. The drum has the standard mantra-inscription — OM MANI PADME HUM — in raised brass lettering across its three rotational bands. She turns the wheel three full revolutions in the clockwise direction. The wheel turns easily on its central axle. The turning takes about forty seconds.

She finds the upper chamber's key-slot at the top of the wheel's north face — a small triangular indentation in the brass cap. She inserts the key. She turns the key one full revolution counter-clockwise.

The upper chamber opens. The chamber is a small lined cavity in the top of the iron drum, about ten centimetres deep, lined with red velvet that has been undisturbed for thirty-seven years (the chamber was last opened in 1989 when the discontinuation-of-use was logged).

The Chintamani sits in the centre of the chamber. The jewel is the size of a chestnut. It is a deep blue-purple in colour, faceted, with the small Avalokitesvara-mark engraved at the side. It has the same exact appearance it has had for sixty-seven years.

Pema-Chöden lifts the jewel out of the chamber with both hands. She has never touched it before. The jewel is warm — warmer than the surrounding air, warmer than her hands. The warmth is consistent with the jewel having been inside the iron drum for forty-seven days, but warmer than that thermal explanation alone would predict.

She walks the forty metres back to the central plaza. She carries the jewel in both hands at the level of her chest. The Refugee Father's seven-hundred followers watch her cross the eastern plaza. About a hundred of them detach from the Refugee Father's cluster and walk back to the Kanchenjunga side of the central plaza. The Refugee Father does not stop them.

She arrives at Kanchenjunga's side at one-oh-six p.m. She holds the jewel out. He places his right palm above the jewel without touching it. He bows three times to the jewel. He says, in Tibetan: "Daughter. Carry it back to the inner sanctum. Open the housing. Place it inside. Close the housing. The dawn-prayer of the forty-eighth day will be performed at five-thirty tomorrow morning. You will be present at the inner sanctum doorway. I will perform the prayer. The prayer will be your second day of administrative office."

She nods. She tucks the jewel into the inside-pocket of the ward-robe Kanchenjunga had given her at the monastery. The pocket is positioned at her left chest — over her heart. The warmth of the jewel sits against her skin through the cotton-lining.

At one-oh-eight p.m., as Pema-Chöden straightens at his left shoulder with the jewel against her chest, Kanchenjunga feels the red-panda-bond pulse through his right collarbone.

The Stage III foreleg-mark begins to form. It begins at the right collarbone — the standard position for the red-panda-line bond — and arcs down across the right pectoral. The mark is the red panda's right forepaw — clawed, semi-extended, in the climbing-down position. The pad-prints at the base of the toes are visible. The five small claws are visible. The fur-pattern at the back of the paw is rendered in the standard Chapter-Two ink-density.

The mark forms in approximately eleven seconds beneath the guardian-robe. It is not visible to the assembled refugees. Kanchenjunga feels the formation as a slow warm pressure across the pectoral — not painful, not uncomfortable, but unmistakable. He has been waiting for this sensation for fourteen years. The waiting is finally over.

He does not show the moment on his face. He continues to address the deputy chief-secretary in the standard administrative-voice, finalising the Refugee Father's exile-paperwork procedures. The deputy chief-secretary signs the exile-order at one-twelve p.m. and serves it to the Refugee Father at one-fourteen p.m. The Refugee Father accepts the document with a Pelliang-style bow. He signs the acknowledgement. He keeps the duplicate copy.

By one-thirty p.m. the Refugee Father has assembled his approximately six hundred remaining followers (the hundred who had defected upon Pema-Chöden's wheel-walk are now confirmed in their defection) at the south-camp's southern gate. The followers are packing their personal effects. The state-government ration-allocation officers have begun issuing the one-month rations from the camp-storeroom. Each follower receives approximately fifteen kilograms of grain, three kilograms of butter, a small medical-kit, and a Sikkim-state border-crossing certificate.

By four p.m. the column has begun moving. The Refugee Father leads the column. His sub-council senior delegates are immediately behind him. The mid-rank delegates and sub-council boys follow. The general followers walk in a long staggered file behind. The column stretches approximately one and a half kilometres along the foothill trail leading toward the Lachen pass.

The Lachen pass is the northeastern crossing-point between Sikkim and the Bhutan-Tibet border zone. The pass is at four thousand seven hundred metres altitude. It is open to foot-traffic in October but closes to motor-vehicles by mid-November. The crossing on foot takes approximately three days from the south-camp's exit-point.

The Refugee Father's column reaches the pass on the morning of the third day. By the eleven a.m. checkpoint at the Lachen pass entry-station, the column has been reduced to approximately five hundred and ten followers — ninety more refugees have detached over the three-day walk and begun the return-trip to the south camp. The detached followers are mostly the mid-rank delegates and the general camp-population followers. The sub-council boys remain. The senior delegates remain.

At eleven-fifteen a.m. on the third day, at the Lachen pass entry-station, the Refugee Father signs his column out of the Beyul-Kshetra. The pass-station officers stamp each follower's border-crossing certificate. The stamping takes about two hours. The Refugee Father is the first to cross. His sub-council senior delegates follow. The column then proceeds in single-file order across the pass.

By two p.m. the last of the column has crossed. The Lachen pass is closed behind them. The Beyul-Kshetra's boundary has been drawn.

Kanchenjunga has not walked to the Lachen pass himself. He has remained at the monastery to perform the forty-eighth-day dawn-prayer with the Chintamani restored to the inner sanctum at five-thirty a.m. on the second day after the boundary-speech. The prayer takes one hour. Pema-Chöden stands at the doorway of the inner sanctum throughout the prayer. She watches the senior guardian perform the prayer she has heard him perform from outside the sanctum-walls four hundred and seventy times in three months. From inside the sanctum-doorway the prayer sounds different. The Chintamani in its housing is two metres in front of her. The senior guardian is at the altar between her and the jewel.

When the prayer concludes at six-thirty a.m. Kanchenjunga turns to face her. He gestures for her to come forward. She walks the two metres to the altar.

He says: "Daughter. The Beyul-Kshetra has a co-administrator. The co-administrator's office is at the south camp's administration-building. You will work there four days a week. You will work at the monastery's ward-coordination office on the other three days. Your first task is to draft the new Compassion-Charter amendment in writing. The amendment will be ratified by the monastery's senior monks council on the seventh day. The amendment text is yours to write. I will review it before ratification. You will be paid the standard ward-coordinator salary from the state-government's monastery-affairs budget. Your barrack-accommodation will be transferred from 17-C to the monastery's south-wing guest-quarters effective today. Your younger cousin Tashi will be assigned to the monastery's rehabilitation-school under Lobsang's supervision — the school's programme for ex-sub-council boys begins next week. He will recover. The recovery will take time. He will recover."

She bows. The bow is not Tibetan-formal. The bow is her own — the bow of a sixteen-year-old refugee girl who has just become the first refugee in the Beyul-Kshetra's nine-century history to hold institutional office.

She says: "Guardian-la. I will write the amendment. The first line will read: The Compassion-Charter remains open to all who suffer; the Charter closes to one who has violated the inner sanctum. I will consult with the senior monks council on the remaining lines."

He says: "The first line is correct. Begin."

The next ten days are an administrative re-organisation that the Beyul-Kshetra has not seen in sixty-seven years. The Compassion-Charter amendment is drafted by Pema-Chöden in a single thirty-eight-hour session at the south-camp administration-office, with Lobsang at her left and Sonam at her right and the Sikkim state-government chief-secretary's deputy at her right-second. The amendment is three pages long. The first page contains the four-clause limit. The second page contains the procedural-test for invoking the limit. The third page contains the appeals procedure.

The four-clause limit reads, in the final ratified text:

I. The Compassion-Charter is reaffirmed for all who arrive seeking refuge. II. The Charter closes only to a refugee who has knowingly violated the inner sanctum — defined as removing a sanctum-relic, deliberately deceiving the intake about identity-of-record, or organising a covert sub-council aimed at the removal of senior monks. III. The procedural-test for invoking Clause II is the senior guardian's public boundary-speech delivered in the central plaza of the affected camp with the state-government chief-secretary present and the affected refugee given the right of response. IV. The closure under Clause II is exile across the nearest international border-crossing with one month's ration-allocation. The exiled refugee's followers retain the right to choose to remain or to leave; followers who remain are reabsorbed into the camp without prejudice; followers who leave receive ration-allocation under the same terms as the exiled principal.

The amendment is ratified by the monastery's senior monks council on the seventh day after the boundary-speech. The vote is twenty-three to one. The single dissenting vote is cast by an elderly senior monk named Tenzin-Norbu (sixty-eight, the monastery's second-most-senior after Kanchenjunga) who has held the Compassion-Charter as unamendable for forty years. His dissent is recorded; he affirms his continued loyalty to the senior guardian and accepts the amendment as the majority will. He resigns from active senior-monks-council duties two days later in protest and takes up a meditation-cave residence in the central forest — a long-anticipated retirement that the chapter's events have accelerated by approximately two years.

The amendment text is published in the south, central, and west camps' bulletin-boards on the eighth day. The text is translated into Tibetan, Hindi, Sikkimese-Bhutia, Nepali, English, and Limbu (the six camp-spoken languages) by a six-translator team that Pema-Chöden assembles from the camp-population in a single afternoon.

The text is read aloud at the south camp's central plaza on the ninth day by Pema-Chöden herself, in Tibetan, in the late afternoon, with about eight hundred returned camp-residents in attendance. The reading takes nineteen minutes. The reading is followed by a senior monk's response-prayer led by Lobsang. The prayer is the Sixth Karmapa's compassion-prayer — the same prayer the Refugee Father had led at the Compassion-Reciprocity Council's closing nine months earlier when Kanchenjunga had walked back to the monastery reassured.

The reabsorption of the seven-hundred refugees who had remained begins on the tenth day. The reabsorption is administered through individual interviews with each adult refugee, conducted by the camp's newly-appointed Re-Integration Committee — eight Compassion-Reciprocity Council senior delegates who had stayed with Kanchenjunga at the boundary-speech, plus three monastery monks, plus Pema-Chöden as chair. The interviews are not interrogations. They are conversations about the refugee's pre-arrival history, the refugee's reasons for joining or not joining the sub-council, and the refugee's current intentions. The committee's standard is forgiveness-with-record: each refugee's account is written down, the account is filed in the camp-archive, no further action is taken unless the account itself indicates a violation under Clause II.

By the end of the second month, the Beyul-Kshetra has fully reabsorbed the seven-hundred remaining refugees. The seven-hundred have re-joined the camp-population's daily life. The Compassion-Reciprocity Council has been restructured under new leadership — a senior delegate named Karma-Topgyal (forty-seven, a Tibetan-Sikkimese mixed-line refugee from the 2008 Lhasa wave, a man Kanchenjunga had personally vetted for the new chair-position) leads the council, with two deputies drawn from the long-standing 1959-wave residents.

By the end of Chapter Two, the Beyul-Kshetra's political-and-spiritual structure has stabilised in a new configuration:

The senior guardian remains Kanchenjunga. He has earned his Stage III red-panda-foreleg mark by drawing his first boundary in nine years. The mark is visible on his right pectoral. The bond now sees a clear ladder-path toward Stage IV (the full-body red panda) by the end of Chapter Three or early Chapter Four — the bond's next test will be the Sanctuary's NE-Dominant ratification vote.

The co-administrator is Pema-Chöden. She is sixteen-and-three-months. She holds salaried administrative office in the Beyul-Kshetra's ward-coordination office four days a week and at the south-camp administration-building three days a week. She has begun studying for the Sikkim state-civil-service-junior-administrative-officer examination at Sonam's suggestion — the examination is in eighteen months. Lobsang is tutoring her in administrative-Tibetan; Sonam is tutoring her in state-administrative-Hindi; the deputy chief-secretary is tutoring her in state-government procedural protocol.

Her younger cousin Tashi has been at the monastery's rehabilitation-school for six weeks. He has begun to speak in his own voice again at week four. The Refugee Father's implanted cadence has begun to fade. The rehabilitation-school's sub-council-recovery programme — designed by Lobsang based on traditional Tibetan post-cult deprogramming methods used in the 1959 reabsorption — is the first formal programme of its kind in the Beyul-Kshetra's history. Eleven other ex-sub-council boys are enrolled with Tashi.

The Compassion-Charter is amended. The amendment is the first amendment in nine centuries. The amendment is limited. The amendment is necessary. The amendment is referenced in the cross-kingdom Sarus-pigeon correspondence between Kanchenjunga and Moirangthem at Imphal as the "Pema-Chöden amendment" — the name has stuck in the kingdom's administrative lexicon by the time of the Chapter Three Sanctuary convening.

The Chintamani is back in the inner sanctum. The forty-eight-day absence is logged in the monastery's archive as the Beyul-Kshetra's only sanctum-relic absence in three hundred and forty-one years (the previous absence had been an 1683 sanctum-relocation during the great Sikkimese earthquake). The forty-eight-day record will not be celebrated. The record will be remembered.

Three weeks after the boundary-speech, a Sarus-pigeon arrives at Rumtek from Imphal. The pigeon is the one Moirangthem has been using for cross-kingdom communications throughout the chapter. The letter is from Moirangthem himself.

The letter reads:

Senior-guardian-la. The pigeon-relay has carried news of the Pema-Chöden amendment from Gangtok to Imphal in twelve days. Kamalavarna and U-Khrawkmen have read the amendment and have asked me to convey their congratulations on the first amendment in nine centuries. The NE Dominant ratification at the Sanctuary in Chapter Three will be a six-kingdom assembly. The Beyul-Kshetra's vote will be the third of six. The other five are: Kamakhya (Kamalavarna), Imphal (Moirangthem, the candidate), Shillong (U-Khrawkmen as inheritor-regent), Kohima (Dzulevira), and the still-pending Tripura. The vote will not be unanimous unless yours is given. Will you give it.

Kanchenjunga writes the reply over two days. The reply is two paragraphs long.

NE Dominant Candidate. I will give my vote at the Sanctuary. The vote will be for you. The Beyul-Kshetra has stabilised after the Refugee Father's exile but the stabilisation is not yet certain — the Lachen pass remains the kingdom's open question, and Krodha at the pass's far side remains the kingdom's pending threat. The Sanctuary's convening will mark the threshold across which the NE Federation will need to coordinate on the Krodha-question. My vote is for you because you will be the regional voice for that coordination.

Sub-paragraph: Pema-Chöden will attend the Sanctuary as my co-administrator. She is sixteen-and-five-months at the Sanctuary's scheduled date. She will not vote — the Sanctuary's vote is the senior-guardian's alone — but she will be present. Please plan accommodation at the Sanctuary's administrative-quarters for her on the same terms as the other senior-guardians' aides. She will be the Sanctuary's youngest attendee. She will also be the Sanctuary's first refugee-attendee in three hundred years.

Senior-guardian-la, — K.

He sends the letter by the same Sarus-pigeon at sunset. Moirangthem receives it at Imphal at noon the next day. He writes the one-line reply: The aide's accommodation is arranged. We will receive her with full honour. He sends the reply by the pigeon returning, at dusk.

Two days after the Imphal correspondence, a second Sarus-pigeon arrives at Rumtek. The pigeon is from Kohima. The letter is from Dzulevira.

The letter is short.

Senior-guardian-la. The Pre-Tribal Pact in the Nagaland kingdom's Kohima state-archives now sits next to your Pema-Chöden amendment in the cross-kingdom mutual-records exchange that Mhabemo has set up with your Lobsang last week. The pact says: "Let no one of us claim purity above another." The amendment says: "The Charter closes to a refugee who has violated the inner sanctum." The pact is six hundred years old and the amendment is nineteen days old and both are saying the same thing in different vocabulary. The boundary you have drawn is the kingdom's right to refuse what is inside it that wishes to dissolve it. The pact I have walked is the kingdom's right to refuse what is outside it that wishes to subdivide it. Both refusals are dharma. Both refusals are also Stage III. I look forward to meeting Pema-Chöden at the Sanctuary. The Naga state-government will send her a Khonoma-clay tea-bowl as the Beyul-Kshetra's welcome-gift on the morning of the Sanctuary's opening.

Sister-in-Boundary, — D.

Kanchenjunga reads the letter twice. He shows it to Pema-Chöden at the south-camp administration-office at four p.m. on the day it arrives. She reads it three times. She does not say anything for nine minutes.

She finally says: "Guardian-la. Two of the Northeast's heroes have already written to me by name. I am sixteen-and-three-months. I do not know how to receive that yet."

He says: "Daughter. You will not know how to receive it for another year. The receiving is itself learned. You will learn it at the Sanctuary. Until then, draft the welcome-reply to Dzulevira. The reply is yours to write. I will only sign it."

She drafts the reply over the next hour. The reply is one paragraph long.

State Hero of Nagaland. The Beyul-Kshetra receives the Khonoma-clay welcome-gift with the gratitude due a six-century-old pact. The Pema-Chöden amendment is nineteen days old, and the writer of these words is sixteen-and-three-months old, but the kingdom's administrative record now contains both, and the cross-kingdom mutual-records exchange that your Mhabemo and our Lobsang have established will preserve them both for whatever future archive needs them. I look forward to meeting you at the Sanctuary. I will bring with me the small wooden housing the Chintamani has rested in for sixty-seven years — the housing is empty now, the jewel is restored to its altar-cavity — and I will bring the housing to the Sanctuary's opening as the Beyul-Kshetra's contribution to the regional mutual-records exhibit. The housing has been opened more times in the past forty-eight days than it has been opened in the previous nine centuries. The housing has earned its display.

Co-administrator, Beyul-Kshetra, — P.

Kanchenjunga signs the reply under her signature. The signature is his standard senior-guardian seal in Tibetan script. He sends the letter by the same Sarus-pigeon at sunset. The pigeon arrives at Kohima at noon the following day.

Six weeks after the boundary-speech. The first week of December. The western face of Khangchendzonga — the third-highest mountain in the world, the Beyul-Kshetra's deity-peak, the kingdom's sacred wall.

A Khangchendzonga National Park ranger named Tashi-Bhutia (no relation to Pema-Chöden's cousin Tashi — the name is a common Bhutia given-name; the ranger is forty-three, an eleven-year park-service veteran, the senior ranger of the western-face patrol-station) is conducting the standard December high-altitude wildlife-census on the morning of the second of December. He is at the western-face base camp at four thousand five hundred metres. He has a high-power binocular and a Park Service satellite-telemetry receiver. He is scanning the western face for the December baseline-count of high-altitude mammals — snow leopards (expected count: 3-4 in the western-face range), Tibetan blue sheep (40-60), Himalayan tahr (15-25), and the occasional opportunistic red panda (0-2, normally in the lower-elevation rhododendron belt).

At seven-forty-three a.m. he sees the red panda on the snowline edge at approximately five thousand four hundred metres altitude. The panda is on the western face. The panda is grazing on a small patch of rhododendron shrub that has somehow survived above the rhododendron belt's normal altitude limit of four thousand eight hundred metres — the shrub itself is anomalous; he logs the anomaly in his field-book as a secondary observation.

The panda is the primary observation. Red pandas live at two thousand two hundred to four thousand eight hundred metres altitude. Above five thousand metres the atmospheric oxygen-content (approximately fifty percent of sea-level oxygen at five thousand four hundred metres) is physiologically lethal to the species. Red pandas have not been documented above five thousand metres in the species' recorded biological history. The biological reason is straightforward: red panda haemoglobin lacks the high-altitude oxygen-affinity mutation that yak haemoglobin and Tibetan-antelope haemoglobin have evolved over the past five million years. A red panda at five thousand four hundred metres should be dead within thirty minutes from acute mountain-sickness combined with cold-stress.

The panda Tashi-Bhutia is observing is not dead. The panda is grazing. The panda's breathing rate is, through the binocular, observable: approximately fourteen breaths per minute, which is the normal red-panda resting rate. The panda is not in distress. The panda is, simply, at five thousand four hundred metres, on a Khangchendzonga rhododendron-patch, eating.

Tashi-Bhutia calls in the observation by satellite-radio to the Park Service's Gangtok central-office at eight a.m. The Gangtok central-office routes the call to the Sikkim state-government wildlife-affairs department by eight-thirty a.m. The wildlife-affairs department routes the call to the Rumtek Monastery's ward-coordination office at nine-fifteen a.m. (the monastery has been the standing recipient of all anomalous-wildlife observations on Khangchendzonga since 1967, by an inter-departmental memorandum-of-understanding the chief-secretary's office had set up after a similar anomalous observation in the 1962 Karmapa-lineage re-ratification year).

Pema-Chöden takes the call at the ward-coordination office at nine-sixteen a.m. She writes down the observation in her co-administrator log-book. She walks the observation across the monastery courtyard to the inner sanctum at nine-twenty a.m. She does not enter the sanctum; she stands at the doorway and waits for Kanchenjunga to finish his morning-prayer.

He finishes at nine-thirty-six a.m. He turns. He sees her at the doorway.

She says, in the standard report-voice that she has been using for two months now: "Guardian-la. The Khangchendzonga western-face patrol-station has logged a red panda at five thousand four hundred metres altitude. The panda is alive. The panda is grazing. The panda is not in distress."

Kanchenjunga sits down on the stone bench against the east wall.

The red-panda-foreleg mark on his right pectoral pulses. The pulse is not pain. The pulse is recognition.

He says: "Daughter. The Maw has begun to test the bond. The Maw is moving a red panda to an altitude that should kill it, to see whether the bond-ladder protects the animal at altitudes the species itself cannot survive. If the panda lives, the Maw will know the bond is stronger than the species. The Maw will then begin moving other bonded animals to other lethal-to-the-species conditions — the Sangai deer at the Loktak Lake's saline-flood zone, the Mithun bull at the Kanha barasingha-meadow oxygen-saturation, the clouded-leopard at the Cherrapunji rainfall-anti-zone. The next sign is the Maw reading the saga's bond-architecture by direct experiment on the bonded species. The next sign is the Maw learning what we can do."

She says: "What do we do."

He says: "The standard. The Karmapa-rule. We name the panda. We log the altitude. We build a small shrine at the western-face base camp at four thousand five hundred metres — the closest a guardian can walk. We do not climb to five thousand four hundred metres to verify or interfere. The panda is the Maw's offered sign and our task is to receive it without converting it into a confrontation. The shrine will be a small stacked-stone marker with a single red-panda-paw-print carved on the eastern face. We will name the marker the Khangchendzonga Boundary Shrine. We will commission the shrine through the Park Service this week. Tashi-Bhutia the ranger will be the shrine-keeper. He will check on the panda's grazing-spot once a week through the binocular from the four-thousand-five-hundred-metre base camp. He will not climb higher. We will not climb higher. We will read the sign at the altitude it was given."

She nods. She writes the procedure in the co-administrator log-book at nine-forty-two a.m.

She says: "Guardian-la. If the Maw is reading our bond-architecture by experiment, the next experiments will not be limited to the Northeast. They will spread. The Karnataka bond, the Goan gaur, the Maharashtra giant-squirrel, the UP barasingha, the MP barasingha-herd, the Assam rhinoceros, the Meghalaya clouded-leopard, the Manipur sangai, the Naga mithun — all of them are vulnerable. We need to inform the other kingdoms."

He says: "Yes. Draft the cross-kingdom Sarus-pigeon-cascade alert. Send it to all nine bonded-kingdom senior guardians. The text should be short. Eight or nine lines maximum. Begin: The Maw has begun to test the bond by experiment. The first experiment is a red panda at five thousand four hundred metres on Khangchendzonga. The animal is alive. The bond is holding. Watch your own bonded species at conditions that should kill them. Build shrines at the threshold. Do not climb past it."

She drafts the cascade-alert in fifteen minutes. The alert is sent by Sarus-pigeon to all nine bonded-kingdom senior guardians by sunset of the same day. By the morning of the third day after the Khangchendzonga panda observation, every senior guardian in the saga has read the alert.

The Beyul-Kshetra's closing image of Chapter Two is a sixteen-year-old refugee girl writing a cross-kingdom alert in administrative Tibetan at a co-administrator's desk while a forty-one-year-old senior guardian watches a small red-panda mark fade and re-pulse on his right pectoral beneath the dark-red guardian-robe, the Chintamani burning steady in its housing in the inner sanctum forty-six metres away, and somewhere on the Lachen pass's far side a man named Krodha walking the Bhutan-Tibet trail toward a transformation he has not yet recognised but the Maw has already prepared for him.

The Hidden Land has drawn its first boundary in nine centuries. The boundary has held.

The next sign comes within the week.

The Pilot's Closing Image

A red panda at five thousand four hundred metres on a rhododendron-patch that should not exist, grazing as if the altitude were ordinary. A sixteen-year-old refugee girl at a co-administrator's desk writing the cross-kingdom alert in administrative Tibetan. A small wooden housing in an inner sanctum that has been opened more times in forty-eight days than in nine centuries previous. A senior guardian's right pectoral marked with the first foreleg of the red-panda Stage III, the ink-dense paw set against weathered skin. A six-hundred-year-old clay tablet at Kohima sitting in a mutual-records exchange beside a nineteen-day-old amendment from Gangtok. A column of six hundred followers crossing the Lachen pass at noon on the third day with a forty-one-year-old man named Krodha leading them. A small stone shrine at four thousand five hundred metres on Khangchendzonga's western face with a single red-panda-paw-print carved on its eastern face, a Park Service ranger named Tashi-Bhutia checking the panda's grazing-spot through a binocular once a week, never climbing higher.
