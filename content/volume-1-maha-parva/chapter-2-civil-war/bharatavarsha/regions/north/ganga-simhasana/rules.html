The Barasingha-Bond — Held in Both Hands

The barasingha-bond of Ganga-Simhasana does not transfer at the Imambara assembly. It splits its attention — the same bond, now visible on two bodies. Gangaputra keeps the bond he has carried since Stage I; Yamunadatta receives his own first-stroke when the Sarus Crane lands on his shoulder. The bond is the kingdom’s, not the king’s. The kingdom now has two living bond-bearers. This is the first split-bond in the saga.

Father • Stage III Complete • Regent
Gangaputra — The Barasingha-Foreleg
Carried since Ch 1 Stage I. Stage II completed at the Triveni Sangam victory. Stage II.5 stalled for twelve months. Completes to half-body Stage III at the Imambara half-kneel — the foreleg extends from his sternum across to his right shoulder, the antler-tip just touching the curve of his neck. The mark is earned by surrender, not victory. Will hold this stage through Chapters Three and Four; the Stage IV full-body mark waits for the Maha-Adhipati seat at Kurukshetra, which Yamunadatta will eventually carry.
Son • Stage I Begun • Maharaj
Yamunadatta — The Barasingha-Hoofprint
No prior bond — the boy has lived in Vrindavan for fourteen years with no animal-bond contact. The Sarus Crane’s landing on his shoulder at the Imambara is the bond’s announcement; the hoofprint forms on his upper-back over the following week. Stage I — The Hoofprint. Stages II, III, IV await Chapters Three and Four. The bond does not contest his father’s claim — the same barasingha-line in central UP has held twelve generations of dual-bonded heir-and-regent pairs; the precedent is old and quiet.
Saga-Rule Established This Chapter • The Split-Bond Mechanic
A bond may bear two living humans at the same time if and only if: (1) one is the active regent or protector, (2) the other is the named-and-acknowledged throne-holder, (3) both are of the bond’s native bloodline, (4) one (the regent) has already passed Stage II of the ladder, (5) the other (the throne-holder) is beginning the ladder freshly. The bond does not split its power; it splits its attention. The barasingha now reads the battlefield through Gangaputra’s feet and listens to court through Yamunadatta’s ears. This is distinct from the Maharashtra double-bond (two different animals on one body); it is one animal across two bodies. Both mechanics will be relevant at the Sanctuary convening in Chapter Three.

The Two Tattoo Paths — Father’s Completion, Son’s Beginning

The same animal. Two ladders. The father’s climbs sideways — he does not need the Stage IV mark, because the regent’s role does not require it. The son’s climbs forward — he is the throne-holder, and his Stage IV is the kingdom’s future.

The Dwadash-Mukut — What It Was, What It Is Now

Before the Imambara
The Twelve-Sun Crown of Bloodline B
A circlet of twelve solar-gold filigree spokes mounted on a band of carbon-steel inlaid with twelve gold-leaf surya-discs — one per sun-king of the Bloodline-B line back to Sagar. Carried by every reigning Maharaj of Ganga-Simhasana since 1947. Worn at all formal state occasions. Gangaputra has worn it for two years.
After the Imambara
The Maharaj’s Crown, Held by Yamunadatta
Now on Yamunadatta’s head, placed there by his father in the half-kneel of dharmic transfer. The boy does not wear it daily — it is too heavy for fourteen-year-old shoulders and his mother explicitly forbids the daily wear. It sits on a side-table in his bedchamber, and he wears it only at council openings and at the four state-occasions of the year. The kingdom approves of the restraint.

Sacred Weapons — Inherited Differently

Ancient Weapon • Now the Regent’s
Dvadasha-Ratha — The Chariot-Staff
Type
A twelve-pronged ceremonial staff in Gangetic-plain ironwood — the chariot-driver’s tool, traditionally carried by the regent or protector rather than the throne-holder. Twelve prongs for the twelve sun-kings the Dwadash-Mukut commemorates.
Ability
Functions as a battle-staff in combat, a flag-pole in ceremony, and a chariot-driver’s steering-pole in actual driving. The Krishna-shadow weapon — the tool of the one who guides, not the one who is guided.
Now Carried By
Gangaputra. He picked it up the morning after the Imambara from the regent’s armoury — a chamber that has not been used in seventy years. The staff was waiting.
Modern Weapon • The Court Tool
Sangam-Tantra — The Confluence-Net
Type
A state-administered information network connecting Lucknow, Banaras, and Mathura through encrypted satellite, fiber, and (in three cases of high-sensitivity counsel) Sarus Crane carrier-pairs.
Ability
Routes around any single point of failure. Vidura-Babu’s instrument — he uses it through the chapter to keep Lucknow administration intact even as the Banaras religious order and the Mathura Krishna-cult publish conflicting positions. The network survives the chapter without compromise.
Now Operated By
Vidura-Babu (administratively) and the new Mathura-Vrindavan third-party arbitration seat (dharmically). Yamunadatta has the operating credentials — he is the throne-holder — but he does not use them in Chapter Two. The boy is learning, not yet operating.
Void Maw Sign • Catalogue Entry • Uttar Pradesh
The Clear Ganga. On the morning after the Imambara assembly, the Ganga at Varanasi runs glass-clear from Assi Ghat to Manikarnika Ghat — the full length of the principal Varanasi waterfront. The river-bottom is visible from boat to bank for the entire day. The river that has carried Himalayan silt for two hundred thousand years runs without suspension for one day. Pundits panic. Tourists film. Scientists scramble. Mechanism inferred (through the barasingha-bond): the Void Maw has read the Ganga and erased her silt-memory for one day. The carbonisation-of-absence pattern that began at Maharashtra’s burnt squirrel-nest extends to UP’s river-silt — the Maw can take history out of a substance and the substance becomes visible-without-memory. The Karnataka rule holds. Gangaputra orders a small shrine built at Assi Ghat by the boatmen-guild — stone and packed earth, a single barasingha-hoof print carved on the riverside face. The water returns to muddy at sunset. The shrine remains. Significance (for the catalogue): UP’s Maw sign is the first landscape-scale sign — a river, not a tree, not a person. The chapter’s Maw signs are escalating in size: people (Karnataka, Goa) → place (Maharashtra) → landscape (UP). The next sign will be larger still.
