═══ FIRST HALF ═══ OPENING — THE RICE-PAPER ENVELOPE Kanyakumari. The first Monday of February. Four-twelve a.m. The southernmost residence of the Sangam-tradition’s oldest active scholar — the Acharya Bharati’s small palm-thatched house at the Vivekananda Rock Memorial’s western shore. The Acharya is eighty-three. He is Sage Agastya’s eleventh-direct-apprentice; the kingdom-council’s standing Sangam-tradition senior-mentor; the chair of the Madurai Sangam Hall’s standing-poetry sub-committee for nineteen years; Kurinjiselvi’s personal mentor since her seventeenth year. He has been awake since three-forty-five. He has just finished his pre-dawn cup of black tea (the second steeping; the first is too strong this morning). He is wearing the standard Sangam-elder cotton, off-white, with the kingdom-council’s senior-mentor blue-bordered shawl across his shoulders. A rice-paper envelope has been pushed under his front door between four-oh-three and four-eleven a.m. He sees it at four-twelve when he stands to take the empty tea-cup to the kitchen. The envelope is approximately fifteen centimetres by ten, sealed with the standard Sangam Hall authorisation-clerk office’s wax-seal in green wax (the standard rota-removal communication-protocol). The seal’s impression is the authorisation-clerk Palinisi’s personal cipher. He picks up the envelope. He sits at the kitchen-table. He opens the seal with the standard Sangam-elder’s thumbnail-break. He reads the rice-paper sheet inside. The sheet is single-spaced in formal Sangam-administrative Tamil. He reads it three times across the next eleven minutes. The sheet reads, in plain Tamil: “Respected Acharya. Following the authorisation-clerk office’s standing protocol for the periodic refreshment of the Sangam Hall standing-poetry sub-committee’s chair, the office has determined that the Acharya’s name shall be removed from the council-rota effective the next Sangam session on Thursday. The removal is administered under the standing pre-1972 charter’s thirty-second clause’s rotation-and-refreshment provision. The replacement-chair has not yet been confirmed; the office will communicate the replacement at the appropriate time. The Acharya’s status as standing senior-mentor of the kingdom-council’s Sangam-tradition remains unchanged. Authorisation-clerk Palinisi. Madurai Sangam Hall. February 1st.” The Acharya finishes the third reading at four-twenty-three. He folds the rice-paper sheet along its standard quarter-fold. He places the folded sheet inside the kitchen-table’s standing-correspondence drawer. He stands. He walks to the small writing-desk against the kitchen’s eastern wall. He sits at the desk. He picks up the standing-correspondence parakeet-courier’s pre-folded leaf-letter pack. He writes a single leaf-letter in his standard Sangam-elder’s Tamil-Brahmi calligraphy. The letter takes him forty-three minutes. He finishes at five-oh-six a.m. The letter is addressed to Kurinjiselvi at her Nilgiri Mountains residence. The leaf-letter is folded according to the standard parakeet-courier-fold and sealed with his personal cipher. He walks to the front door at five-eleven a.m. He calls the courier-parakeet at the eastern lattice. The parakeet (his standing courier, a fourteen-year-old female rose-ringed parakeet named Pavalam) arrives within ninety seconds. He places the leaf-letter in her sleeve-pouch. He releases her westward at five-fourteen a.m. She flies at the standard rose-ringed-parakeet courier-pace (approximately forty kilometres per hour). She will be at the Nilgiri residence by sundown. The Acharya returns to the kitchen. He boils a third tea. He sits at the kitchen-table. He waits. He dies at five-forty-eight a.m. on the morning of Thursday, the fourth of February — three days after the rota-removal letter. Cause of death recorded on the kingdom’s death-certificate: ‘natural causes; cardiac arrhythmia consistent with the patient’s age and medical history.’ The Kanyakumari District Medical Officer’s on-site assessment notes that the Acharya’s pre-Thursday-tea cup was still on the kitchen-table and approximately one-third full when the body was found. The tea was the second steeping. The second is honest. THE LEAF-LETTER REACHES THE NILGIRIS Kurinjiselvi is at the Nilgiri summit when Pavalam reaches her residence at five-forty-three p.m. on the chapter’s opening Monday. She is sitting cross-legged on a flat granite outcrop at two thousand four hundred metres, watching the Kurinji-flower fields. The flowers have not bloomed. The bloom-year is twelve months away. The fields are the standard year-eleven grey-green configuration. The fourteen Nilgiri tahrs of the standing-mountain herd are with her. They have been with her every dawn-and-dusk vigil for the past nine years; she has fed them daily by hand; they know her by scent and breath. She descends the western trail at six p.m. when the residence-keeper Iyer-Mama (her standing administrative aide; a sixty-eight-year-old former Sangam-poet who took the residence-keeper seat after his retirement from the assembly in 2019) calls her back from the summit. Pavalam is at the residence’s eastern lattice. The leaf-letter is in the sleeve-pouch. Kurinjiselvi reads the letter at the residence’s evening-tea room. Iyer-Mama is across the table from her. The letter reads: “Daughter. The authorisation-clerk has removed me from the council-rota effective Thursday. The removal is the ninety-third in eighteen months. The pattern is no longer compatible with the standing pre-1972 charter’s thirty-second clause’s honest interpretation. The pattern is procedural-control under a different name. I will not contest the removal directly — the contest’s outcome would be procedural and slow and would obscure the pattern. I will instead permit the removal’s public-record-entry. The entry will be the kingdom-council’s standing evidence of the pattern’s scope. The next sequence will not be mine to direct. I am eighty-three. The next forty-eight hours will likely be my last. I write to you because the next sequence will be yours. The Sangam-tradition cannot be repaired by procedural reform alone; the procedure has been the tradition for two millennia and the procedure has now been weaponised. The repair must be a public-speech that breaks the procedure’s authorisation-rule. The repair must be unauthorised. The Kurinji-flower will not bloom for another year. Your power is at the standard year-eleven attenuated state. The unauthorised speech must still take place. The eleven syllables of a Thirukkural couplet’s opening will be enough. The mark will form in the chamber. The kingdom-council will be in attendance. The amendment will follow within twelve days. The child Avvaiyar-the-Younger is twelve. She has been at the Sangam Hall every council session for the past five years under her grandmother’s mentor-seat. She has memorised every silenced voice. She is the kingdom’s standing living-archive without yet knowing it. Find her after the unauthorised speech. The post-amendment open-assembly evening will be hers to administer. The poet-warrior Tholkappiyan-the-Younger is twenty-seven. He has been training at the Sangam Hall’s standing poet-warrior gymnasium since age fourteen. He is the kingdom-council’s standing succession-candidate for the Sangam-tradition’s visible seat. Introduce him at the post-amendment open-assembly evening. The Sanctuary in Chapter Three will receive him as Tamil Nadu’s formal representative. You will step back to the elder seat by the autumn. The Madurai Manimekalai manuscript will record what has happened. The recording will not be by anyone’s hand. The recording is the kingdom’s evidence that the kingdom remembers. Even the sharp sword bends before the sharper thought. The sharper thought has been the procedure for two thousand years. The thought is now the sword. The bending must be ours. Acharya Bharati. Eleventh-direct-apprentice of Sage Agastya. Kanyakumari. February 1st.” Kurinjiselvi reads the letter twice. She does not weep. She has not wept since she was seventeen when her teacher of the Thirukkural-recitation tradition died at the same Kanyakumari residence. She does not weep now. She says, in plain Tamil, in the Thirukkural-couplet register she uses for formal statements: “Iyer-Mama. The Acharya will be dead by Thursday. The kingdom-council will not contest the cause of death. The pattern will continue under Palinisi’s authorisation-clerk office until the assembly itself rises. The assembly will not rise without a public-speech. The speech must be mine. I will walk down from the mountains on the morning of the Thursday. I will be at the Madurai Sangam Hall by the Friday morning council session. The unauthorised speech will be at the Friday session if Palinisi’s authorisation-clerk office has not approved my rota-entry by then. The authorisation-clerk office will not approve. The speech will be unauthorised.” Iyer-Mama nods. He says: “The Kurinji-staff. The tahrs. The two-week walk to Madurai. The crescent-blade must be sharpened. The Thirukkural-couplet must be memorised in its full version even though only the eleven syllables will be spoken. The memorisation is the kingdom-council’s standing pre-speech-preparation protocol’s required completeness.” She says: “I will memorise the full couplet. The full couplet is the Acharya’s own composition from 1989. The full version is one hundred and seven syllables across the standard Thirukkural seven-and-four-meter. The eleven syllables that will be spoken are the opening line’s first phrase. The full version is the unsaid kingdom-council’s permanent record. The eleven syllables are the speech.” She stands. She walks to the residence’s western training-courtyard. She begins the standard Sangam-warrior pre-speech weapons-preparation sequence. The crescent-bladed Kurinji-staff is at her standing-stand against the western wall. The blade is sharpened across the next two hours. The tahrs watch from the southern fence. She begins the walk down from the Nilgiris the next morning at four a.m. on Tuesday the second of February. The walk to Madurai is approximately fourteen days at the standard mountain-warrior’s pace. She will be at the Madurai Meenakshi temple-city by the morning of the Friday the fifteenth of February. She will be at the Sangam Hall by ten a.m. Friday morning. THE NINETY-THREE SILENCINGS The Madurai Sangam Hall’s authorisation-clerk office’s rota-record across the eighteen months preceding the chapter shows a hundred and forty-seven silenced poets. The standing-record is publicly accessible at the kingdom’s Sangam-archives wing on the Hall’s northern side. The record is preserved at the kingdom-council’s standing-archive standard format. Each silenced-poet entry has: the poet’s name (in Tamil and English transliteration); the poet’s registered Sangam-poet-roll identifier; the rota-removal date; the rota-removal-justification (the standing thirty-second clause’s rotation-and-refreshment provision in 89 cases; the clarification-query-pending status in 47 cases; the procedure-incomplete status in 11 cases); the kingdom-council’s standing-administrative review-status. The hundred and forty-seven silenced-poet entries are not classified by topic. The kingdom-council’s standing-administrative review-status reads ‘closed-no-action’ on 142 entries and ‘pending-clarification’ on 5 entries. Avvaiyar-the-Younger’s grandmother — a sixty-eight-year-old Sangam-tradition standing-mentor named Avvaiyar-Periya (the elder Avvaiyar; the child Avvaiyar is named after her in the standard Sangam-tradition rename-of-the-poet-child protocol) — has been attending every Sangam council session for fifty-seven consecutive years. She has been at every session where a silencing occurred. She has watched the procedure absorb a hundred and forty-seven voices without ever objecting publicly — the public-objection-protocol’s 24-hour-clarification-query window has been weaponised against her three times already; her objections have all been absorbed into the same procedure-incomplete status as the silenced poets’ rota-entries. She has, however, brought her granddaughter to every session since the child was seven. The child has been at the Sangam Hall’s standing-poet-children’s gallery for five years. She has memorised every silenced poem. The child Avvaiyar-the-Younger is twelve. She is small for her age — approximately one metre forty in height — with shoulder-length black hair and the standard Tamil-Brahmin caste-mark at her forehead. She wears the standard Sangam-poet-child’s plain off-white cotton with the assembly’s blue-bordered shawl. She speaks in the Tamil-Brahmi-classical register she has learned from her grandmother. She has, in the last five years, memorised approximately twenty-three thousand Sangam-tradition poems — the standard-curriculum five thousand classical Sangam works, plus approximately eighteen thousand additional poems from the eighteen-month silencings (one hundred and forty-seven silenced poems, plus their authors’ entire registered Sangam-poet-roll backlog). She has not recited any of the silenced poems publicly. The Sangam-poet-children’s gallery does not have speaking-rights at the council; the children are admitted as observers under their mentors’ standing seats. She has, however, recited them privately to her grandmother every evening at their Madurai Old City residence. The grandmother has confirmed each recitation against her own memory of the originals across the five-year-long evening-recitation discipline. The confirmation-rate is one hundred per cent. The grandmother knows what the child is. She has not told the child what the child is. She has been waiting for the right council-session. THE FIFTEENTH OF FEBRUARY MORNING Friday the fifteenth of February. Ten-oh-three a.m. The Madurai Meenakshi temple-city’s eastern gate. Kurinjiselvi arrives on foot at the city’s eastern border. She has walked the fourteen-day mountain-trail at the standard mountain-warrior’s pace. She is in plain Nilgiri-cotton, dark grey, with the kingdom-council’s standing-warrior shoulder-cloth. She carries the crescent-bladed Kurinji-staff at her right side. The blade is freshly sharpened (she has sharpened it again at the trail’s last waypost the previous evening; the blade’s edge holds the standard mountain-warrior killing-edge configuration). She walks through the Meenakshi temple’s northern outer-courtyard at ten-twenty-three. The temple’s standing morning-pilgrimage congregation parts for her at her ten-metre approach. The pilgrims have not seen the kingdom’s state-hero at the temple-city in three years; her presence is the kingdom-council’s standing-public-signal that a chapter-level event is at the temple-city’s threshold. She is at the Sangam Hall’s northern entrance at ten-forty-seven. The Sangam Hall is the kingdom’s standing-assembly building — a large open-air pavilion-style hall approximately forty metres east-west by sixty metres north-south, with raked seating around three sides and a central speaker’s-mark stone at the western edge. The hall has been the Sangam-tradition’s standing-assembly site since 1872 (the previous Sangam Hall at the Madurai Old City’s southern district was destroyed by the 1872 Madurai-floods; the current hall was built across 1873-1879 on the temple-city’s northern side under the kingdom-council’s standing-charter authority). The Friday morning council session begins at eleven a.m. by the standing-protocol. The session is the standing-poetry sub-committee’s weekly review-session; the agenda is set by the authorisation-clerk office on the preceding Wednesday afternoon; the session’s standing-speaker-rota is administered by the authorisation-clerk Palinisi from her standing-desk at the eastern speaker’s-mark stone’s southern side. Palinisi is at her desk at ten-fifty-three a.m. She is fifty-three. She is in the authorisation-clerk office’s standing dark-blue Sangam-administrative cotton with the office’s small green-bordered shoulder-cloth (the standard kingdom-council authorisation-clerk insignia). She has the day’s rota-page open at her desk. The rota-page has approximately forty registered-speakers entries; thirty-one have the authorisation-clerk’s standard green-stamp; nine have the clarification-query-pending yellow-stamp; one has the procedure-incomplete red-stamp. The rota’s green-stamped speakers will be called in the standing-rota order across the session’s three-hour duration. The yellow-stamped will not be called this week. The red-stamped will not be called. Kurinjiselvi’s name is not on the rota-page. She has not submitted a registered-speaker entry for the session. The authorisation-clerk office’s standing-protocol requires a registered-speaker entry to be submitted via the standing-pre-clearance window’s 48-hour-prior submission-protocol; Kurinjiselvi has been on the mountain-trail for the prior fourteen days; she has not had access to the submission-protocol. She walks into the Sangam Hall’s central seating-area at ten-fifty-seven. The standing-poet-children’s gallery is on the hall’s southern raked-seating section; the gallery has approximately forty children present this morning; Avvaiyar-the-Younger is in the gallery’s third row from the front, beside her grandmother Avvaiyar-Periya at the senior-mentor’s standing-seat’s adjoining position. The grandmother sees Kurinjiselvi at the central seating-area’s eastern entrance. She places her right hand on her granddaughter’s left forearm. The contact is the grandmother’s standing-signal that this council session will be the one she has been waiting for. The child looks up at her grandmother. The grandmother nods. The child understands. Kurinjiselvi walks to the central speaker’s-mark stone at the western edge. She stops at three metres from the stone — the standard kingdom-council’s pre-speaker’s-mark waiting-position. She stands. She does not approach the stone. She does not speak. She is the kingdom’s state-hero; her presence at the pre-speaker’s-mark waiting-position is itself a standing-procedural-signal that she intends to speak. Palinisi at the eastern speaker’s-mark stone’s southern side looks up at Kurinjiselvi’s entry. She picks up the rota-page. She picks up the kingdom-council’s standing pre-clearance-window submission-protocol’s reference-volume. She picks up the standing-clarification-query-pending procedural-handbook. She prepares the procedural-response. The procedural-response will be the standing-handbook’s 47-page section on uninvited-speaker-at-the-pre-speaker’s-mark waiting-position’s administrative-handling. The handbook’s standard-response is: the uninvited-speaker is administratively-acknowledged but procedurally-deferred to the next session’s pre-clearance window; the speaker is invited to leave the central seating-area and to submit a registered-speaker entry through the standard 48-hour-prior submission-protocol; the kingdom-council’s standing pre-clearance review-window is restarted at the next Wednesday afternoon’s authorisation-clerk-office’s session. Palinisi begins the procedural-response statement at ten-fifty-nine. The statement is read aloud from the handbook’s standard-response template. The statement takes approximately one minute and forty-three seconds. She reads it in the standing Sangam-administrative Tamil register that the kingdom-council’s charter requires for procedural-responses. The session-start time is eleven a.m. The statement’s completion is at eleven-oh-one-forty-three. The session has therefore formally begun before the statement’s completion. The kingdom-council’s standing procedural-handbook’s 47-page section on uninvited-speaker-handling specifies that the procedural-response must be completed before the session’s formal start to be considered procedurally valid. Palinisi’s statement crossed the session-start threshold by approximately one minute and forty-three seconds. The statement is procedurally invalid. THE ELEVEN SYLLABLES Kurinjiselvi waits for the procedural-response statement to complete. She does not interrupt. She does not speak. She stands at the pre-speaker’s-mark waiting-position. When the statement completes at eleven-oh-one-forty-three a.m., Kurinjiselvi walks the three metres from the waiting-position to the central speaker’s-mark stone. She steps onto the stone. The stone is approximately one-and-a-half metres in diameter, slightly raised from the hall floor by twenty centimetres, polished granite. The stone has been the Sangam-tradition’s standing-speaker’s-mark stone since 1873. She plants the Kurinji-staff at her right side at the stone’s standing pre-recitation position. The crescent-blade points upward. The blade’s sharpened edge catches the morning’s eastern light through the hall’s open-air eastern roof-opening. The hall is silent. Palinisi at her desk has frozen at the procedural-response statement’s completion; she has not yet begun the next procedural-response (the standing-handbook’s standard-secondary-response for an uninvited-speaker who has approached the speaker’s-mark stone after the primary procedural-response’s completion). The kingdom-council’s standing-poetry sub-committee’s chair (the standing-acting-chair after the Acharya Bharati’s death; a sixty-one-year-old Sangam-tradition senior-administrator named Iyer-Periya, no relation to Iyer-Mama at the Nilgiris) is at the sub-committee’s standing-seat on the eastern raked-seating’s front row. The chair is in the standard Sangam-administrative cotton with the sub-committee chair’s gold-bordered shoulder-cloth. He places his palms together at the chest in the standing-acknowledgement gesture. He does not speak. The gesture is the kingdom-council’s standing-chair’s recognition of an uninvited-speaker who has approached the speaker’s-mark stone outside the rota-page’s authorisation. The gesture is procedurally ambiguous: it is the chair’s acknowledgement that the speaker has approached the stone but it is not a grant of speaking-rights. The kingdom-council’s charter does not provide a clear interpretation of the gesture; the gesture has not been performed in the Sangam Hall in three centuries. Kurinjiselvi speaks. She speaks in plain Tamil. She speaks in the standard Thirukkural-couplet-opening register. She speaks the first phrase of the Acharya Bharati’s 1989 composition. The phrase is eleven syllables. She speaks: “Vaay-pakka adai-ppū varum-mun moli-y&aacr; nuŗ-kindra varai.” The phrase translates: “Before the mouth’s entry is sealed, the un-spoken word is the kingdom’s open door.” She speaks the eleven syllables across approximately five-point-three seconds at the standard Thirukkural-couplet recitation-pace. At the eleventh syllable’s completion — at eleven-oh-two-eighteen a.m. — the kingdom-council’s Stage III Nilgiri-tahr-foreleg-first-stroke appears across her left side. The mark forms as a single continuous ink-line across the half-body tahr-tattoo’s left foreleg, completing the previously-lighter foreleg-ink to the standard kingdom-council Stage III completion-pattern. The mark is visible to every member of the assembled council — the standing-poetry sub-committee’s chair, the senior-mentor seats, the authorised-speakers, the gallery’s observers, the poet-children. The mark forms in approximately eleven seconds. Palinisi at her desk has, during the same eleven seconds, begun the standing-handbook’s standard-secondary-response. The response is procedurally appropriate. The response is also, in the kingdom-council’s standing-charter’s honest reading, no longer enforceable; the kingdom-council’s standing Stage III bond-mark’s formation in the council chamber during an uninvited-speaker’s speech is the kingdom’s standing-charter’s highest-procedural-authority recognition-event. The procedural-handbook does not have a section on Stage III bond-mark formation during procedural-response. Palinisi stops reading the standard-secondary-response at the third sentence. The standing-poetry sub-committee’s chair Iyer-Periya stands at the sub-committee’s standing-seat. He bows the standard kingdom-council’s state-hero’s recognition-bow toward the central speaker’s-mark stone. He says, in plain Tamil: “State-hero Kurinjiselvi. The kingdom-council’s standing-charter’s highest-procedural-authority recognition-event has occurred. The session is the kingdom-council’s emergency-session under the standing-charter’s seventh clause. The emergency-session’s standing-agenda is the kingdom-council’s amendment to the Sangam-tradition’s pre-1972 charter. Please continue.” Kurinjiselvi continues. She speaks the second phrase of the Acharya Bharati’s 1989 composition. The phrase is nine syllables. She speaks the third phrase, eleven syllables. She speaks the full hundred-and-seven-syllable Thirukkural-couplet across the next eleven minutes. The kingdom-council’s emergency-session listens. The hundred-and-forty-seven silenced poets’ names are not in the composition; the composition is the Acharya’s framework-poem on the principle of un-silenceable speech. The hundred-and-forty-seven names will come later, in Avvaiyar-the-Younger’s post-amendment recitation. The Acharya’s framework-poem is the kingdom-council’s charter-amendment’s principle-foundation. She completes the recitation at eleven-thirteen-forty-seven a.m. She steps back from the speaker’s-mark stone. She bows the standard kingdom-council’s post-speech acknowledgement-bow to the standing-poetry sub-committee’s chair. He returns the bow at the same depth. The kingdom-council’s emergency-session begins the formal-amendment-drafting at eleven-fifteen. ═══ INTERVAL ═══ THE TWELVE-DAY AMENDMENT The kingdom-council’s emergency-session sits across the next twelve days. The session meets in continuous-hours mode (the kingdom-council’s standing-procedure for charter-amendments requires continuous-hours sessions of approximately fourteen hours per day with two-hour-and-one-hour meal-and-prayer breaks). The amendment’s drafting is led by Iyer-Periya (the standing-poetry sub-committee’s chair) with the kingdom-council’s senior-mentor seats (twelve mentors including Avvaiyar-Periya the elder, the recently-deceased Acharya Bharati’s replacement-chair, the Tamil Sahitya Akademi’s standing-fellows, and the kingdom-council’s standing-Sangam-archives senior-archivist). Kurinjiselvi attends the daily sessions but does not speak after the first day’s formal opening-statement. She has spoken; the speech is complete; the amendment’s drafting is the kingdom-council’s standing senior-mentor work. She sits at the kingdom-council’s state-hero’s standing-observer seat on the eastern raked-seating’s front-row third-position. The amendment’s draft is presented at the end of day eleven. The amendment is twenty-three pages. The amendment’s seven core provisions are: First. The authorisation-clerk office is dissolved as a procedural-control institution. The office’s standing pre-clearance window’s 48-hour-prior submission-protocol is replaced by an open-registration protocol; any Sangam-poet-roll-registered poet may speak at any council session by signing the standing open-registration roll at the session’s opening. Second. The kingdom-council’s standing public-objection-protocol’s 24-hour-clarification-query window is amended to a one-hour-clarification-query window with mandatory resolution within twenty-four hours; the kingdom-council’s standing-procedural-review cell is the standing-arbiter. Third. The kingdom-council’s standing-rota-removal protocol is amended to require: (a) seventy-five per cent senior-mentor seat-vote; (b) the affected poet’s response-right within seven days; (c) the kingdom-council’s standing-procedural-review cell’s independent assessment within fourteen days. Fourth. The kingdom-council’s standing-poet-children’s gallery is granted observer-and-recitation rights at the council’s standing open-assembly evenings. The recitation is administered through the children’s mentor-seats but the child-poets’ voices are themselves the recitation. Fifth. The Sangam-tradition’s standing living-archive seat is established. The seat is non-rotational and is held by the kingdom-council’s standing-recognised living-archive recogniser (the seat-holder is determined by the senior-mentor seat-vote upon evidence of qualifying memorial-capacity). The seat-holder’s standing-duty is to attend every council session and to be the kingdom-council’s standing-recourse for unrecorded silencings, retrieved voices, and reconstructed-from-memory un-written poems. Sixth. The kingdom-council’s standing post-amendment open-assembly evening will be held at the Sangam Hall on the evening of the twelfth-day. The evening will be open to all registered Sangam-poet-roll members and to the standing-poet-children’s gallery. The standing living-archive seat’s candidate-recognition-recitation will be held at the evening’s opening hour. Seventh. Palinisi’s authorisation-clerk seat is dissolved. Her standing-employment within the kingdom-council is preserved through transfer to the Sangam-archives wing’s junior-archivist seat under standing-mentor supervision. The transfer is not a punishment; the transfer is the kingdom-council’s acknowledgement that her administrative-capacity is real and should be repurposed to the archive’s standing-work. She accepts the transfer at the kingdom-council’s day-twelve administrative-session. The amendment is ratified by the kingdom-council’s emergency-session by 38 to 4 on day twelve at three-thirty-seven p.m. The four dissenting votes are from senior administrative-tradition seats whose objections are procedural (the pre-1972 charter’s thirty-second clause is being reformed rather than abolished; the dissenters had argued for abolition). The four dissenters affirm their loyalty to the new charter at the post-vote affirmation-statement. THE LIBRARY ON THE MORNING OF DAY FIFTEEN The vault-keeper of the Madurai Maraimalai Adigal Tamil-Language Public Library at the Meenakshi temple precinct’s northern side is a fifty-eight-year-old administrator named V. Sundara-Rajan. He has held the vault-keeper seat for sixteen years. He performs the standard quarterly maintenance-check on the kingdom’s great Tamil-language manuscript-collection across four days every three months. The current quarter’s maintenance-check began on day forty-six of the chapter and concluded on day forty-nine.
