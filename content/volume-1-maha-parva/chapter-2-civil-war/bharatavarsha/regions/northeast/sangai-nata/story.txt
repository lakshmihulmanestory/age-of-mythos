Imphal. Five-fifteen a.m. The Sri Sri Govindaji temple courtyard. The valley's morning mist is thick enough to hold its own shape between the two cremation-pagodas at the courtyard's eastern edge. The temple's grey sandstone walls are dark with overnight dew. The pre-monsoon air is still cold enough that breath shows.

Twelve men in white kurtas with yellow shawls are arranged in the Thang-Ta opening formation — a three-by-four grid, each man two arm-lengths from his neighbours, the senior position empty at the front.

Moirangthem walks out of the temple's side-door at five-eighteen. He is thirty-seven. He is wearing the same white-and-yellow as the twelve. He has a Thang — the curved Meitei sword — in a baldric-sheath across his back, and a Ta — the seven-foot Meitei spear — carried at the right shoulder. He takes the senior position at the front of the formation.

The twelve are his troupe. He has trained eleven of them for at least sixteen years. The twelfth — Khelendra, the youngest at twenty-nine — he has trained for six. They are the Govindaji temple's senior dancer-warriors. They are, in formal Manipuri classification, his nupi-pala — his "line-of-spears."

He counts off the opening tempo with a single tongue-click. The formation begins the morning sequence: the eight-step Krishna-approach, the twelve-step ras-leela bow, the sixteen-step Thang-Ta circle. The movement is perfect. He has done this morning's sequence with this exact formation for nine years.

On the temple steps, his eight-year-old son Sangaputra watches. The boy is wearing a small khadi kurta, no shawl — he is too young to wear yellow. He has been watching the morning drill from this step every dawn since he was four. He knows the sequences by heart. He has not yet been allowed to join.

At the back of the courtyard, partly hidden by the mist, an older woman sits on a stone bench. She is sixty-six. Her name is Leishemba. She is the senior elder of the Khwairamband Bazaar — Asia's largest women-run market — and she has been waiting for the morning drill to end so she can speak to Moirangthem before the temple opens to the public.

She is waiting longer than she intended. The drill is forty-seven minutes today. It is forty-seven minutes most days.

When it ends, she stands.

She walks to where Moirangthem is sheathing the Thang. She bows three degrees less than protocol — she is a bazaar-elder, not a temple-elder; the protocols are different.

She says, in Meitei: "Oja. May I speak."

Moirangthem nods. He sets the Ta against the courtyard wall. He gestures her to the stone bench.

She sits. He sits. The twelve troupe-members disperse to the temple kitchen for the morning rice. Sangaputra moves quietly along the courtyard's edge to be within hearing distance.

Leishemba says: "Oja, the young dancers at the bazaar have a real complaint. The classical form has frozen. It cannot speak about the present. There is no choreography for the gas-cylinder shortage. There is no choreography for the four-year-old who drowned in the Imphal flood in May. There is no choreography for the army convoy that passes the bazaar at noon. The classical form is sacred but it does not answer the questions we are living in. The young dancers are answering them — roughly. They are mixing things that should not be mixed. Some of what they make is bad. Some of it is alive. Come listen once. They perform Friday evenings under the canvas awning at the south stalls."

Moirangthem listens. He does not interrupt. He does not promise to come.

He says: "I will think about this."

Leishemba bows. She walks away. Sangaputra is too small to be noticed; he has heard every word.

That Friday evening Moirangthem arrives at the bazaar at six p.m. He is in plain khadi — not the temple white-and-yellow. He sits on a low stool at the back of the awning. The crowd is about seventy people, mostly under thirty.

The performance starts at six-fifteen. The lead dancer is a man named Ibomcha. He is twenty-four. He trained at the Govindaji gurukul from age six to age sixteen; Moirangthem knows him by sight. Ibomcha's opening is a classical Manipuri ras-leela bow — precise, formal — that breaks abruptly mid-sequence into a hip-hop low-glide followed by a Naga war-dance leap-and-stamp. The transitions are rough. The audience is rapt.

The performance runs forty minutes. It includes seventeen distinct dance-traditions. Six of them clash. Two of them sing. One — a section where Ibomcha holds a Thang-Ta sword in the wrong hand and uses it to follow a Mizo street-rhythm beat-pattern — is the closest thing Moirangthem has seen to a new form in nine years of formal practice.

He stays for the whole forty minutes. He does not approach Ibomcha. He walks home alone in the dark.

That night he tells Ka-Memcha: "The bazaar-elder is correct. The form has frozen. I do not yet know what to do about it."

Ka-Memcha says: "Sleep on it."

He does. He sleeps badly.

Two weeks later. A Friday evening. Moirangthem has not returned to the bazaar. He has been thinking. He has been failing to think.

At the Friday performance, a new dancer arrives at the awning. He is older — forty-eight, by Ibomcha's later estimate. He is tall. He carries himself the way a former gurukul student carries himself: trained, posture-corrected, the body remembering forms the mind has tried to leave.

He introduces himself to Ibomcha as "a wanderer who has been listening for a teacher." He does not give a name that first night. He performs one fifteen-minute improvisation that combines Thang-Ta with what he calls "the Lhasa sword-stillness" — a slow, knife-edged form that lengthens every transition into a long held breath. The crowd is hypnotised. Ibomcha is unsettled and impressed.

The next Friday the wanderer returns. He performs again. The Friday after that he gives a name: Tamasa-Nrita. Ibomcha recognises the name — the Govindaji temple's 1998 expulsion-case. Ibomcha does not know what to do. Tamasa-Nrita asks to teach the bazaar dancers. The dancers, who have been struggling to articulate their form, accept gratefully.

Over the next four months Tamasa-Nrita teaches at the bazaar Friday evenings and at a private rehearsal-hall in the old British Cantonment three other evenings a week. His teaching is precise and beautiful. His teaching also includes, by week six, real knives. The first knife is presented as a "Lhasa training-blade" — ceremonial-looking, not obviously sharp. By week eight it is replaced with a true Meitei dao — razor edge, properly weighted, capable of taking a finger.

Ibomcha objects to the dao at the rehearsal-hall in week six. Tamasa-Nrita silences him by promoting another raw-dancer above him — a 22-year-old named Niranjan-Tomba who is fully Tamasa-Nrita's. Ibomcha continues attending but stops trusting.

Month four. A Tuesday morning. The temple courtyard.

A bicycle-messenger delivers a sealed envelope at the temple gate. The envelope is addressed to Moirangthem and carries the bazaar-stall seal of the south-end awning. The letter inside is in formal Meitei, calligraphed.

Oja Moirangthem,

The young dancers of the Khwairamband Bazaar respectfully request a joint performance with the senior Thang-Ta troupe of the Sri Sri Govindaji temple. The performance to be held at the Loktak central phumdi at dawn on the next Sangai-Mela full moon — eleven days from this letter. The performance to be a meeting of forms: classical and raw, formal and improvised, master and rebellion. The Sangai deer of the phumdi to be the only judges.

The invitation is sincere. The form requires the master's presence to be honoured. The rebellion requires the master's presence to be acknowledged.

Respectfully — the Khwairamband Bazaar South-Awning Dance-Collective. Signed: Tamasa-Nrita, lead teacher.

Moirangthem reads the letter twice. He reads the signature line a third time. He has not seen Tamasa-Nrita's handwriting in twenty-six years.

He thinks for an hour. He calls his twelve troupe-members to the temple kitchen at midday. He reads them the letter. He says: "We will go. Twelve of us. Classical formation. Classical weapons. Classical opening. The rebellion has named us as the form. We will answer as the form."

Khelendra — the youngest at twenty-nine, the one who has been to the bazaar performances three times himself — raises his hand. He says: "Oja. May I say one thing." Moirangthem nods. Khelendra says: "There is a man named Tamasa-Nrita teaching at the bazaar. I do not trust him. Could we go in classical formation but bring — ourselves — ready for something other than dance."

Moirangthem considers. He says: "We are the form. The form does not go armed-for-violence to a dance. If we bring readiness-for-violence we have already lost the argument. We go as the form."

The twelve agree. They begin the eleven-day preparation.

Sangaputra hears it all. He is eight.

The night before the joint performance. The boy sneaks out of the house. He is wearing his small khadi kurta and a thin blue shawl Ka-Memcha gave him last winter. He has packed a small cloth bundle with three flatbreads, a bottle of water, and the toy Thang his father carved for him at age five.

He walks. The walk from Imphal to the Loktak shore is fourteen kilometres. He does it in four hours, mostly along the highway shoulder. He arrives at the Loktak shore at midnight. The boats are tied at the southern landing.

He pays a sleeping boatman five rupees from his pocket-money to row him to the central phumdi. The boatman, half-awake, does not realise he is rowing an eight-year-old alone into the lake at midnight. By the time the boatman registers, they are at the phumdi and the boy is climbing out.

The boy walks onto the phumdi. The phumdi is alive — it is a floating biomass-island, vegetation and soil and water-rooted reeds, ten kilometres across in places, the only floating-island ecosystem of its scale on earth. He walks until he finds a clump of phumdi-reeds tall enough to hide behind. He eats one of the flatbreads. He sleeps.

He wakes at first light. He is in position. He waits.

The two troupes arrive at five-thirty a.m. Moirangthem's twelve in the classical white-and-yellow, Thang and Ta crossed at the back, formal opening positions. Tamasa-Nrita's thirty in mixed costume — bazaar streetwear with Naga-style red threads at the wrists, some with khadi kurtas, some with Bangalore-diaspora hip-hop hoodies pulled back.

The two troupes form their respective opening positions. Moirangthem and Tamasa-Nrita exchange the formal bow. The Sangai deer of the central phumdi — about forty animals, watching from a clearing two hundred metres east of the dance-circle — do not move.

The performance begins at five-forty-five.

The first three minutes are honest. Moirangthem's troupe performs the classical ras-leela opening. Tamasa-Nrita's thirty perform an improvised counter-sequence that genuinely engages the classical form — the choreography is rough but it is choreography. Ibomcha, on the raw-dance side, is performing with full attention — he is, at this point, still believing the performance will be a performance.

At minute four, three of Tamasa-Nrita's thirty — including Niranjan-Tomba in the lead position — produce daos from concealed waist-sheaths. The daos are not ceremonial. They are not Lhasa-trained-stillness blades. They are Meitei working-knives, properly sharpened, six inches long. The three move from raw-dance choreography into the classical-troupe formation as if continuing the dance.

Tonjam — the eldest of Moirangthem's troupe, fifty-four, lead choreographer for the annual Lai Haraoba — is the first to die. He is in the front-right corner of the three-by-four formation. He is in the eight-step Krishna-approach's third-step pose — right knee forward, both arms extended — when Niranjan-Tomba reaches him. The dao enters at the soft point below his sternum. Tonjam goes down without seeing the blade.

Moirangthem sees the second blade. It is in another raw-dancer's hand and it is two seconds from Ratna-priya, his female lead, who is in the centre of the formation. He is in the eighth step of the Krishna-approach himself — left foot forward, Thang at the shoulder. He breaks his sequence. He draws the Thang. He moves to intercept.

He is too late. Ratna-priya falls. The third blade reaches Manaobi.

In the next nine minutes, six more of his troupe die. Khelendra — the one who warned about Tamasa-Nrita, the youngest, the only one who had registered the possibility of violence — gets the Thang out in time, defends himself, and pulls Inaocha and Tomba (the two oldest survivors) into a fighting retreat toward the phumdi's northern edge. Moirangthem covers the retreat. His Ta-spear keeps three raw-dancers at bay long enough for the three survivors to reach the phumdi-edge and the cold-water of the lake.

Eleven minutes total. Nine dead. Three survivors plus Moirangthem.

He retreats last. He goes into the lake-water with the Thang still drawn. The water is sixteen degrees Celsius. He swims south for two hundred metres to a smaller phumdi where Khelendra has signalled a hiding-point. He arrives. He vomits lake-water and grief. Inaocha holds him by the shoulders so he does not slide into the water again.

Tamasa-Nrita's thirty do not pursue beyond the phumdi-edge. They lose the four survivors in the morning mist. By eight a.m. the raw-dance group has retreated back to Imphal in two hired tempo-trucks.

Sangaputra is twenty metres from the dance-circle when the massacre starts. He is behind a clump of phumdi-reeds two metres tall. The reeds save his life.

He watches all eleven minutes. He does not move. He does not cry. He is eight, but he is the son of a Thang-Ta master and the grandson on his mother's side of a Tangkhul-Naga warrior-clan. He has been trained, without quite knowing it, to be still under threat. The training holds.

When the raw-dancers retreat back across the lake, he stays. He does not climb out of the reeds for two hours. When he does, the sun is high. The bodies of the nine are in the dance-circle. The Sangai deer have not approached the bodies. The deer are still watching from the eastern clearing, exactly where they were before the massacre, unmoved.

Sangaputra walks to the bodies. He recognises Tonjam, Ratna-priya, Manaobi, Ngambom, Bishorjit, Inakhumb, Sapam, Lalit, Bobby — all of them. He has been at their houses for festivals. He has eaten their wives' food. He sits next to Tonjam for the longest. Tonjam taught him the first eight-step Krishna-approach when he was four.

After an hour the boy stands up. He walks east toward the deer-clearing. The Sangai do not flee.

He sits at the clearing's edge. The deer watch him. He watches them. He does not approach them. They do not approach him.

This is the boy's first day on the phumdi. He will be there ten more.

Moirangthem returns to Imphal by mid-afternoon. He, Khelendra, Inaocha, and Tomba are met at the boat-landing by a state-government police-detail Ka-Memcha has called — she received word at noon by Sarus-pigeon and acted within the hour. The four are escorted to the temple. The temple is sealed.

Moirangthem will not see anyone for eleven days. He sits in the temple's small east room. He does not eat for the first three days. He does not speak for the first six. Ka-Memcha sits with him for hours each evening without speaking.

The state government declares a three-day mourning period at the news. The Imphal cremation ground holds the joint ceremony for the nine on day two. Moirangthem does not attend the cremation. Ka-Memcha attends in his place. She carries his Thang for him — the formal mark of his mourning that excuses absence from the cremation itself.

On day four, the police bring news. Sangaputra is missing. He has been missing since the night before the massacre.

Ka-Memcha tells Moirangthem at five p.m. on day four. He looks up for the first time in four days. He says: "He went to see."

She says: "He is alive. The phumdi-boatmen have reported sightings — a small boy at the central phumdi with the deer. He is not approaching anyone. He is not in danger. The state department of biodiversity has arranged a quiet watch. He has food — he is eating fruit from the phumdi-vegetation that the deer also eat."

He says: "Leave him."

She says: "How long."

"Until he comes back. Or until he asks to be brought back. He is on the phumdi for a reason."

She does not argue.

On day eleven a search-team brings him back at sunset. He has been on the phumdi for eleven days. He has eaten phumdi-fruit and slept in the Sangai-deer scrape-bed every night since day five. He is not malnourished. He is sun-darkened. His khadi kurta is in three pieces tied at the waist with phumdi-reeds. He carries the toy Thang.

He walks into the small east room where his father is sitting. He stops two paces inside the door.

He says, in a clear voice: "Pita. The deer dance and stop. The pause is the dance too."

Moirangthem looks at him.

The boy explains. He explains for forty minutes. He has watched the Sangai deer for eleven days. He has noticed what no human has noticed in formal Sangai-dance scholarship for sixty years. The deer move in classical-precision sequences — identifiable opening-postures, identifiable transitions, identifiable closings — and then they stop. The stop is not a rest. The stop is part of the sequence. The stop has duration. The stop is held with the same body-discipline as the moving-positions. Then the next sequence begins.

The pause is the dance.

Moirangthem listens. He does not interrupt. When the boy finishes, the father stands up for the first time in eleven days. He picks up his Thang. He goes outside.

The next six days. Moirangthem and Sangaputra in the temple courtyard from dawn to dusk. The father trying. The son watching and saying, occasionally: "No. Not that. Hold it longer. The deer hold it longer."

The form they invent has three parts. (1) A classical sequence performed at full-form precision. (2) An absolute stop — total stillness, the body held in the closing-position with the eyes fixed at a single point. (3) A break — the closing-position releases into the next opening-position with the rage of an animal launching from cover. The rage is what was gathering in the pause.

The break is also, in combat, the strike. The pause is when the breath gathers, the muscles load, the attention narrows. The strike does not telegraph because the body has been still for the pause-duration. The opponent loses the rhythm-reading by which they were tracking the dancer's next move. The strike is over before the opponent has registered the shift from pause to motion.

Ibomcha — who has come to the temple on day three after the massacre and confessed everything — joins the rehearsals on day fourteen. He has trained in the form before he became a raw-dancer; he has also trained in the rebellion-mixed-forms; he is the only living dancer who knows both vocabularies. He learns the new pause-form in two days.

Moirangthem names the new school Moirang-Tandava. Moirang because the central pause carries the same body-discipline as the death-stillness Lord Krishna held when he listened to Arjuna's questions in the Bhagavad Gita; Tandava because the break-strike carries the same rage as Shiva's cosmic dance.

Day eighteen. The Sri Sri Govindaji temple courtyard. Mid-afternoon. Open to the public.

The courtyard is full. The state government has cleared its schedule for the announced "new form demonstration"; international cultural press are present (a Japanese kabuki troupe on tour and a German contemporary-dance documentarian filed the press requests within hours of the announcement). The Khwairamband Bazaar elders are in the second row. Leishemba is in the centre seat of that row.

Moirangthem enters in white-and-yellow with the Thang and Ta crossed. Ibomcha enters in mixed bazaar-streetwear with Naga-red wrist-threads. The two stand at opposite ends of the courtyard.

They perform. The performance is twenty-seven minutes long. It includes nine full classical sequences, eight full pauses, and seven full break-strikes. The pauses range from four seconds to twenty-eight seconds. The break-strikes are blade-precise — their Thangs sing against each other in the air with the speed of the rage gathered in the pause.

At minute nineteen Moirangthem feels the Sangai-bond complete on his chest. The Stage III foreleg-mark forms — the deer's right foreleg arching down his right pectoral, the head turned in profile across his sternum, the antlers cresting at his collarbone. The mark's ink does not fade; it does not crack; it does not mirror. It is a clean Stage III earned by the new form.

The performance ends with both dancers in the closing pause, holding for nineteen seconds — the longest pause of the performance — before they release into the formal bow.

The Mrs Hume's Pheasant on the temple roof sings during the central pause. The state-government cultural officer in the front row writes one word in his notebook and circles it twice: new.

The next morning a sealed envelope arrives at the temple gate by bicycle-messenger. The handwriting is Tamasa-Nrita's. The challenge is one paragraph long.

Oja. Loktak central phumdi. Three days. Sunrise. You alone. Me alone with my twelve closest. We will dance once more. The phumdi will judge. — Tamasa-Nrita.

Moirangthem accepts by silence. He sends no reply.

Three days later he travels to the Loktak shore at four a.m. with no troupe. He has Thang and Ta. He has the new bond on his chest. Sangaputra is at the shore with Ibomcha and Ka-Memcha. The boy is forbidden the phumdi this time — explicitly forbidden, with all three of them watching him. He will wait at the shore.

Moirangthem rows himself across. He arrives at the central phumdi at five-fifteen a.m. Tamasa-Nrita is waiting with twelve raw-dancers, all carrying daos. The Sangai deer of the phumdi are in their usual eastern clearing, watching, unmoved.

It is not a duel. It is a dance.

The thirteen raw-dancers approach Moirangthem in a thirteen-position circle. Tamasa-Nrita is at the south point. The twelve others are at twelve compass-points around the dance-circle. They begin a coordinated raw-dance approach-sequence — the same opening their group used at the massacre, the kill-choreography.

Moirangthem stands at the centre. He waits for the first attack. He has decided to teach the new school its first combat-lesson here.

The first attacker is a twenty-two-year-old named Niranjan-Tomba — the same dancer who killed Tonjam. He breaks the circle's coordination at the second beat and strikes from the west. His dao is at chest-height, edge angled for Moirangthem's ribs.

Moirangthem performs the new form. He completes a classical eight-step opening sequence in the four seconds Niranjan-Tomba takes to cross the circle. At step eight he stops. He holds the closing-position for two full seconds — long enough for Niranjan-Tomba to commit to his strike-path. At second three of the pause Moirangthem releases the break-strike. The Thang clears its sheath, traces a perfect Meitei-curve down through Niranjan-Tomba's strike-arc, and continues into Niranjan-Tomba's neck. The dao falls. The body falls a heartbeat later.

The pause-strike has worked.

The other eleven raw-dancers, who have not registered the pause-strike as a technique, attack in a coordinated rush. Moirangthem repeats the form — classical sequence, pause, break-strike — against each in turn. The combat takes eight minutes. Eleven raw-dancers fall. None of the eleven completes a successful strike. The pause-strike is, in this hour, unanswerable.

Tamasa-Nrita is the twelfth. He has been watching from the south point. He has registered the technique. He has been Moirangthem's peer at the gurukul; he knows the classical sequences; he can match them.

He approaches. They dance.

The duel is twenty-three minutes. Tamasa-Nrita matches Moirangthem's pauses with his own — Lhasa-trained sword-stillness against the Sangai-deer pause-form. The two are technically equal. The break-strikes are blocked, parried, redirected. Neither lands.

At minute twenty-two Moirangthem changes the form. He extends his pause from the standard four seconds to nineteen seconds — the same nineteen-second close he held at the temple demonstration. Tamasa-Nrita cannot match the nineteen. He has the Lhasa technique for four seconds, six, twelve. He has never trained past fifteen. At second seventeen of the held pause he twitches — an involuntary muscle-release at the shoulder, the kind of small fail that no opponent should be able to see.

Moirangthem sees it. He releases the break-strike at second eighteen. The Thang clears, traces the Meitei-curve, and arrives at Tamasa-Nrita's sword-arm at the elbow-joint. The arm parts cleanly at the joint. The dao falls. Tamasa-Nrita drops to one knee. The Ta — not yet used in the duel — comes off Moirangthem's shoulder and pins Tamasa-Nrita at the phumdi-edge through the chest-armour without entering the chest itself, just holding him there.

Tamasa-Nrita is alive. He is unable to move. His sword-arm lies on the phumdi between them.

Moirangthem looks at him for a long pause — this one not part of the form.

He says: "You corrupted a real grievance into murder. You will live with that. The third school is what the rebellion deserved. You will not be its teacher. Go."

He withdraws the Ta. He gestures east toward the Burmese border.

Tamasa-Nrita rises, holding his stump. He walks. He does not look back. By nightfall he has crossed into the Chin-state border zone. The Manipur state government will issue a formal notification six weeks later that Tamasa-Nrita is no longer to be considered present in Indian jurisdiction.

That night. Loktak. Eleven-forty-seven p.m. A Pawan Hans tourist-helicopter is on its return route from Moirang town to the Imphal airport — the pilot is a forty-two-year-old man named Captain Tomthing who has flown this route four times a week for eight years.

He is at 2,400 feet over the central phumdi. The lake is dark below. The phumdis appear as floating shapes — ten kilometres across in places, scatter-patterned in a way Captain Tomthing has long since stopped noticing.

He notices tonight. The scatter-pattern is not scattering. The phumdis are arranging.

He banks. He turns on his cockpit-video camera — a standard pilot-safety device. He films for the next eleven minutes.

In the cockpit-video footage, the floating phumdis of Loktak Lake arrange themselves into the recognisable shape of a closing mouth. A four-kilometre arc of biomass forming the lower mandible-curve. A second four-kilometre arc forming the upper lip. Two rows of dense phumdi-clusters arrayed inside the arc that the pilot will later describe as "teeth-like." The arrangement is held for seven minutes. The mouth closes — the two arcs touch at the centre. The teeth disappear. The lake is a closed mouth on the surface of the world.

The arrangement holds for ninety seconds in the closed configuration. Then it begins to drift apart. By thirteen minutes after first observation the phumdis are back in their normal scatter-pattern.

Captain Tomthing's footage is uploaded to the Pawan Hans incident-report server at twelve-forty-five a.m. By two a.m. it has been leaked to a Manipuri news-blogger. By six a.m. it has been viewed eleven million times across three social-media platforms.

Moirangthem watches the video at six-fifteen a.m. when his phone alerts him to the upload. He understands what the video shows. The Maw lives in the gap between forms; the Maw is now able to make its own form on the surface of a lake. This is new. This is escalation.

He sends word by Sarus-pigeon to Kamalavarna at Kamakhya, Dvadashashringa at Bhopal, Gangaputra at Lucknow, Krishnadevaraya-Bhrata at Kerala, U-Khrawkmen at Shillong, and Shivagati at Pratapgad. The message is one sentence:

The Maw has signed itself on Loktak. The escalation has crossed into form-making. The Council must sit before the next sign.

By the end of the week three letters arrive at Moirangthem's house.

The first is from Kamalavarna at Kamakhya, in her own hand:

Cousin. Your father's sister's daughter is your aunt-cousin and your sister's niece by the corrected matrilineal-tree my Chapter Two opened. We are kin three different ways. I am yielding the political throne of the Northeast to you. The cosmic seat stays with me and my sister at Kamakhya. You will hold the visible voice. The Sanctuary convening in Chapter Three will name you Dominant; my hand will be on your shoulder at the naming.

Come to Kamakhya at the next new moon. Bring Sangaputra. We will perform the small acknowledgement-rite for his pause-discovery. The deer have already chosen him — they have not chosen lightly in nine centuries.

The second is from U-Khrawkmen at Shillong:

Cousin-brother. Aunt-mother (Meghanadi) wrote to expect this. The Meghalaya council backs you. The Mixed Council I am building will federate under your political seat into Chapter Three. Ka-Sienlyngdoh will visit Manipur at the new moon when the leopard settles on her — you and Sangaputra should meet her at the temple. Two twelve-year-olds carrying two different bonds will recognise each other faster than any adult can introduce them.

The third is from Dzulevira at Kohima, his sister-in-law's formal letter:

Brother-in-law. I cannot yet ratify the NE political throne to you. My sixteen tribes are in pure-lineage war and the Naga seat is not stable enough to vote on the regional question. My elder sister sends her love and asks you to send Sangaputra to Ukhrul for a fortnight before the new moon — the boy needs his Tangkhul-Naga grandmothers more than he needs you to teach him pause-strikes. Send him. I will hold his side of the kingdom while my own is in disorder. The vote on the NE political throne will come from Nagaland in Chapter Three or not at all.

Moirangthem reads all three. He writes brief replies to each. He sends Sangaputra to Ukhrul the same day with Ka-Memcha and a small retinue. He himself begins the eleven-day fast and preparation that the new-moon Kamakhya visit requires.

Three weeks later. The Govindaji temple courtyard. Mid-afternoon. The new troupe begins its first formal class.

The class has fifteen students. Three are the surviving members of Moirangthem's original twelve (Tomba, Inaocha, Khelendra). Four are converted raw-dancers from the bazaar who attended the public demonstration and asked to learn (Ibomcha and three others). Three are bazaar-elders who had no formal dance-training but who want to learn the pause as a contemplative practice (Leishemba is one). Five are children — the youngest is Sangaputra, returned from Ukhrul; the oldest is twelve.

Moirangthem opens the class with the same tongue-click count-off he has used for nine years in the morning drill. The fifteen begin the eight-step Krishna-approach.

At step eight they stop.

The pause holds for four seconds. Then nine. Then sixteen. Then nineteen. Then twenty-one.

At twenty-three seconds Moirangthem releases the break. The fifteen follow. The Mrs Hume's Pheasant on the temple roof sings.

Across the courtyard, at the temple wall, the Sangai-foreleg mark on Moirangthem's chest pulses once. The kingdom of Sangai-Nata has its first new dance-form in fifty-three years. The Northeast has its future political voice. The Maw has signed itself on a lake.

The chapter ends.

The Pilot's Closing Image

The Govindaji temple courtyard at dusk. Fifteen dancers in a three-by-five formation holding a twenty-three-second pause. A Mrs Hume's Pheasant on the temple roof. A father's Stage III mark pulsing under white-and-yellow cloth. An eight-year-old at the front-left corner of the formation holding the pause better than the four-year-olds and the bazaar-elders both. Across Loktak Lake at the same hour, the phumdis in their normal scatter, but a small new sandstone shrine at the western shore where a helicopter pilot's flight path crossed a four-kilometre mouth-arc. Letters from five cousin-kingdoms in the sleeve-pocket of a man who has not yet decided whether to feel like the Dominant he is about to be named. The form alive. The pause held. The Maw signing itself for the first time. The boy who watched the deer dance and stop. The form is form-with-rage. The dance includes the silence. The silence is the dance.
