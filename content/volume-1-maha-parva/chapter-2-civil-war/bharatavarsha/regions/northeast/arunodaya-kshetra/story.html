The Night-Cultists — A Film in Two Halves
═══ FIRST HALF ═══ OPENING — FIVE-FORTY A.M. AT THE TAWANG RIDGE Tawang. The first Tuesday of September. Five-forty a.m. The eastern ridge above the Tawang Monastery at three thousand four hundred metres altitude. The first eastern light is forming behind the Bhutan-border ranges; the sky is rose at the horizon, deepening to indigo at the zenith. Udayagiri is at the ridge-stone. He is thirty-one. He is in the dawn-walker robe, knee-length, deep amber, with the gayal-hide sash at his waist. He carries the Uday-spear — the dawn-forged spear his line has carried for four hundred and seven years, re-forged each dawn in the first light’s heat. The spear’s shaft is hill-bamboo; the head is a thin double-edged copper-alloy blade; the binding is gayal-leather wrapped in twelve turns. He has been at the ridge-stone every dawn for two thousand seven hundred and forty-eight consecutive days. The first-light arrives at five-forty-three a.m. He turns the spear’s blade-edge to the east. The blade catches the first ray. The metal warms in his hand — the daily re-forging that the saga’s gayal-bond performs at the moment-of-light. He greets the dawn aloud in Apatani-Adi-Galo — the three-tribe ceremonial trade-tongue that has been Arunodaya-Kshetra’s greeting-language for six centuries. He says, into the morning air: “The dawn cannot be stopped. The kingdom is greeted. The day is begun.” The three-tribe trade-tongue is heard, as it has been heard every morning for six centuries, by approximately four hundred dawn-walkers across the kingdom — one ridge-elder per tribal sub-village — performing the same greeting at the same minute at their own local first-light. The greetings overlap across the time-zone-staggered ridges; the kingdom-net of dawn-walkers covers the state in concentric expanding light-rings from east to west across about thirty-eight minutes (the time it takes the first-light to walk from the Roing eastern ridge at the Mishmi Hills to the Tawang western ridge at the Bhutan border). Udayagiri completes his greeting at five-forty-five a.m. He returns down the ridge-trail to the monastery’s administrative wing where the kingdom’s morning-council convenes at six. The morning-council is a thirty-minute briefing with the senior administrative monk Sonam-Lama-Bumla (no relation to Sikkim’s Sonam at Rumtek; the name Sonam is common across the Karmapa-and-Tawang lineages), the state-government district magistrate’s representative, and the three regional dawn-walker captains (Lower Subansiri, Anjaw, and West Kameng). At the morning-council Sonam-Lama-Bumla raises a single new agenda-item: “The Tawang night-cult has grown to eleven hundred members. The new membership in the past fortnight is three hundred and twelve. The growth-rate is accelerating, not stabilising. The fourteenth-month threshold the night-cult appears to have set has been crossed. We must address it at the kingdom-level. Today.” Udayagiri sets down the tea-cup he had just lifted to his mouth. He has been hearing about the night-cult for fourteen months. He has approached it the way he approaches every shadow that forms inside the kingdom: more dawn, more first-light walks, more institutional outreach. He has personally led six morning-walks for war-trauma survivors over the past year. The morning-walks have helped no one. The walkers have politely attended; some have cried at the first-light moment; none have returned. The night-cult has been growing, in those fourteen months, by approximately fifteen new members per week. The eleven-hundred-member threshold means the cult is now larger than five of the kingdom’s twenty-six recognised village-clusters. He says, into the council chamber: “The Comforter. Has he been seen. Confirmed seen.” Sonam-Lama-Bumla says: “Three times in the last six weeks. The Sela Pass shadow-zone. Always in the late afternoon, two hours before sunset, when the pass-shadow is at maximum extent. Always speaking to the Galwan-veterans first — the 2020 survivors. Then the Tawang-clash bereaved, the 2022 families. Then the Ziro forest-fire burned, the 2024 survivors. The pattern is consistent. He is finding the wounds the kingdom’s dawn-walks have not reached.” Udayagiri says: “Description. Voice. Build. Gait.” Sonam-Lama-Bumla says: “Forty-four. Tall. Soft-voiced. Speaks Hindi and Apatani-Adi-Galo and what one of the older clash-veterans described as ‘cave-Tibetan’ — the dialect spoken in the deep-cave-system the kingdom’s northern border touches. Walks slowly. Sits with people. Listens for hours before speaking. When he speaks the listeners report a sense of being seen. The sense is the cult’s primary recruitment-vehicle. They are not joining a doctrine. They are joining a man who finally heard them.” Udayagiri does not respond for thirty seconds. He says: “Is he Asta-Asura.” Sonam-Lama-Bumla says: “The cave-Tibetan dialect is the indicator. So is the listening-pause. So is the height. The kingdom has not seen Asta-Asura since the cave-system battle nine years ago. The description matches.” THE SHADOW-TWIN Asta-Asura is, by the kingdom’s oldest dawn-walker commentary tradition, Udayagiri’s own shadow split off at the moment of his birth. He was born — that is, came into separate existence — in the first second of Udayagiri’s first dawn-greeting at the Tawang ridge thirty-one years ago, when the infant Udayagiri’s shadow cast eastward by the rising sun did not retract when the sun cleared the ridgeline. The shadow remained. It walked away. It grew. By the time Udayagiri was nine years old the shadow was a fully separate person, living in the deep-cave-system that runs beneath the Bhutan-Arunachal border, ten kilometres north of the Tawang valley. The shadow had a face. The face was Udayagiri’s face inverted — the same bone-structure, but the planes of light and dark reversed. Where Udayagiri’s cheek caught the morning sun, Asta-Asura’s cheek was in deeper shadow. Where Udayagiri’s eye reflected the dawn, Asta-Asura’s eye absorbed it. In Chapter One Udayagiri had not fought Asta-Asura directly. The Ch 1 antagonist had been the elder sister Tamasi, taken from the family at age three by raiders from across the northern border, raised in the cave-system that had become Asta-Asura’s territory. Tamasi’s suffering and Asta-Asura’s growth had occurred along the same cave-system but on different lineage-axes — she by human trauma, he by metaphysical detachment. At the Chapter One climax Tamasi had retreated deeper into the cave-system with the surviving cave-dwellers; Asta-Asura had vanished from sight altogether, his location unconfirmed for nine years. He has now reappeared at the Tawang night-cult’s recruitment-zone in the form of the Comforter. The reappearance is the chapter’s first event. THE COMFORTER’S OFFER Week two of the chapter. Tawang Sela-Pass shadow-zone — the wide afternoon-shadow that the four-thousand-one-hundred-metre Sela ridge casts down into the upper Tawang valley from about three p.m. to sunset. The shadow-zone is roughly four kilometres long and two kilometres wide. The temperature inside the shadow is approximately seven degrees Celsius cooler than the sunlit valley floor. The night-cult meets at the centre of the shadow-zone at four p.m. on weekdays. The meeting-place is a low wooden platform — about eight metres by six — constructed by the cult members over the past year out of timber recovered from the 2024 Ziro forest-fire’s burn-zone. The platform is the cult’s only physical structure. It has no walls, no roof. The shadow is the only ceiling. The cult does not call the platform a temple; they call it the listening-floor. Udayagiri walks to the listening-floor at four-thirty p.m. on the twelfth day of the chapter. He walks alone. He does not carry the Uday-spear (he has left it at the monastery; carrying it into the shadow-zone would be a hostile statement and he is not yet making one). He is in plain Adi-tribe wool with no insignia. He has dressed deliberately. He does not want to be seen as the kingdom’s representative; he wants to be seen as a member of the kingdom who has questions. The cult’s outer ring — about three hundred members today, the weekday-low — recognises him at about eighty metres. The recognition propagates inward. By the time he reaches the listening-floor’s edge there are perhaps forty cult-members in his immediate line-of-sight, all of them studying him with the careful absence-of-aggression that characterises every interaction inside the shadow-zone. The cult has explicit instructions from the Comforter: “If the dawn-walker comes, do not greet him as opposition. Greet him as a man who has not yet rested. Offer him shade. Do not insist on it.” Asta-Asura is on the listening-floor. He is in plain dark-grey homespun. He is sitting cross-legged at the floor’s eastern edge with a Galwan-survivor named Tashi-Norbu (the name Tashi is common in Tawang; this Tashi is a 2020 Galwan veteran, fifty-one years old, missing his left arm below the elbow from the river-stone clash). Asta-Asura has been sitting with Tashi-Norbu for two hours. Tashi-Norbu has been talking for the last forty minutes. Asta-Asura has not spoken during those forty minutes. He has nodded twice. Udayagiri stops at the listening-floor’s western edge. He does not step onto the floor. He waits. Asta-Asura looks up. He sees Udayagiri. He says, in plain Hindi, in the Comforter’s soft register: “Brother. You have come. Will you sit. There is room at the western edge.” The address is the kingdom’s neutral kinship-form — bhai, brother, used between strangers across the tribal-line. It carries no doctrinal weight. It is also, in the cave-Tibetan tradition Asta-Asura learned from, the form used to address a shadow-twin. Udayagiri does not sit. He stands at the western edge. He says, in the same plain Hindi, at the same volume: “Comforter. I have come to talk. I will stand. The talking will be brief.” Asta-Asura nods. He gestures to Tashi-Norbu in a way that indicates: Continue when you are ready; this conversation is between brothers; you are welcome to listen or to step off the floor as you choose. Tashi-Norbu stays. He has been silent in his life for a long time; he wants to hear what the dawn-walker will say to the man who finally listened to him. Udayagiri says: “What you are doing here is real. The grief is real. The shade you are offering is real. The cult members are real. I have come to ask three questions. Will you answer them.” Asta-Asura says: “Yes.” Udayagiri says: “First question. Are you Asta-Asura.” Asta-Asura says: “Yes.” Udayagiri says: “Second question. Are you here to destroy the dawn-walk.” Asta-Asura says: “No.” Udayagiri says: “Third question. What would you ask of the kingdom.” Asta-Asura is silent for about fifteen seconds. He looks at Tashi-Norbu beside him. He looks at the perhaps three hundred cult-members in the shadow-zone around them. He looks at the four-thousand-one-hundred-metre Sela ridge above them. He looks back at Udayagiri. He says: “I would ask that the kingdom build the room the morning will agree to wait outside of. The room is one room. The morning is the kingdom’s vocation. The room is the kingdom’s mercy. Build the room and I will be its caretaker. The cult-name will not change — the members have earned the name. The room will be the cult’s home and the kingdom’s recognised institution. The shade will not be hidden any more. The shade will be administered.” Udayagiri does not respond for a long time. He says, finally: “I will think.” He turns. He walks back across the shadow-zone toward the sunlit valley floor. The cult-members watch him leave. None of them speaks. Tashi-Norbu, after Udayagiri is about a hundred metres west of the listening-floor, says to Asta-Asura: “Comforter-la. Will he build the room.” Asta-Asura says: “He will. The brother does not yet know he will. He will know in about five weeks.” BABA PARASHAR ABOVE THE CLOUDS Week three. Above the cloud-line at four thousand two hundred metres on the Sela ridge’s northern face. The hermit’s shelter — a single stone hut three metres by two, no door (the wind does not enter at this altitude in this orientation), a single low entrance, a butter-lamp inside, a small stove, no other furniture. Baba Parashar has lived in the shelter for forty-one years. Baba Parashar is sixty-eight. He is in faded saffron robes, wool socks, no shoes. He has not descended below the cloud-line since 1985. He speaks to the clouds. The clouds report the weather-patterns of the entire subcontinent to him as patterns of vapour-shape. He interprets the patterns as metaphors. His prophecies are weather-forecasts in metaphor and they are always accurate. He has been Udayagiri’s mentor since Udayagiri was twelve. Udayagiri climbs to the shelter approximately twice a year. The climb takes three days from Tawang. Udayagiri arrives at the shelter at dusk on the seventh day after the listening-floor conversation. He carries one wool blanket, one small bag of tsampa-and-tea, one small bottle of mustard-oil, no spear (he never carries the spear above the cloud-line; the daily re-forging requires the dawn-light, which the cloud-line interrupts). He has been thinking about Asta-Asura’s offer for seven days. The thinking has produced no clarity. He has climbed for clarity. Baba Parashar is at the shelter’s entrance when Udayagiri arrives. He is looking northwest at a low cloud-bank that is forming over the Bhutan-border ranges. He does not turn at Udayagiri’s arrival. He waves toward the stove. He says, in Apatani-Adi-Galo: “Make the tea. Three cups. The third is for the cloud that arrives at midnight. The cloud is bringing news from the Lower Subansiri.” Udayagiri makes the tea. He boils the water on the stove with a small handful of pine-needle kindling. He pours three cups. He places the third at the shelter’s entrance for the midnight cloud. He sits beside Baba Parashar at the entrance. He says: “Baba-la. The Comforter has asked the kingdom to build a room. The room would hold managed shade alongside the dawn. The cult would be its caretakers. The kingdom would recognise the cult institutionally. I have been thinking for seven days. I do not know whether to build the room or to refuse it.” Baba Parashar does not respond immediately. He watches the northwest cloud-bank for about three minutes. The cloud-bank is moving slowly south-east; the cloud’s shape is a long thin ribbon with a slight curve at the western end. He says, finally: “The cloud at the western end is curved because the wind at four thousand metres is pushing it down and the wind at three thousand metres is holding it up. The cloud is being shaped by two opposing winds. The cloud does not choose between them. The cloud holds both shapes at once and the result is the curve. The curve is the cloud’s honest answer.” He pauses. He sips the tea. He says: “Your kingdom is being shaped by two opposing winds. The first wind is the kingdom’s six-century-old vocation of dawn-walking. The second wind is the eleven hundred unhealed members of the night-cult, plus the seventeen thousand five hundred war-trauma survivors and bereaved families and burned forest-survivors who have not joined the cult only because they do not yet know it exists. The first wind is the kingdom’s vocation. The second wind is the kingdom’s wound. You cannot push the second wind back to the cave-system; the wound is already inside the kingdom. You cannot push the first wind aside; the vocation is the kingdom’s name. The honest answer is the curve.” Udayagiri says: “Build the room.” Baba Parashar says: “Build the room. But understand the cost. The dawn-tribes will not forgive you for three or four generations. The night-cult will follow you only as long as the Comforter follows the agreement. Both factions will believe you have surrendered to the other. The kingdom will not understand the curve as the honest answer for at least a decade. You will be the dawn-walker who built the first shadow. The history-books in your lifetime will record this as your shame. The history-books in your great-grandchildren’s lifetime will record this as the kingdom’s saving. You will not be alive for the second history-book. You will live with the first one.” Udayagiri sits with the tea-cup at his lips for about a minute without drinking. He says: “Will you come down with me. Will you announce the room from the dawn-walker’s side. The dawn-tribes will not accept it from me alone. They will accept it from you.” Baba Parashar does not respond for thirty seconds. He says: “Yes. I will come down. I have been above the cloud-line for forty-one years. I am due to descend. The kingdom requires it. I will descend tomorrow at first light. I will walk to the Tawang valley pass with you. I will speak at the kingdom-council. I will say what I have said to you. The dawn-tribes will hear me. The night-cult will hear me. The kingdom will hear me.” He pauses. He looks at the northwest cloud-bank again. The cloud-bank has moved further south-east and the curve at the western end has deepened. He says: “The cloud is telling me one more thing. Both factions will be carrying weapons at the pass tomorrow. The cloud cannot tell me which faction will fire first. The cloud can only tell me that whoever stands at the centre of the pass between the two factions will be the cloud-shape’s curve. The curve does not survive being shaped by two opposing winds. The cloud knows this. The cloud is telling me anyway.” Udayagiri says: “Baba-la. Then I will stand at the centre. Not you.” Baba Parashar says: “No. You are the dawn-walker. The kingdom needs you alive on the other side of the curve. The curve needs a different body. The cloud has told me whose body. The cloud is honest. The cloud is also kind. The cloud has given me a forty-one-year warning. I have had enough warning. I will stand at the centre. You will stand at the dawn-tribe’s line, ten paces from me. You will not move when the arrows arrive. You will let me be the curve. The dawn will be your teacher in this matter and the cloud will be mine.” The midnight cloud arrives. The third tea-cup is drunk by the cloud. Udayagiri does not see the drinking but the cup is empty at one a.m. when he checks it. The cloud’s news from the Lower Subansiri is that the Lower Subansiri night-cult subsidiary has reached six hundred members; the news confirms the cult is no longer a Tawang-local phenomenon. The kingdom needs the room within the month. Udayagiri sleeps three hours. They begin the descent at four a.m. TWO ARROWS AT THE TAWANG VALLEY PASS Week three, day three. The Tawang valley pass at three thousand three hundred metres altitude — the principal valley-crossing between the eastern dawn-tribe villages and the western Sela-Pass shadow-zone where the night-cult meets. The pass is the kingdom’s natural geographical division between the two communities. Baba Parashar has descended below the cloud-line for the first time in forty-one years. The descent has taken him eleven hours; he is at the pass by three p.m. He is exhausted but composed. His feet, unused to walking at altitudes below four thousand metres, are blistered. He has refused medical attention. He says he will rest after the council. The kingdom-council has been convened on a Thursday-emergency basis. The eastern dawn-tribe representatives are arrayed on the pass’s eastern edge — about a hundred dawn-walker captains and tribe-elders from the thirteen recognised sun-tribes. They are armed with traditional dawn-walker spears (long copper-headed, eastern-light forged); some carry modern rifles purchased through the state-government’s tribal-defence allocation. The night-cult representatives are arrayed on the pass’s western edge — about a hundred and twenty cult-members. They are not armed with traditional weapons (the cult forbids them) but a significant minority carry modern hunting-rifles. The rifles are not officially cult-sanctioned but the cult’s senior delegates have not confiscated them. The pass itself — the open ground between the two edges — is about sixty metres wide and a hundred and twenty metres long. It is empty. Baba Parashar walks alone to the centre of the pass at three-fifteen p.m. Udayagiri stops at the eastern edge, ten paces from the dawn-tribe representatives’ front rank. Sonam-Lama-Bumla stops beside Udayagiri. Tashi-Norbu the Galwan-veteran has come from the western edge and stops at the night-cult representatives’ front rank. Baba Parashar reaches the centre. He stops. He raises his right hand. The gesture is the dawn-walker’s neutral-address gesture — palm forward, fingers spread, held at eye-level. He speaks. He speaks in Apatani-Adi-Galo, then repeats each sentence in cave-Tibetan, then repeats each sentence in Hindi. The triple-language address takes about twelve minutes. He says: “Kingdom of the rising sun. Children of the dawn-walk. Children of the listening-floor. I have come down from the cloud-line for the first time in forty-one years because the kingdom requires that I come down. The kingdom is being shaped by two opposing winds. The first wind is the dawn-vocation. The second wind is the kingdom’s wound. The honest answer is the curve. The curve is the building of the room the morning will agree to wait outside of. The room will be built at Tawang in the eleventh week of this chapter. The night-cult will be its caretakers. The dawn-walkers will recognise it institutionally. The kingdom-council will register the cult as the Order of the Dawn-Shadow Temple. The Comforter will be the Temple’s shade-master. Udayagiri will be the Temple’s dawn-master. Tani-of-the-Veil —” He pauses. He has not yet been introduced to Tani-of-the-Veil. He has been told her name by the cloud. He says it for the first time. “Tani-of-the-Veil will be the Temple’s translator between the two masters. The kingdom-council registers the Temple by my authority as the senior cloud-speaker of the kingdom. The registration is effective at the moment of this address’s completion. The dawn-tribes will accept the registration because the cloud-speaker has authority over kingdom-institutional matters under the kingdom’s six-century-old founding charter. The night-cult will accept the registration because the Comforter has agreed to the architecture in advance. The kingdom is now whole in the curve.” He lowers his right hand. The eastern dawn-tribe’s third-rank archer — an unnamed twenty-three-year-old dawn-walker who has been at the Tawang clash in 2022 and has lost his elder brother and his cousin in the same hour — raises his bow at three-twenty-seven p.m. He has not been authorised to fire. His bow-arm is shaking. The arrow is on the string. The western night-cult’s second-rank rifleman — an unnamed thirty-one-year-old Ziro forest-fire survivor whose grandmother died in the second-day burn-wave and whose face is half-scarred from the same fire — raises his rifle at three-twenty-seven p.m. He has not been authorised to fire. His finger is on the trigger. The rifle is unsafetied. Neither faction’s senior delegates see the raising. Neither faction’s discipline-officer intervenes. The dawn-walker fires the arrow at three-twenty-seven-thirty-eight p.m. The arrow travels approximately fifty-five metres at an initial velocity of fifty-four metres per second. The rifleman fires the bullet at three-twenty-seven-forty-two p.m. The bullet travels approximately forty-eight metres at an initial velocity of seven hundred and forty metres per second. The arrow reaches Baba Parashar’s chest at three-twenty-seven-thirty-nine-point-one p.m. The arrowhead enters between the third and fourth ribs on the right side and pierces the right lung at a depth of about eleven centimetres. The bullet reaches Baba Parashar’s back at three-twenty-seven-forty-two-point-three p.m. The bullet enters between the seventh and eighth thoracic vertebrae and exits through the upper-left abdomen. Baba Parashar is dead at three-twenty-eight-fourteen p.m. The death is approximately thirty-six seconds after the first arrow. The pass is silent for about eighteen seconds. Then both senior delegate-lines move forward simultaneously to apprehend their own shooters. The dawn-walker is restrained at three-twenty-eight-forty p.m. by Sonam-Lama-Bumla and two dawn-tribe elders. The rifleman is restrained at three-twenty-eight-forty-five p.m. by the night-cult’s discipline-officer Karma-Norbu (no relation to Tashi-Norbu). Udayagiri walks to the centre of the pass at three-twenty-nine-twelve p.m. He kneels beside Baba Parashar’s body. He does not weep. He places his right palm above his teacher’s forehead in the dawn-walker’s farewell gesture. He says, in plain Apatani-Adi-Galo, audibly to the eastern and western edges: “The curve has held. The cloud was honest. The cloud was kind. The forty-one-year warning was the cloud’s last gift.” Asta-Asura walks to the centre of the pass at three-twenty-nine-thirty p.m. He arrives from the western edge unarmed. He kneels at the opposite side of the body from Udayagiri. He places his right palm above his own forehead and lowers it — the cave-Tibetan farewell gesture, mirror to the dawn-walker’s. The two men do not look at each other. They look at the body. Asta-Asura says, in cave-Tibetan, then in Hindi: “Brother. The kingdom has begun the room with this man’s body at its first cornerstone. We will build the room. We will name the man at the consecration. We will not let his name fade. The cloud was right. The curve was honest. The kingdom needed this man’s body. I am not glad. I will help.” Udayagiri does not respond. He stays at the centre of the pass with the body until Sonam-Lama-Bumla and Karma-Norbu jointly arrange the stretcher and the carrying party. The body is carried up to the cloud-line that night for cremation in the traditional dawn-walker manner; Asta-Asura and three of his senior cult-delegates accompany the cremation party. The cremation is performed at first light the following morning by Udayagiri and Asta-Asura together — the first joint act across the faction-line. The ashes are divided equally; half are scattered above the cloud-line at the hermit’s shelter; half are returned to the Tawang valley and interred at the future Dawn-Shadow Temple site as the cornerstone. The two shooters are arrested by the Sikkim-Arunachal joint district-magistrate’s order at six p.m. that evening. Their trials are referred to the Arunachal High Court under regular criminal jurisdiction. Both men plead guilty within eleven days. Both serve fifteen-year sentences. Neither faction publicly defends the shooter. The kingdom’s common position, articulated by Sonam-Lama-Bumla at the post-pass press conference: “The two shooters are the kingdom’s failure to heal in time. They are not the kingdom’s representatives. They are the kingdom’s unhealed.” ═══ INTERVAL ═══ TANI-OF-THE-VEIL Week four. Tawang valley. The morning after the cremation. Tani-of-the-Veil arrives at the monastery’s administrative wing at five-thirty a.m. She is twenty-nine. She is a Galo-tribe woman from the Aalo district originally; she had been at the 2022 Tawang clash front-line as a battlefield medic with the Indian Army’s Arunachal Scouts; she had pulled seven wounded soldiers off the river-stone before being struck herself by a flying piece of granite and losing the sight in her right eye. She had been with the night-cult for thirteen months. She had served as Asta-Asura’s primary interpreter at the listening-floor — she was the only cult-member fluent in all four of the cult’s working languages (Apatani-Adi-Galo, cave-Tibetan, Hindi, and English). She is in the night-cult’s plain dark-grey homespun. She has a black wool veil draped across the right side of her face from temple to chin, covering the scarred right eye-socket and the surrounding tissue. The veil is held by a small silver clip at her right temple. The veil is the source of her cult-name: Tani-of-the-Veil. She has worn the veil since the cult’s third month. She walks into the monastery’s administrative wing alone, unannounced, having travelled from the Sela-Pass shadow-zone overnight on foot. She has not been authorised by the cult to make the journey. The cult’s senior delegates do not yet know she has come. She finds Udayagiri at the morning-tea station. He is alone — the morning-council has been postponed by three hours in observance of mourning. He looks up. She says, in plain Hindi, in a steady administrative-voice: “Dawn-Walker-la. I am Tani-of-the-Veil. The cloud-speaker named me yesterday at the pass. I have come to learn what the naming means.” Udayagiri sets down the tea-cup he has just lifted. He looks at her for about ten seconds. He sees the veil. He sees the steady voice. He sees the absence of cult-doctrine in the address. He says: “Sit, sister. The tea is fresh.” She sits. She accepts the tea. She does not speak again until the tea-cup is empty. She says, finally: “The naming means I am the translator. The cloud-speaker said the kingdom needs a translator between the dawn-master and the shade-master at the Temple. I am willing to be the translator if the kingdom is willing to receive me. The cult is willing to release me to the role if you formally request it of the Comforter; the request must come from your side because the cult’s rule against unilateral defection requires that any cross-over be initiated by the receiving institution, not by the defector. The Comforter will agree to the release. He told me last night, before the cremation. He named the cloud’s seeing as authority enough.” Udayagiri says: “Why you. Why this role.” She says: “Because I am the only cult-member who has lived in both worlds. I was at the Tawang clash, in the morning light, holding wounded men’s arteries closed with my hands. I was at the listening-floor, in the afternoon shadow, holding wounded men’s grief without trying to close it. The two practices are the same practice in two languages. The translator the kingdom needs is the one who has learned both languages by doing them. I am the only person currently alive in the kingdom who has done both.” He says: “The Tani in your name. The Adi-tribe creator-figure. Are you claiming descent from him.” She says: “The Tani in my name is from the cult. Roing village in the lower Dibang valley, approximately two hundred and eighty kilometres east of Tawang, at the kingdom’s eastern edge.
