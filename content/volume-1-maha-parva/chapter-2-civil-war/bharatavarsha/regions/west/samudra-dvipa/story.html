ಆಲಿಸಿ — ಕನ್ನಡ

ಇದು ಇಬ್ಬರು ಸಹೋದರರ ಕಥೆ — ಒಂದು ವಾಗ್ದಾನವನ್ನು ಕಾಪಾಡಲು ಪ್ರಾಣ ಕೊಟ್ಟ ಅಣ್ಣ ಸಾಗರದೇವ, ಮತ್ತು ವಿದೇಶಿ ಸಮವಸ್ತ್ರ ಧರಿಸಿ ಮರಳಿ ಬಂದ ತಮ್ಮ ಮಹಾ-ವೇಗ, ಕೊನೆಗೆ ಒಂದೇ ತೋಪಿನಲ್ಲಿ ಅಕ್ಕಪಕ್ಕದಲ್ಲಿ ಹೂಳಲ್ಪಟ್ಟ ಒಡಹುಟ್ಟಿದವರ ಕಥೆ.

ಕ್ವೆರಿಂ ಕಡಲತೀರ. ಕೊಂಕಣ ಕರಾವಳಿಯ ಅತ್ಯುತ್ತರ ಅಂಚು. ಉಬ್ಬರ ಎಷ್ಟು ದೂರ ಹಿಂದೆ ಸರಿದಿದೆಯೆಂದರೆ ಒದ್ದೆ ಮರಳು ಮುನ್ನೂರು ಮೀಟರ್ ಹಬ್ಬಿದೆ. ಇಬ್ಬರು ಮನುಷ್ಯರು ಒಂದೇ ಬಲೆಯನ್ನು ಹೆಣೆಯುತ್ತಿದ್ದಾರೆ. ಒಬ್ಬನಿಗೆ ನಲವತ್ತೆರಡು. ಒಬ್ಬನಿಗೆ ಎಂಭತ್ತು. ಎಂಭತ್ತು ವರ್ಷದವನು ಭಾವು ತೆಂಡುಲ್ಕರ್. ನಲವತ್ತೆರಡರವನು ಸಾಗರದೇವ.

"ನನ್ನ ಮುತ್ತಜ್ಜ ಮುಳುಗದ ವರ್ಷ ನಿನಗೆ ನೆನಪಿದೆ. ಆ ದಿನ ಅವನು ನಿನ್ನ ವಂಶಕ್ಕೆ ಒಂದು ವಾಗ್ದಾನ ಕೊಟ್ಟ." "ಆ ವಾಗ್ದಾನ ಏನು?" "ನಿನಗೆ ಆ ವಾಗ್ದಾನ ಗೊತ್ತು." "ಹೇಳು." ಭಾವು ತೆಂಡುಲ್ಕರ್ ಬಲೆಯಿಂದ ತಲೆ ಎತ್ತುತ್ತಾನೆ. ಅವನ ಕಣ್ಣುಗಳು ಅತ್ಯಂತ ಕಡಿಮೆ ಉಬ್ಬರದ ಸಮುದ್ರದ ಬಣ್ಣ. "ಸಾಗರವಂಶ ಗೋವಾ ಕರಾವಳಿಯನ್ನು ಯಾವುದೇ ಹೊರಗಿನ ನೌಕಾಪಡೆಯ ವಿರುದ್ಧ, ಶುದ್ಧ ಉಬ್ಬರ-ಪಾಲಕನಾಗಿ, ಸೂರ್ಯ ಮುಳುಗದ ಯಾವ ದಿಕ್ಕಿನಿಂದಲೂ ರಕ್ಷಿಸುತ್ತದೆ. ನೀನು ಯಾವ ವಿದೇಶಿ ಕೈಯ ಸಹಾಯವನ್ನೂ ಬೇಡುವುದಿಲ್ಲ. ಸ್ವೀಕರಿಸುವುದಿಲ್ಲ. ನೀನೇ ಉಬ್ಬರ. ಆ ಉಬ್ಬರವೇ ವಾಗ್ದಾನ." "ನಾನು ಅದನ್ನು ಕಾಪಾಡುತ್ತೇನೆ," ಎನ್ನುತ್ತಾನೆ ಸಾಗರದೇವ. "ನನಗೆ ಗೊತ್ತು ನೀನು ಕಾಪಾಡುತ್ತೀಯೆ. ನಾನು ಈಗ ಹೇಳುತ್ತಿದ್ದೇನೆ ಏಕೆಂದರೆ ಆ ಉಬ್ಬರವನ್ನು ಬೇಗ ಕಾಪಾಡಬೇಕಾಗುತ್ತದೆ. ನಾನು ಹೊಸ ಹಡಗುಗಳನ್ನು ಕಂಡಿದ್ದೇನೆ. ಅವು ದೊಡ್ಡವು. ನನಗೆ ಓದಲಾಗದ ಯಾವ ಬಾವುಟವನ್ನೂ ಹಾರಿಸುತ್ತಿಲ್ಲ."

ಮೂರು ವಾರಗಳ ನಂತರ, ಮೋರ್ಮುಗಾವ್ ಕೊಲ್ಲಿ. ಒಂದು ಕಂಟೈನರ್ ಹಡಗಿನ ಮೇಲೆ ಪತ್ರಿಕಾಗೋಷ್ಠಿ. ಪಾದ್ರೆ ಫೆರ್ನಾಂಡೋ ಕೊರ್ಡೆಯ್ರೋ ನಗುತ್ತಾ ಮಾತಾಡುತ್ತಾನೆ: "ಮೋರ್ಮುಗಾವ್ ಆಳಸಮುದ್ರ ಯೋಜನೆ ಗೋವಾವನ್ನು ಜಾಗತಿಕ ಸಾಗರ ಆರ್ಥಿಕತೆಗೆ ತರುತ್ತದೆ. ಸಾಂಪ್ರದಾಯಿಕ ಹಳ್ಳಿಗಳ ಮೀನುಗಾರಿಕೆ ಹಕ್ಕುಗಳನ್ನು ನಾವು ಗೌರವಿಸುತ್ತೇವೆ." ಅವರು ಗೌರವಿಸುವುದಿಲ್ಲ. ಆರು ದಿನಗಳ ನಂತರ, ಬಂದರು ಸಂಘ ಕರಾವಳಿಯ ಮೀನುಗಾರಿಕೆ ವಲಯಗಳನ್ನು ಮರುಚಿತ್ರಿಸುತ್ತದೆ, ತೆಂಡುಲ್ಕರ್-ವಂಶದ ಹಳ್ಳಿಗಳನ್ನು ಕಡಿಮೆ ಮೀನಿರುವ ಒಂದು ಕಿಲೋಮೀಟರ್ ದೂರಕ್ಕೆ ತಳ್ಳುತ್ತದೆ. ಸಾಗರದೇವ ಆಕ್ಷೇಪಣೆ ಸಲ್ಲಿಸುತ್ತಾನೆ. ಅದು ತಿರಸ್ಕರಿಸಲ್ಪಡುತ್ತದೆ. ಮೀನುಗಾರರು ಮತ್ತಷ್ಟು ದೂರ ಹೋಗುತ್ತಾರೆ. ದೋಣಿಗಳು ಚಿಕ್ಕದಾಗಿ ಮರಳುತ್ತವೆ. ತೆಂಡುಲ್ಕರ್-ವಂಶದ ವಿಧವೆಯರ ಸಂಖ್ಯೆ ಒಂದೇ ಋತುವಿನಲ್ಲಿ ಮೂರು ಪಟ್ಟಾಗುತ್ತದೆ.

ನಾಲ್ಕು ತಿಂಗಳ ನಂತರ, ಮುಂಜಾವಿನಲ್ಲಿ ಪಶ್ಚಿಮ ಕ್ಷಿತಿಜದಲ್ಲಿ ಒಂದು ನೌಕಾಪಡೆ ಕಾಣಿಸಿಕೊಳ್ಳುತ್ತದೆ. ಏಳು ಹಡಗುಗಳು. ಮೂರು ಬಾವುಟಗಳು — ಯಾವುದೂ ಭಾರತೀಯವಲ್ಲ. ಅದು ತನ್ನನ್ನು 'ಪರಂಪರಾ ನೌಕಾ ವಿನಿಮಯ' ಎಂದು ಕರೆದುಕೊಳ್ಳುತ್ತದೆ. ಹದಿನೆಂಟನೆಯ ದಿನ, ಒಬ್ಬ ಸಮವಸ್ತ್ರಧಾರಿ ಅಧಿಕಾರಿ ಪ್ರಧಾನ ಹಡಗಿನಿಂದ ಇಳಿಯುತ್ತಾನೆ. ಒಂದು ಕೋನದಿಂದ ಸಾಗರದೇವನನ್ನು ಕಂಡವರಿಗೆ ಅವನ ಮುಖ ಭಯಂಕರ ಪರಿಚಿತ. ಅವನನ್ನು ನೌಕಾಪಡೆಯ ಸಾಂಸ್ಕೃತಿಕ ಸಂಪರ್ಕ ಅಧಿಕಾರಿ ಎಂದು ಜಗತ್ತಿಗೆ ಪರಿಚಯಿಸಲಾಗುತ್ತದೆ. ಅವನ ಹೆಸರು ಮಹಾ-ವೇಗ. ಅವನು ಇಷ್ಟು ಕಾಲ ಜೀವಂತವಾಗಿದ್ದ.

ವಿದೇಶಿ ನೌಕಾಪಡೆಯ ಮೂರನೆಯ ವಾರದ ಚಂದ್ರನಿಲ್ಲದ ರಾತ್ರಿ, ಭಾವು ತೆಂಡುಲ್ಕರ್ ಹೊಸ ಗಡಿ ದಾಟಿ ಮೀನು ಹಿಡಿಯಲು ಹೋಗುತ್ತಾನೆ — ಅವನಿಗೆ ಹಕ್ಕಿದೆ, ಅವನು ಎಂಭತ್ತು ವರ್ಷದವನು ಮತ್ತು ಯಾವ ಗಡಿ ಜ್ಞಾಪಕಪತ್ರವೂ ಅವನ ಮುತ್ತಜ್ಜ ಹಾಕಿದ್ದನ್ನು ಕಿತ್ತುಕೊಳ್ಳಲಾರದು. ಮೋರ್ಮುಗಾವ್‌ನಿಂದ ವೇಗವಾಗಿ ಹೊರಡುತ್ತಿದ್ದ ಒಂದು ಸರಕು ಹಡಗು ತನ್ನ ರಾಡಾರ್‌ನಲ್ಲಿ ಅವನ ದೋಣಿಯನ್ನು ಗುರುತಿಸಲು ವಿಫಲವಾಗುತ್ತದೆ. ಅದು ಹಳೆಯ ಮರವನ್ನು ಚೂರುಚೂರು ಮಾಡುವ ಕೋನದಲ್ಲಿ ದೋಣಿಗೆ ಡಿಕ್ಕಿ ಹೊಡೆಯುತ್ತದೆ. ಹಡಗು ನಿಧಾನಿಸುವುದಿಲ್ಲ. ಭಾವು ತೆಂಡುಲ್ಕರ್ ಮುಂಜಾವಿನಲ್ಲಿ ತನ್ನ ದೋಣಿಯ ತುಂಡನ್ನು ಇನ್ನೂ ಹಿಡಿದು ಸಿಗುತ್ತಾನೆ. ಅಧಿಕೃತ ವರದಿ ಹೇಳುತ್ತದೆ: ಗೋಚರತೆ ವೈಫಲ್ಯ. ತನಿಖೆ ಮುಚ್ಚಲ್ಪಡುವುದಿಲ್ಲ.

ಭಾವು ತೆಂಡುಲ್ಕರ್‌ನನ್ನು ಕ್ವೆರಿಂ ಮೇಲಿನ ಸಮುದ್ರ-ಬಂಡೆ ಸ್ಮಶಾನದಲ್ಲಿ ಹೂಳಲಾಗುತ್ತದೆ. ಅವನ ಮಗಳು ಸೋನಾ ಅವನ ಮಡಚಿದ ಕೈಗಳಲ್ಲಿ ಒಂದು ಬಲೆಯ ಗಂಟು ಇಡುತ್ತಾಳೆ. ಸಾಗರದೇವ ಸಮುದ್ರ ಮತ್ತು ಲಹರ ಎಂಬ ಅವಳಿ ತ್ರಿಶೂಲಗಳನ್ನು ಬೆನ್ನಿಗೆ ಅಡ್ಡ ಕಟ್ಟಿ ಇಡೀ ಮೆರವಣಿಗೆಯಲ್ಲಿ ನಡೆಯುತ್ತಾನೆ. ಅವನು ಮಾತಾಡುವುದಿಲ್ಲ. ಆ ರಾತ್ರಿ ಅವನು ತನ್ನ ಗುಡಿಸಲಿನಲ್ಲಿ ಹೇಳುತ್ತಾನೆ: "ವಾಗ್ದಾನ ವಂಶಕ್ಕೆ ಮಾಡಲ್ಪಟ್ಟಿತ್ತು. ಎಂಭತ್ತು ವರ್ಷದವನು ತನ್ನ ಸ್ವಂತ ದೋಣಿಯಲ್ಲಿ ಸತ್ತಿದ್ದಾನೆ. ನಾನು ವಾಗ್ದಾನವನ್ನು ಕಾಪಾಡುತ್ತೇನೆ."

ಸಾಗರದೇವನಿಗೆ ವಿದೇಶಿ ಪ್ರಧಾನ ಹಡಗಿಗೆ ಸೌಜನ್ಯವಾಗಿ ಪ್ರವೇಶ ಸಿಗುತ್ತದೆ. ಅಡ್ಮಿರಲ್ ಅವನನ್ನು ಸ್ವೀಕರಿಸುತ್ತಾನೆ. ಮಹಾ-ವೇಗ ಅಡ್ಮಿರಲ್‌ನ ಬಲ ಭುಜದಲ್ಲಿ ತನ್ನ ಬಿಳಿ-ಚಿನ್ನದ ಸಮವಸ್ತ್ರದಲ್ಲಿ ನಿಲ್ಲುತ್ತಾನೆ. ಮಹಾ-ವೇಗ ಮಾತಾಡುತ್ತಾನೆ: "ಸಹೋದರ. ಕೂರು. ಅವರು ನಮಗೆ ಕರಾವಳಿಯನ್ನು ಹಂಚಿಕೊಳ್ಳಲು ಬಿಡುತ್ತಾರೆ. ಈ ಪ್ರದೇಶದಲ್ಲಿ ಕಾಡುಕೋಣ-ಬಂಧ ಹೊತ್ತವರು ನಾವಿಬ್ಬರೇ. ಬಂಧ ನಮಗೆ ಪರಸ್ಪರ ಹೋರಾಡಲು ಕೇಳುತ್ತಿಲ್ಲ. ಬಂಧ ಹಂಚಿಕೊಳ್ಳಲು ಕೇಳುತ್ತಿದೆ." ಸಾಗರದೇವ ಕೂರುವುದಿಲ್ಲ. "ಬಂಧ ನಿನ್ನ ಸಮವಸ್ತ್ರಕ್ಕಿಂತ ಹಳೆಯದು." "ಬಂಧ ನಿನ್ನ ವಾಗ್ದಾನಕ್ಕಿಂತಲೂ ಹಳೆಯದು. ನೀನು ಅವುಗಳಲ್ಲಿ ಒಂದನ್ನು ಬಿಡಬಹುದು." "ನನಗೆ ಸಾಧ್ಯವಿಲ್ಲ." ಅವನು ತಿರುಗಿ ಹಡಗಿನಿಂದ ಹೊರ ನಡೆಯುತ್ತಾನೆ.

ಮೂರು ರಾತ್ರಿ ಮೊದಲು, ಒಬ್ಬ ಹೆಣ್ಣು ಬಡಿಯದೆ ಅವನ ಗುಡಿಸಲಿಗೆ ಬರುತ್ತಾಳೆ. "ನನ್ನ ಹೆಸರು ಫೆರೇರಾ-ದ-ಸಂಗಮ್. ನಾನು ಮಿಶ್ರ ಉಬ್ಬರದ ಮಗಳು. ನೀನು ಗೋವಾ ಎಂದು ಕರೆಯಲು ನಿರಾಕರಿಸುತ್ತಿರುವ ಗೋವಾ ನಾನು. ಮೋರ್ಮುಗಾವ್‌ನ ನೌಕಾಪಡೆ ತನ್ನನ್ನು ನನ್ನ ತಂದೆಯ ಜನರ ವಾರಸುದಾರ ಎಂದು ಕರೆದುಕೊಳ್ಳುತ್ತದೆ. ಅವರು ಅಲ್ಲ. ಆದರೆ ನೀನು ಅವರನ್ನು ಮರಳಿ ಬರುತ್ತಿರುವ ಸಾಮ್ರಾಜ್ಯವೆಂದೂ, ನಿನ್ನನ್ನು ಶುದ್ಧ-ರಕ್ತದ ಉಬ್ಬರ-ಪಾಲಕನೆಂದೂ ಭಾವಿಸಿ ಹೋರಾಡುತ್ತಿದ್ದೀಯೆ. ಗೋವಾ ಶುದ್ಧವಲ್ಲ. ನಾಲ್ಕುನೂರು ವರ್ಷಗಳಿಂದ ಶುದ್ಧವಾಗಿಲ್ಲ. ನನ್ನನ್ನು ಸಹಾಯ ಮಾಡಲು ಬಿಡು. ನಾನು ಸೇತುವೆಯಾಗುತ್ತೇನೆ." "ನೀನು ವಾಗ್ದಾನ ಸ್ವೀಕರಿಸಬೇಡ ಎಂದ ಸಹಾಯ." "ಹೌದು." "ಹಾಗಾದರೆ ವಾಗ್ದಾನ ನನಗೆ ನಿನ್ನ ಬಗ್ಗೆ ಹೇಳಿತ್ತು. ಮನೆಗೆ ಹೋಗು." ಫೆರೇರಾ ಒಮ್ಮೆ ತಲೆಯಾಡಿಸಿ ಹೊರ ನಡೆಯುತ್ತಾಳೆ.

ಮುಂಜಾವಿನ ಮೊದಲು ನದಿಯಲ್ಲಿ ಅಗಸ್ತ್ಯ ಋಷಿಯ ಆತ್ಮ ಅಲೆಯೇಳುತ್ತದೆ. ಸಾಗರದೇವ ಸಂದೇಶ ಓದುತ್ತಾನೆ: ಕರ್ಣ ತನ್ನ ತಾಯಿಯನ್ನು ಮೂರು ಬಾರಿ ನಿರಾಕರಿಸಿದ. ಮೂರನೆಯ ನಿರಾಕರಣೆ ಅವನನ್ನು ಕೊಂದಿತು. ನೀನು ಎರಡನೆಯದರಲ್ಲಿದ್ದೀಯೆ. "ಹಾಗಾದರೆ ಮೂರನೆಯದು ಬಿರುಗಾಳಿ," ಎನ್ನುತ್ತಾನೆ ಸಾಗರದೇವ.

ಬಂದರು ಸಂಘದ ಉದ್ಘಾಟನೆಯ ಹಿಂದಿನ ರಾತ್ರಿ. ಸಾಗರದೇವ ಸೂರ್ಯಾಸ್ತಕ್ಕೆ ಮೂರು ಗಂಟೆ ಮೊದಲು ತನ್ನ ಅಜ್ಜ ಕಟ್ಟಿದ ಅತ್ಯಂತ ಹಳೆಯ ದೋಣಿಯಲ್ಲಿ ಒಬ್ಬಂಟಿಯಾಗಿ ಹೊರಡುತ್ತಾನೆ. ಬಲಗೈಯಲ್ಲಿ ಸಮುದ್ರ, ಎಡಗೈಯಲ್ಲಿ ಲಹರ. ಬೇರೇನೂ ಇಲ್ಲ. ಅವನು ಮೋರ್ಮುಗಾವ್ ಕೊಲ್ಲಿಯ ಕೇಂದ್ರಕ್ಕೆ ಹೋಗುತ್ತಾನೆ. ಸೂರ್ಯ ಪಶ್ಚಿಮ ಕ್ಷಿತಿಜವನ್ನು ಮುಟ್ಟಿದಾಗ, ಅವನು ಎರಡೂ ತ್ರಿಶೂಲಗಳನ್ನು ಎತ್ತಿ ಸಾಗರವಂಶದ ಅತ್ಯಂತ ಹಳೆಯ ಸಮುದ್ರ-ಮಂತ್ರವನ್ನು ಹೇಳುತ್ತಾನೆ — ೧೮೨೦ರಿಂದ ಯಾವ ವಾರಸುದಾರನಿಗೂ ಹೇಳುವ ರಕ್ತ-ಹಕ್ಕಿರಲಿಲ್ಲ. ಅವನಿಗೆ ಆ ಹಕ್ಕಿದೆ. ಅವನು ಹತ್ತು ಸಾವಿರ ವರ್ಷ ಕೊಲ್ಲಿಯನ್ನು ರೂಪಿಸಿದ ಕೊಂಕಣ ಚಂಡಮಾರುತ-ಆತ್ಮವನ್ನು ಕರೆಯುತ್ತಾನೆ. ಮೊದಲ ಗಾಳಿ ಬಡಿಯುತ್ತದೆ.

ಇಪ್ಪತ್ತು ನಿಮಿಷಗಳಲ್ಲಿ ಬಿರುಗಾಳಿ ವಿದೇಶಿ ನೌಕಾಪಡೆಯನ್ನು ಕೊಲ್ಲಿಯಲ್ಲಿ ಸಿಕ್ಕಿಸುತ್ತದೆ. ಅಲೆಗಳು ಎಂಟು ಮೀಟರ್. ಮೋರ್ಮುಗಾವ್‌ನ ನೀರು ಮಹಾ-ವೇಗ ಕೆಳಗಿನಿಂದ ಏಳುವಲ್ಲಿ ಕುದಿಯುತ್ತದೆ. ಅವನು ತನ್ನ ಬಿರುಗಾಳಿ-ರೂಪದಲ್ಲಿ ಏಳುತ್ತಾನೆ — ಕರಾಳ ನೀರು ಮತ್ತು ಮಿಂಚಿನಿಂದ ಮಾಡಲ್ಪಟ್ಟ, ಈಗ ಸಾಗರದೇವ ಎಬ್ಬಿಸಿದ ಚಂಡಮಾರುತದೊಂದಿಗೆ ಹೆಣೆದು. ಎರಡು ಬಿರುಗಾಳಿಗಳು ಪರಸ್ಪರ ಗುರುತಿಸಿಕೊಳ್ಳುತ್ತವೆ. ಅವು ಯಾವಾಗಲೂ ಎರಡು ಮುಖ ಧರಿಸಿದ ಒಂದೇ ಹವಾಮಾನವಾಗಿದ್ದವು. "ಸಹೋದರ. ನೀನು ಅತ್ಯಂತ ಹಳೆಯ ಚಂಡಮಾರುತವನ್ನು ಎಬ್ಬಿಸಿದೆ. ನಾನು ಅದರೊಳಗೆ ನಿನ್ನೊಂದಿಗಿದ್ದೇನೆ. ನಾವು ಸಮಾನರು. ಕೊಲ್ಲಿಯನ್ನು ಹಂಚಿಕೊಳ್ಳೋಣ. ಸಹೋದರರಿಗೆ ಮರಳೋಣ." "ನೀನು ಅವರ ಸಮವಸ್ತ್ರ ಧರಿಸಿದ್ದೀಯೆ." "ಸಮವಸ್ತ್ರ ಕಳಚುತ್ತದೆ. ಸಹೋದರ ಕಳಚುವುದಿಲ್ಲ."

ಸಾಗರದೇವ ಉತ್ತರಿಸುವುದಿಲ್ಲ. ಅವನು ಸಮುದ್ರವನ್ನು ಎಸೆಯುತ್ತಾನೆ. ತ್ರಿಶೂಲ ಚಂಡಮಾರುತದ ಗೋಡೆಯ ಮೂಲಕ ಸಾಗಿ, ಬಿರುಗಾಳಿ ಮನುಷ್ಯನನ್ನು ಭೇಟಿಯಾಗುವ ಗಂಟಿನಲ್ಲಿ ಮಹಾ-ವೇಗನ ರೂಪಕ್ಕೆ ತಾಗುತ್ತದೆ. ಆ ಹೊಡೆತ ಕೊಲ್ಲುವುದಿಲ್ಲ. ಅದು ತೆರೆಯುತ್ತದೆ. ಒಂದು ಸೆಕೆಂಡಿಗೆ ಮಹಾ-ವೇಗನ ಬಿರುಗಾಳಿ-ರೂಪ ಬಿಚ್ಚಿಕೊಳ್ಳುತ್ತದೆ, ಮತ್ತು ಒಳಗೆ ಮನುಷ್ಯ ನಿಲ್ಲುತ್ತಾನೆ — ತೋಯ್ದ ಸಮವಸ್ತ್ರ, ತಾನು ಕಾಣಲ್ಪಟ್ಟ ಆಶ್ಚರ್ಯದಿಂದ ಅಗಲವಾದ ಕಣ್ಣುಗಳು. ಸಾಗರದೇವ ಲಹರವನ್ನು ಎಸೆಯುತ್ತಾನೆ. ಎರಡನೆಯ ತ್ರಿಶೂಲ ಬಿರುಗಾಳಿಯೊಳಗಿನ ಮನುಷ್ಯನ ಕೊರಳಿನ ಗಂಟಿಗೆ ತಾಗುತ್ತದೆ. ಮಹಾ-ವೇಗ ಮುಂದಕ್ಕೆ ಕೊಲ್ಲಿಗೆ ಬೀಳುತ್ತಾನೆ. ನೀರು ಅವನನ್ನು ತೆಗೆದುಕೊಳ್ಳುತ್ತದೆ. ಬಿರುಗಾಳಿ ತನ್ನ ಎರಡನೆಯ ಧ್ವನಿಯನ್ನು ಕಳೆದುಕೊಂಡಿದೆ. ಅದು ಈಗ ಸಾಗರದೇವನದ್ದು ಮಾತ್ರ.

ಅವನ ಬಳಿ ಯಾವ ತ್ರಿಶೂಲವೂ ಇಲ್ಲ. ಬರಿಗೈಯಲ್ಲಿ ದೋಣಿಯಲ್ಲಿ ನಿಂತಿದ್ದಾನೆ. ಅವನು ಈಗ ಬಿರುಗಾಳಿಯನ್ನು ಬಿಡುಗಡೆ ಮಾಡಲಾರ — ಬಿಟ್ಟರೆ ವಿದೇಶಿ ನೌಕಾಪಡೆ ಚೇತರಿಸಿಕೊಂಡು ಮಹಾ-ವೇಗನ ಸಾವು ವ್ಯರ್ಥವಾಗುತ್ತದೆ. ಪ್ರಧಾನ ಹಡಗು ಒಡೆಯುವವರೆಗೆ ಅವನು ಬಿರುಗಾಳಿಯನ್ನು ಹಿಡಿಯಬೇಕು. ತ್ರಿಶೂಲಗಳಿಲ್ಲದೆ ಚಂಡಮಾರುತ ತನ್ನ ಕರೆದವನನ್ನೇ ಒಳಗಿನಿಂದ ತಿನ್ನುತ್ತದೆ. ಆದರೂ ಅವನು ಹಿಡಿಯುತ್ತಾನೆ. ಪ್ರಧಾನ ಹಡಗು ಬಂಡೆಗೆ ತಾಗಿ ತಳಭಾಗದಲ್ಲಿ ಒಡೆಯುತ್ತದೆ. ಮುಳುಗಲಾರಂಭಿಸುತ್ತದೆ. ಸಾಗರದೇವ ಚಂಡಮಾರುತವನ್ನು ಬಿಡುಗಡೆ ಮಾಡುತ್ತಾನೆ. ಬಿರುಗಾಳಿ ಅವನ ಮೇಲೆ ಕುಸಿಯುತ್ತದೆ.

ಮುಂಜಾವು. ಕೊಲ್ಲಿ ಅವಶೇಷ. ಹಳೆಯ ಮೀನುಗಾರರು ಬಿರುಗಾಳಿ ಉಳಿಸಿದ ದೋಣಿಗಳಲ್ಲಿ ಹೊರಬರುತ್ತಾರೆ. ಸೋನಾ ತೆಂಡುಲ್ಕರ್ ಅವರನ್ನು ಮುನ್ನಡೆಸುತ್ತಾಳೆ. ಅವರು ಸಾಗರದೇವನ ದೇಹವನ್ನು ಸಿನ್ಕ್ವೆರಿಂ ಬಳಿ ಅವನ ದೋಣಿಯಲ್ಲಿ ಕಾಣುತ್ತಾರೆ. ಎರಡೂ ತ್ರಿಶೂಲಗಳು ಹೋಗಿವೆ. ಅವನ ಕೈಗಳು ತೆರೆದಿವೆ. ಇನ್ನೂರು ಮೀಟರ್ ದಕ್ಷಿಣದಲ್ಲಿ ಮಹಾ-ವೇಗನ ದೇಹ ಸಿಗುತ್ತದೆ, ಇನ್ನೂ ಬಿಳಿ-ಚಿನ್ನದ ಸಮವಸ್ತ್ರದಲ್ಲಿ. ಅವನ ಮುಖ ಇನ್ನು ಕೋಪಗೊಂಡಿಲ್ಲ. ಸೋನಾ ತೆಂಡುಲ್ಕರ್ ಒಂದು ತೀರ್ಮಾನ ಮಾಡುತ್ತಾಳೆ. ಅವಳು ಇಬ್ಬರು ಸಹೋದರರನ್ನು ಒಂದೇ ಕರಾವಳಿ ತೋಪಿಗೆ ಒಟ್ಟಿಗೆ ಒಯ್ದು ಅಕ್ಕಪಕ್ಕದಲ್ಲಿ ಹೂಳುತ್ತಾಳೆ. ಸಾಗರದೇವನ ಬಲಗೈಯಲ್ಲಿ ಒಂದು ಬಲೆ-ಗಂಟು ಇಡುತ್ತಾಳೆ. ಮಹಾ-ವೇಗನ ಸಮವಸ್ತ್ರದಿಂದ ಹಿತ್ತಾಳೆ ಭುಜ-ತುಂಡುಗಳನ್ನು ತೆಗೆದು, ತೋಪಿನ ಪ್ರವೇಶದ್ವಾರದಲ್ಲಿ ಇಡುತ್ತಾಳೆ, ಎಲ್ಲರೂ ಏನನ್ನು ಕೆಳಗಿಡಲಾಗುತ್ತಿದೆ ಎಂದು ಕಾಣುವಂತೆ. ಕಪ್ಪು-ಜುಟ್ಟಿನ ಬುಲ್‌ಬುಲ್ ಸಮಾಧಿಗಳ ಮೇಲೆ ಒಮ್ಮೆ ಹಾಡುತ್ತದೆ.

ಗೋವಾದ ಒಳನಾಡಿನ ಬೆಟ್ಟಗಳಲ್ಲಿ, ಅದೇ ಮುಂಜಾವು, ಸಾಗರದೇವ ತನ್ನ ಬಂಧ ಹೊಂದಿದ್ದ ಗೋವಾ ಕಾಡುಕೋಣ ಹುಲ್ಲಿನಿಂದ ತಲೆ ಎತ್ತುತ್ತದೆ. ಅದಕ್ಕೆ ಗೊತ್ತು. ಅದು ಉತ್ತರಕ್ಕೆ ತಿರುಗುತ್ತದೆ. ಅದು ಗೋವಾ-ಮಹಾರಾಷ್ಟ್ರ ಗಡಿಯಲ್ಲಿ ನಿಲ್ಲುವುದಿಲ್ಲ, ಏಕೆಂದರೆ ಅದಕ್ಕೆ ಎಂದೂ ಗಡಿಯೇ ಇರಲಿಲ್ಲ. ಅದು ಸಹ್ಯಾದ್ರಿಯ ಒಂದು ಕೋಟೆಯೆಡೆಗೆ ನಡೆಯುತ್ತದೆ, ಅಲ್ಲಿ ತನ್ನ ಕಿರಿಯ ಚಿಕ್ಕಪ್ಪನ ಬಂಧ ತನ್ನೆಡೆಗೆ ಬರುತ್ತಿದೆ ಎಂದು ಇನ್ನೂ ಹೇಳಲ್ಪಡದ ಒಬ್ಬ ವೀರೆ ಇದ್ದಾಳೆ.

ಮತ್ತು ಬಿರುಗಾಳಿ ಗೋವಾದ ಮೇಲೆ ಬೇರೊಂದನ್ನೂ ಬಿಡುತ್ತದೆ. ಹಳೆಯ ಗೋವಾದ ಅವಶೇಷಗಳ ಕೆಳಗಿನ ಕಡಲತೀರದಲ್ಲಿ ಒಬ್ಬ ಮನುಷ್ಯ ಅಲೆಗಳಿಂದ ಹೊರ ನಡೆಯುತ್ತಾನೆ. ಅವನ ಉಡುಪು ಹದಿನೇಳನೆಯ ಶತಮಾನದ್ದು, ಮತ್ತು ಅದು ಪುರಾತನ ವಸ್ತುವಲ್ಲ. ಅವನಿಗೆ ತನ್ನ ಹೆಸರು ನೆನಪಿಲ್ಲ. ಅವನ ನೆರಳು ಮುಂಜಾವಿನ ಸೂರ್ಯನಿಗೆ ನಿಖರವಾಗಿ ಹೊಂದಿಕೊಳ್ಳುವುದಿಲ್ಲ. ಪಾದ್ರೆ ಕೊರ್ಡೆಯ್ರೋ — ಒಂದು ಗಂಟೆ ಮೊದಲು ಬಂದರು ಸಂಘಕ್ಕೆ ರಾಜೀನಾಮೆ ಕೊಟ್ಟವನು — ಅವನು ಏನೆಂದು ಗುರುತಿಸುತ್ತಾನೆ. ಅವನು ಕರ್ನಾಟಕದ ಸುದ್ದಿ ಓದಿದ್ದಾನೆ. ಮೂರನೆಯ ಸಂದೇಶವಾಹಕನ ಬಗ್ಗೆ. ಅವನು ಆ ಮನುಷ್ಯನನ್ನು ಫೆರೇರಾ-ದ-ಸಂಗಮ್‌ನ ಮನೆಗೆ ಕರೆದೊಯ್ಯುತ್ತಾನೆ.

"ನಿನ್ನ ಹೆಸರೇನು?" ಮನುಷ್ಯ ಯತ್ನಿಸುತ್ತಾನೆ. ಹೆಸರು ಬರುವುದಿಲ್ಲ. ಫೆರೇರಾ ತೀರ್ಮಾನಿಸುತ್ತಾಳೆ. "ಹಾಗಾದರೆ ನಾನು ನಿನಗೊಂದು ಕೊಡುತ್ತೇನೆ. ವಾಸ್ಕೋ. ಹಡಗಿಲ್ಲದ ವಾಸ್ಕೋ. ನೀನು ಇಲ್ಲಿ ಇರುತ್ತೀಯೆ. ಸಿದ್ಧವಾಗುವವರೆಗೆ ನೆನಪಿಸಿಕೊಳ್ಳುವಂತೆ ಕೇಳಲ್ಪಡುವುದಿಲ್ಲ. ಕರ್ನಾಟಕ ಬಳಸಿದ ಅದೇ ನಿಯಮ. ಸಂದೇಶವಾಹಕನಿಗೆ ಒಂದು ಹೆಸರು ಕೊಡಲಾಗುತ್ತದೆ. ಸಂದೇಶವಾಹಕ ಕೊಲ್ಲಲ್ಪಡುವುದಿಲ್ಲ."

The Foreign Sea — A Film in Two Halves
═══ FIRST HALF ═══ OPENING — THE PROMISE AT LOW TIDE Querim beach. The northernmost edge of the Konkan coast. The tide is so far out that the wet sand stretches three hundred meters before it finds the surf. Two men are mending the same net. One is forty-two. One is eighty. The eighty-year-old is Bhau Tendulkar. The forty-two-year-old is Sagaradeva. They have been mending the net for ninety minutes. They have spoken about eleven words. This is how it should be. Goan fisherman-talk is a thing of sea-pauses and net-knots and not very many sentences. "You remember the year my great-grandfather did not drown." "I remember." "He made a promise to your line that day. You remember the promise." Sagaradeva's hands do not stop on the net. He nods. "I am eighty," Bhau Tendulkar says, "and my great-grandfather’s promise is the only thing left of him in the world. It has been three generations. The Tendulkars have kept the promise to your line. We have not asked your line to fish for us. We have not asked you to help us when the monsoon took our boats. We have kept the promise we extracted, and we have kept it on our side too." "What is the promise." "You know the promise." "Say it." Bhau Tendulkar looks up from the net. His eyes are the colour of the sea at the deepest reach of low tide — pale, salt-pale, looking through. "The Sagaravamsha will defend the Goan coast against any outside fleet, as a pure tide-keeper, from any direction the sun does not set. You will not need help from any foreign hand. You will not accept any. You are the tide. The promise is the tide. That is the promise." Sagaradeva says, "I keep it." "I know you keep it. I am telling you now because the tide is going to need to be kept very soon. I have seen the new ships. They are not the ships my grandfather warned us about. They are new ships. They are larger. They fly no flag I can read." Above them, in the sky, the black-crested bulbul lands on the gunwale of the upturned boat behind Sagaradeva. The bird does not sing. It watches. Sea-birds know when a promise is being witnessed. THE PORT CONSORTIUM Mormugao Bay, three weeks later. A press conference held aboard a freshly-painted container vessel. A man in a tropical-weight white suit speaks Konkani, then English, then Portuguese, in that order, smiling. His name is Padre Fernando Cordeiro. "The Mormugao Deepwater Initiative," Padre Cordeiro says, "will bring Goa into the global maritime economy. Three thousand jobs. A two-hundred-metre quayside. Container traffic that will compete with Colombo and Cochin. Heritage protections for the old jetty. Of course we will respect the fishing rights of the traditional villages. Of course." Of course they will not. Six days after the press conference, the port consortium files a memorandum with the state government redrawing the fishing zones for the coast from Aguada to Vasco. The new boundaries push the Tendulkar-line villages a kilometre offshore in waters with fewer fish. Sagaradeva is not at the press conference. Sagaradeva is at Querim, with the nets. By the time he reads the memorandum, three of his villages have already lost their season. He goes to the state secretariat in Panaji. He speaks to four officials. He explains. The officials are polite. The officials say the memorandum is filed and signed. The officials suggest he file an objection in writing within thirty days. He files the objection. The objection is denied on a procedural ground that no one in any village has the lawyer to contest. The fishermen go out further. The boats start coming back smaller. The Tendulkar-line widows triple in number across one season. THE FOREIGN NAVY ARRIVES Mormugao Bay, four months after Bhau Tendulkar said the promise aloud. A naval fleet appears on the western horizon at dawn. Seven ships. The flagship is a destroyer with a white-and-gold hull and three flags — one historical, two modern, none Indian. The other six ships are escorts. The fleet is conducting what its press release calls a Heritage Naval Exchange. Goa is a port of call. The state government has approved the visit because the port consortium has guaranteed the docking fees. The fleet will be in Mormugao for fifteen days. On the eighteenth day — the fleet did not leave on the fifteenth — a uniformed officer disembarks the flagship in the morning and walks alone down the gangway. He is tall. White-and-gold tailored uniform. Brass on his shoulders. His face is hauntingly familiar to anyone who has seen Sagaradeva from a certain angle. He shakes hands with Padre Cordeiro on the quay. He gives interviews. He is introduced to the world as the fleet’s Cultural Heritage Liaison — their Goan-born specialist. His name is Maha-Vega. He has been alive the whole time. The black-crested bulbul flies over the quay once and does not land. THE DEATH OF BHAU TENDULKAR The accident happens on a moonless night in the third week of the foreign fleet's stay. Bhau Tendulkar has taken his fishing boat out past the new boundary — he has the right because he is eighty and no boundary memorandum can take what his grandfather’s grandfather laid. He is in his usual place at the usual hour. A port consortium freighter on its way out of Mormugao, leaving heavy and travelling fast, fails to register his boat on its bridge radar. The freighter hits the boat at the angle that splits old wood into kindling. The freighter does not slow. Bhau Tendulkar is found at sunrise by the next morning’s fishermen, three kilometres south of where his boat was hit, still gripping a piece of his own gunwale. The official report says: visibility failure. The freighter’s captain is Indian. His employer is Singaporean. His insurer is in London. The investigation into the death is filed in Panaji and forwarded to Mumbai and never closed. Bhau Tendulkar is buried in the sea-cliff cemetery above Querim. His daughter Sona puts a single fishing-net knot into his folded hands. Sagaradeva walks the entire procession with the twin tridents Samudra and Lahara crossed on his back. He does not speak. The black-crested bulbul sits on the cliff edge and stays there until the grave is closed. That night Sagaradeva sits in his hut with the two tridents across his knees. He says, to no one and the bird: "The promise was made to the line. The grandson kept the promise. The great-grandson kept it. The eighty-year-old is dead in his own boat. I will keep the promise." The Karna-shadow becomes complete in him in that hour. The bird in the rafters does not look at him. THE FIRST CONFRONTATION — THE FLAGSHIP Sagaradeva is permitted aboard the foreign flagship as a courtesy. He climbs the gangway with no weapons. Admiral Carvalho-Almeida receives him in the wardroom. Maha-Vega stands at the admiral's right shoulder in his white-and-gold uniform. "My fleet is in Mormugao under an exchange protocol approved by the Government of India," Carvalho-Almeida says, in serviceable English. "Your complaints belong to your port consortium and your state. We are guests." "You are guests who brought my brother." "Mr Maha-Vega is a Goan-born specialist we recovered from a foreign port. We brought him home." "He is not home." Maha-Vega speaks. His voice is the same voice from Chapter One but warmed by years of being addressed as “sir.” "Brother. Sit. They will let us share the coast. You and I. The fleet will sail in two months. The port will operate. The fishing zones can be renegotiated. We can do this together. We are the only two who carry the gaur-bond in this region. The bond is not asking us to fight each other. The bond is asking us to share." Sagaradeva does not sit. "The bond is older than your uniform." "The bond is also older than your promise. You can let one of them go." "I cannot." He turns and walks off the flagship. Halfway down the gangway he looks up. The bulbul is on the lower spar of the foreign foremast. The bird is sitting where bulbuls do not sit — on a foreign ship. The bird does not look at him. Sagaradeva understands what the omen is saying, and chooses not to. ═══ INTERVAL ═══ His own hut. Three nights before the storm. A woman walks in without knocking. She is thirty-three. Sun-dark skin, green eyes that are not the green of the Konkan sea but of a different latitude entirely. Hair tied back with a scrap of patterned silk. She is wearing the simple cotton of a Pernem village woman, but the cross at her throat is silver and the silver was minted in Lisbon. "My name is Ferreira-de-Sangam. My father’s great-great-great-great-great-grandfather was a shipwright in Old Goa in 1693. My mother’s family has been in Pernem for as long as the wells have had water. I am the daughter of the mixed tide. I am the Goa you have been refusing to call Goa." Sagaradeva does not stand. "I am here to offer help. The fleet at Mormugao calls itself heir to my father’s people. They are not. They are a corporation with sails. But you are fighting them as if they were a returning empire and you were a pure-blood tide-keeper. Goa is not pure. Goa has not been pure for four hundred years. If you try to defend it as if it were, you will lose, and the loss will be louder than the defence." "I made a promise." "Yes. To Bhau Tendulkar’s great-grandfather. About what counts as foreign. The promise was made in 1873. The world that the promise named no longer exists. Let me help. Let me be the bridge. The fleet does not know how to fight a Goa that calls me one of its tide-keepers, because I am one of them and one of you. They cannot frame me as the invader." He looks at her for a long time. "You are the help the promise told me not to accept." "Yes." "Then the promise told me about you." She is quiet. Her green eyes do not waver. "That is one reading," she says. "There is another reading. The promise told you not to accept help from a foreign hand. I am not foreign. I have been here three hundred years. The hand the promise warned against was a hand that had not yet washed itself in the river that runs to this sea. My hand has. Touch it. See if it feels foreign." He does not touch it. He says, "Go home." Ferreira-de-Sangam nods once. She walks out. She does not slam the door. The door does not need slamming. In the river before dawn, Sage Agastya’s spirit ripples across the surface. Sagaradeva walks down to the river and reads the message in the rapids: Karna refused his mother three times. The third refusal killed him. You are at the second. Sagaradeva says aloud, to the rapids: "Then the third will be the storm." ═══ SECOND HALF ═══ THE STORM HE RAISES The night before the port consortium’s ceremonial opening of the Mormugao Deepwater Initiative. The state Chief Minister is scheduled to attend. The foreign admiral will speak. There will be fireworks. There will be press helicopters. The bay will be brightly lit and full of dignitaries. Sagaradeva sails out alone three hours before sunset in his oldest outrigger — the one his grandfather built. He carries Samudra in his right hand and Lahara in his left. He carries nothing else. He has not eaten in two days. He has not spoken in three. He sails to the centre of Mormugao Bay. He waits for the sun to set. The bay is calm. The foreign flagship is at anchor in the deepwater channel with all its lights on. The other six ships of the fleet are arranged around it in formation. The press helicopters circle. When the sun touches the western horizon, Sagaradeva raises both tridents. He says the oldest sea-mantra of the Sagaravamsha — the one that has not been said in two hundred years because no Sagaravamsha heir since 1820 has had the bloodline-claim to say it. He has the claim. He says the mantra. He calls the cyclone of the Konkan coast that has shaped the bay every August for ten thousand years — not the season’s natural cyclone, which has already passed, but the cyclone-spirit that lives beneath the season and answers only to a Sagaravamsha trident. The mantra takes seven minutes. By the time he finishes the sky is the colour of old bronze. The press helicopters are turning back to land. The first wind hits. Within twenty minutes the storm has the foreign fleet trapped in the bay. The waves are eight metres. The flagship’s anchor chain breaks. The escort ships scatter and one runs aground on the rocks below Aguada Fort. Two more are dismasted. The press helicopters are gone. Sagaradeva is in his outrigger at the centre of the storm. The cyclone wraps around him. He is not wet — the storm respects its caller. He sails toward the flagship. MAHA-VEGA RISES The bay’s water boils where Maha-Vega rises from beneath. He has not been on the flagship. He has been waiting. He rises in his own storm-form — the same form he wore in Chapter One, made of dark water and lightning, but now braided with the cyclone Sagaradeva has just raised. The two storms recognize each other. They were always one weather wearing two faces. "Brother," Maha-Vega says, and his voice carries through the storm because the storm is his too. "You have raised the oldest cyclone. I am inside it with you. We are equals. Let us share the bay. Let the foreign fleet leave. Let us return to brothers." "You wear their uniform." "The uniform comes off. The brother does not." Sagaradeva does not answer. He throws Samudra. The trident travels through the cyclone’s wall and strikes Maha-Vega’s storm-form at the joint where the storm meets the man. The strike does not kill. The strike opens. Maha-Vega’s storm-form unravels for one second, and inside it the man stands — tailored uniform soaked through, brass shoulders steaming, eyes wide with the surprise of being seen. Sagaradeva throws Lahara. The second trident strikes the man inside the storm at the joint of the throat. Maha-Vega falls forward into the bay. The water takes him. The storm above the bay continues, but the storm has lost its second voice. It is only Sagaradeva’s now. He has no tridents. He stands in the outrigger with empty hands. The flagship is two hundred metres away. The cyclone is still spinning. THE FLAGSHIP Admiral Carvalho-Almeida is on the flagship’s bridge with three officers. The flagship’s engines are out. The flagship’s anchor is gone. The flagship is drifting toward the same rocks below Aguada Fort that have already claimed one of its escorts. The admiral orders the flagship’s deck guns to fire on the outrigger. The guns fire. They do not hit the outrigger. The cyclone deflects the shells, sends them spinning back at the flagship. Two shells strike the flagship’s own hull. The flagship lists ten degrees. The admiral orders the missile system. The cyclone deflects the missiles too. They fall into the sea or strike the escorts. Sagaradeva is empty-handed in the outrigger. He has no weapons. He has only the cyclone. He cannot release it now — if he releases it, the foreign fleet will recover, regroup, and the death of Maha-Vega will mean nothing. He has to hold the storm until the flagship breaks. He holds. The cyclone is too much for one man without tridents. He feels his hands burning. He feels his lungs burning. The Sagaravamsha mantra requires a balance the tridents provide — the cyclone is meant to be channelled through metal, not flesh. Without the tridents the storm consumes its caller from the inside. He holds anyway. The flagship hits the rocks. The flagship breaks at the keel. The flagship begins to sink. Admiral Carvalho-Almeida dies on the bridge giving an order that never finishes. The escorts that can still float are limping out of the bay; they will not return. Sagaradeva releases the cyclone. The storm collapses on him. THE MORNING AFTER Dawn. The bay is wreckage. The flagship is half-submerged on the rocks below Aguada Fort. Three escorts are sunk. Two limped south during the night and have not been heard from. One escort has surrendered to the Indian Coast Guard at Marmagao with white flags flying. The Old Fishermen come out at first light in their own boats — the boats the storm spared, because the storm knew which boats it was for and which boats it was not for. Sona Tendulkar leads them. They find Sagaradeva’s body in his outrigger, washed against the coast at Sinquerim. Both tridents are gone. He is holding nothing. His hands are open. The black-crested bulbul is on the bow of the outrigger and does not move when Sona reaches in to lift his body. They find Maha-Vega’s body at the same beach, two hundred metres south. He is still wearing the white-and-gold uniform, soaked dark. His face is no longer angry. Sona Tendulkar makes a decision that is not hers to make and that no one in the village contradicts. She has the two brothers carried up together to the same coastal grove. They are buried side by side. She places a fisherman’s net-knot in Sagaradeva’s right hand. She removes the brass shoulder-pieces from Maha-Vega’s uniform and lays them at the grove’s entrance, polished, where everyone arriving for the burial can see them and know what is being put down. The bulbul sings once over the graves. The sound is not a celebration. It is the song the Konkan bulbul sings when a storm has ended and the season is going to be different next year. THE WALKING GAUR In the inland hills above Goa, the same morning, the Gaur of Goa — the Indian gaur whose pulse Sagaradeva read through the soil — lifts her head from the grass. She is the largest wild bovine in Bharatavarsha. Her shoulders are taller than most men. Her horns are old. She has been bonded to Sagaradeva since he was twenty-two. She knows. She turns north. She begins to walk. She does not stop at the Goa-Maharashtra border because there has never been a border for her. She walks through the Konkan night-forest. The black-crested bulbul leaves the grove and flies above her at canopy height. The two of them go north together. In Maharashtra, on a fort in the Sahyadri, an Indian Giant Squirrel will, three nights later, fall silent for the first time in her own observed lifetime. THE SECOND HERALD AT OLD GOA Old Goa, the colonial ruins, the morning of the dawn after the storm. Tourists are not allowed in — the area was evacuated for the storm warning — but the ruins have a watchman, and he is the one who sees it first. A man walks out of the surf on the small beach below the ramparts. He is wet. He is not gasping — he walks as if he had stepped off a gangway, not as if he had swum ashore. His shirt is white linen. His breeches are dark wool. The cut of both is seventeenth century. The wool is not heavy with seawater the way wool gets. The linen is salt-stained in a pattern that does not match a single recent immersion. The watchman speaks to him in Konkani. The man does not answer. The watchman speaks in English. The man looks at him without recognition. The watchman tries Portuguese, which the watchman does not really speak but has the vocabulary of a tourist guide. The man’s eyes shift. Something flickers. He tries to speak. He gets three syllables out before the sentence falls away. He cannot remember the word. The watchman fetches Padre Cordeiro, who is in Old Goa that morning because Padre Cordeiro has, an hour earlier, resigned from the port consortium and walked to the cathedral to think. Padre Cordeiro speaks Portuguese fluently. He tries. The man cannot remember. Padre Cordeiro sees, in the man’s clothing and the man’s eyes and the angle at which his shadow does not exactly match the morning sun, what he is. He has read the Karnataka news from two months earlier. He has read about the Third Herald. He does not raise his voice or call for help. He takes the man’s hand. "Come with me." He walks the man across Old Goa to Ferreira-de-Sangam’s rebuilt-house. Ferreira is awake. Ferreira is on her front step with a small notebook in her lap and a coffee she has not drunk. She has been making a list since dawn. Padre Cordeiro says, "He came out of the surf. He cannot remember his name. His clothes are from 1730 and they are not antique." Ferreira looks at the man. She does not flinch. She has been waiting all night for whatever the storm would leave on her step. This is what the storm left. "What is your name?" The man tries. His mouth moves. The name does not come. She thinks. She decides. "Then I will give you one. Vasco. Vasco-without-a-ship. You will stay here. You will sit at this table. You will not be asked to remember until you are ready, and you will not be asked to leave for as long as I can keep you. The same rule that Karnataka used. The Herald is given a name. The Herald is not killed." The man — Vasco-without-a-ship now — sits down on her front step. His shadow does not match the morning sun. It points slightly off, the way the Karnataka herald’s did. Ferreira marks the angle in her notebook without commenting. Padre Cordeiro stays. He has nowhere to go. THE BIRD FROM THE WEST By midday a second Indian Roller arrives at Ferreira’s house — not the bulbul, an Indian Roller, the South-region courier-bird. It carries a folded slip of paper. The hand is Krishnadevaraya-Bhrata’s, written eight days earlier in Kerala, where he has just begun his training under old Parashurama for the 109th form. The note reads: The throne is broken. The bond passed to me. Your Gaur is walking north. The Council must sit. The mouth has opened. Stay where you are. I will find you when I can. Ferreira reads the note. She does not understand all of it. She understands enough. She folds the note. She puts it in her notebook between two pages. She looks up at the sky — not the sun, the sky — and she begins, quietly, to plan a Goa that Sagaradeva would not have allowed and that Bhau Tendulkar’s great-grandfather did not foresee. The black-crested bulbul, now somewhere on the Goa-Maharashtra border above a walking gaur, sings once at noon. The Sagaravamsha-line is not extinguished. It is migrating.
