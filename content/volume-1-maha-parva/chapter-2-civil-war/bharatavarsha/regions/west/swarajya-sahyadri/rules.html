The Double Bond — A Saga First

Shivagati is the first hero in Bharatavarsha to carry two living animal-bonds at the same time. The bonds do not contest each other — they share the body the way two languages share a fluent speaker. Each bond has its own tattoo, its own power, its own animal. They cannot be separated except by the death of the bearer.

Native • Earned Ch 1 • Stage II.5 at chapter end
Shvet — The Indian Giant Squirrel
Native bond of her grandfather’s line. Reads the friction of bark with body weight. Grants Shivagati impossible balance — the wall-climb is its expression. Shvet himself dies in the burnt-nest event mid-chapter; a new young squirrel arrives on her shoulder within a week (the species moves to fill the bond-vacancy automatically — this is a property of Sahyadri Giant Squirrels recorded in her grandfather’s bond-commentary). Foreleg-mark completes at the wall-climb.
Inherited • Arrives mid-Ch 2 • Stage I at chapter end
The Goan Gaur — Sagaradeva’s Inheritance
Walked north from the Konkan forest after Sagaradeva’s death. Grants seismic-pulse reading of the ground for kilometres — the bovine equivalent of the squirrel-balance reading, but tuned to ground instead of bark. Hoofprint-mark begins on Shivagati’s upper back when the gaur kneels at her outpost. Stage I completes within a fortnight. Will transfer to Simhavikrama of Gujarat at the end of Ch 3.
Saga-Rule Established This Chapter • The Double-Bond Mechanic
A hero may carry two animal-bonds only if: (1) one bond is native by lineage at birth, (2) the second bond is walked-in from a dying Bloodline-related heir, (3) the bearer’s body has the bond-fluency to hold both without one drowning the other. The squirrel-bond’s balance and the gaur-bond’s seismic-read are complementary — small-frame, fine-detail vs large-frame, coarse-ground. They share fluency. Shivagati is the saga’s example. Per restructure D9 the only other double-bonded hero in Volume I will be Simhavikrama (lion + gaur, Chapter 3).

The Two Tattoo Ladders — Squirrel & Gaur

Each bond has its own four-stage ladder. Shivagati ends Ch 2 with the squirrel-ladder at Stage II.5 and the gaur-ladder at Stage I. The two ladders climb in parallel but at different rates — the squirrel is older in her body and climbs faster. Both are interrupted in Chapter Three. The squirrel-ladder dies with her. The gaur-ladder transfers and continues with Simhavikrama, beginning again at his Stage I.

Sacred Weapons — Vanara & Its Modern Echo

Ancient Weapon • Earned in Ch 1, Used Mid-Ch 2
Vanara — The Rock-Iron Staff
Type
Six-foot quarterstaff of meteoric Sahyadri rock-iron, forged in 1893 from the meteorite that fell on Pratapgad’s western slope.
Ability
Carries the seismic memory of the meteorite’s impact — when grounded firmly and struck against rock, it transmits a low-frequency pulse through the mountain that her gaur can hear from kilometres away. The squirrel-bond’s balance lets her wield it one-handed on cliff faces no climber could weight a staff on.
Chapter Two Use
She does not raise it in the command-room scene with Tanaji-the-Second — the staff is left leaning against the wall. The non-use is the rule. Vanara is for the moment after the choice, never the moment of the choice.
Modern Weapon • The Fort-Net
Hariyal-Net — The Pigeon Mesh
Type
Encrypted radio-and-pigeon hybrid relay connecting all forty-seven Sahyadri forts.
Ability
Two-channel redundancy: encrypted VHF radio for daily traffic, Yellow-footed Green Pigeon for messages too sensitive to send by radio. The pigeons fly the same routes their ancestors flew under Shivaji’s scouts. The mesh survived the Murud-Janjira defection because each fort holds its own keys.
Custodian
Operationally run by Bhausaheb at Sinhagad. Strategically Shivagati’s. Will pass to Tanaji-the-Second in Ch 3 after Shivagati’s death and to Simhavikrama-via-federation in late Ch 3 when Maharashtra is absorbed.

The Wall-Climb Rite — New Recruitment Form

Tanaji-the-Second’s acceptance of Shivagati establishes a new form for follower-recruitment in the saga — one that becomes a model for later kingdoms in Chapter Three.

  1. The hero arrives alone at the follower’s threshold — not with army, not with retinue.
  2. The hero arrives by the difficult route — up the wall, not down the road. The route is the credential.
  3. The hero leaves the weapon at the door — Vanara is set down before the command-room is entered.
  4. The hero asks to be chosen on character, not on inheritance or contract. Specifically: “Choose me by what I did, not by who my father was.”
  5. The follower studies the body — the cuts on the hands, the marks of the climb. The body is the certificate.
  6. The follower kneels in half-recognition, not full subjugation. This is fort-commander custom — never the full bow.
  7. The follower returns the weapon with a remark about its next use, not the current one.
  8. Tea is poured.

The rite has no documented source older than this chapter. It is observed once, then becomes lore. By Chapter Three at least three other heroes attempt their own variants: Jagannath-Bandhu (Odisha) pulls the chariot alone; Moirangthem (Manipur) dances the disqualification dance; Krishnadevaraya-Bhrata (Karnataka, in retrospect) climbs the buried-temple sublevel three blueprints rather than a wall. The wall is the original.

Void Maw Sign • Catalogue Entry • Maharashtra
The Burnt Squirrel-Nest. An Indian Giant Squirrel’s nest at the top of a Sahyadri ironwood tree, four kilometres from a failed-fire sabotage attempt — but unrelated to it. The nest is ash. The tree below is untouched. The squirrel is gone. No body, no feathers, no claws, no drag-marks. The carbonisation is upper-canopy only, room-temperature when found. Mechanism inferred (through the squirrel-bond): the Void Maw has read the Sahyadri and removed one squirrel from the record. The carbonisation is what remains when a creature’s presence is erased without anything ever having been there to remove. The Karnataka rule holds — Shivagati does not retaliate. Tanaji-the-Second builds a small shrine at the tree’s base from Sahyadri rock and packed clay. The shrine becomes a pilgrimage site within the season. Maharashtra’s Maw sign is the first place-based sign in the chapter, where Karnataka and Goa’s signs were person-based (Heralds). The Maw appears in three forms across Chapter Two: people, places, and absence-of-things.
