ಆಲಿಸಿ — ಕನ್ನಡ

ಇದು ಒಬ್ಬ ಅಣ್ಣ ಮತ್ತು ಅವನ ತಂಗಿಯ ಕಥೆ — ಈ ಬಾರಿ ಖಡ್ಗದಿಂದ ಅಲ್ಲ, ಕಾನೂನಿನ ಕಾಗದಗಳಿಂದ ತನ್ನ ತಂಗಿಯ ವಿರುದ್ಧ ನಿಂತ ಅಣ್ಣ ವನರಕ್ಷ, ಮತ್ತು ಆ ಗೋಡೆಗೆ ತಾನು ತರಬೇತಿ ಪಡೆದಿರಲಿಲ್ಲ ಎಂದು ಅರಿತ ತಂಗಿ ಶಿವಗತಿಯ ಕಥೆ.

ಪ್ರತಾಪಗಡ. ಬೆಳಗ್ಗೆ ಐದೂವರೆ. ಪರ್ವತ ಉಸಿರಾಡುತ್ತಿದೆ — ಕೋಟೆಯ ಕೆಳಗಿನ ಮೋಡಗಳು ಮೇಲಿನ ಮೋಡಗಳಿಗಿಂತ ಬೇರೆ ದಿಕ್ಕಿನಲ್ಲಿ ಚಲಿಸುತ್ತವೆ. ಗೋಡೆಯ ಮೇಲೆ ನಿಂತ ಹೆಣ್ಣು ತನ್ನ ಬದುಕಿನ ಪ್ರತಿ ಮುಂಜಾವೂ ಇದನ್ನು ಗಮನಿಸಿದ್ದಾಳೆ. ಅವಳ ಹೆಸರು ಶಿವಗತಿ. ಮೂವತ್ತೊಂದು ವರ್ಷ. ಅವಳ ಬೆನ್ನಿಗೆ ಕಬ್ಬಿಣದ ದಂಡ ವಾನರ ಕಟ್ಟಲ್ಪಟ್ಟಿದೆ. ಅವಳ ಭುಜದ ಮೇಲೆ ಅವಳ ಸಹ್ಯಾದ್ರಿ ದೈತ್ಯ ಅಳಿಲು ಶ್ವೇತ. ಆರು ಗಂಟೆಗೆ ಸರಿಯಾಗಿ ಹಳದಿ-ಪಾದದ ಹಸಿರು ಪಾರಿವಾಳ ಹರಿಯಾಲ್ ಗೋಡೆಯ ಮೇಲೆ ಹಾರುತ್ತದೆ — ರಾಜ್ಯದ ಗಡಿಯಾರ.

ಶಿವಗತಿ ಸಂತೋಷವಾಗಿದ್ದಾಳೆ. ನಲವತ್ತೇಳು ಕೋಟೆಗಳು ಒಕ್ಕೂಟವಾಗಿವೆ. ಅಳಿಲು-ಬಂಧ ಭದ್ರವಾಗಿದೆ. ವಸಂತದಲ್ಲಿ ತಾನು ಸೋಲಿಸಿದ ಅಣ್ಣ ಮೌನವಾಗಿದ್ದಾನೆ. ಪರ್ವತ ಅವಳೊಂದಿಗೆ ಒಪ್ಪುತ್ತದೆ. ಪರ್ವತ ಮೊದಲೂ ತಪ್ಪಾಗಿದೆ. ಇದು ಆ ಮುಂಜಾವುಗಳಲ್ಲೊಂದು.

ಬೆಳಗ್ಗೆ ಒಂಬತ್ತು ಗಂಟೆ, ಇನ್ನೂರು ಕಿಲೋಮೀಟರ್ ಆಗ್ನೇಯ. ಪುಣೆ. ಮಹಾರಾಷ್ಟ್ರ ಪರಂಪರಾ ನ್ಯಾಯಾಲಯ. ಕಪ್ಪು ನಿಲುವಂಗಿಯ ವಕೀಲ ನೂರ ಮೂವತ್ತೆರಡು ಪುಟಗಳ ಕಡತವನ್ನು ಹೊತ್ತು ಮೆಟ್ಟಿಲೇರುತ್ತಾನೆ. ಅವನ ಕಕ್ಷಿದಾರನ ಹೆಸರು ವನರಕ್ಷ. ಆ ಕಡತ ೧೯೪೭ರ ನಂತರದ ನ್ಯಾಯಾಲಯ ಕಂಡ ಅತ್ಯಂತ ಸ್ವಚ್ಛ ಪ್ರಾಥಮ್ಯ ಅರ್ಜಿ. ಅದು ಒಂದೇ ವಾಕ್ಯದ ಪರಿಹಾರ ಕೇಳುತ್ತದೆ: [ಅದೇ ತಂದೆತಾಯಿಯ] ಮಗ ಮತ್ತು ಇಬ್ಬರು ಮಕ್ಕಳಲ್ಲಿ ಹಿರಿಯನಾದ ವನರಕ್ಷನನ್ನು ಸಹ್ಯಾದ್ರಿ ಒಕ್ಕೂಟದ ನ್ಯಾಯಯುತ ಉತ್ತರಾಧಿಕಾರಿ ಎಂದು ಗುರುತಿಸಿ, ಶಿವಗತಿ ತನ್ನ ಆಡಳಿತವನ್ನು ಹಿಂತೆಗೆದುಕೊಳ್ಳುವಂತೆ ನಿರ್ದೇಶಿಸಬೇಕು. ಅದು ಸರಿಯಾಗಿದೆ. ಕಾನೂನುಬದ್ಧವಾಗಿ ಸರಿಯಾಗಿದೆ.

ಹನ್ನೊಂದು ಗಂಟೆಗೆ ಆ ಸುದ್ದಿ ಶಿವಗತಿಗೆ ತಲುಪುತ್ತದೆ. ಅವಳು ಅದನ್ನು ಎರಡು ಬಾರಿ ಓದುತ್ತಾಳೆ. ಶಪಿಸುವುದಿಲ್ಲ. ಏನನ್ನೂ ಎಸೆಯುವುದಿಲ್ಲ. ಅವಳು ಗೋಡೆಗೆ ನಡೆದು, ಭುಜದ ಮೇಲಿನ ಶ್ವೇತನಿಗೆ ಗಟ್ಟಿಯಾಗಿ ಹೇಳುತ್ತಾಳೆ: "ಅವನು ಹೋರಾಡಲು ಬರುತ್ತಿಲ್ಲ. ಅವನು ಅರ್ಜಿ ಸಲ್ಲಿಸಲು ಬರುತ್ತಿದ್ದಾನೆ. ನಾನು ಇದಕ್ಕೆ ಎಂದೂ ತರಬೇತಿ ಪಡೆದಿಲ್ಲ."

ಮೂರನೆಯ ದಿನಕ್ಕೆ ಮೂರು ಕೋಟೆಗಳು ಅರ್ಜಿಯನ್ನು ಸಾರ್ವಜನಿಕವಾಗಿ ಬೆಂಬಲಿಸುತ್ತವೆ. ಮೂರನೆಯ ವಾರದ ಕೊನೆಗೆ, ಇಪ್ಪತ್ತನಾಲ್ಕು ಕೋಟೆಗಳು ವನರಕ್ಷನ ಹಕ್ಕಿಗೆ ಸಹಿ ಹಾಕಿವೆ. ಒಕ್ಕೂಟದ ಅವ್ಯವಸ್ಥೆಗಿಂತ ಉತ್ತರಾಧಿಕಾರದ ಊಹಿಸಬಹುದಾದ್ದನ್ನು ಆದ್ಯತೆ ನೀಡುತ್ತಾರೆ. ಶಿವಗತಿ ತನ್ನ ಉಳಿದ ಹದಿನಾಲ್ಕು ಕೋಟೆಗಳನ್ನು ಹಿಡಿದಿಡುತ್ತಾಳೆ. ಅವಳು ಪ್ರತಿ ಕೋಟೆಗೂ ಸ್ವತಃ ಪ್ರವಾಸ ಮಾಡುತ್ತಾಳೆ. ಪ್ರತಿ ಕೋಟೆಯಲ್ಲೂ ಕೇಳುತ್ತಾಳೆ: "ನೀನು ಯಾಕೆ ಉಳಿಯುತ್ತಿದ್ದೀಯೆ?" ಸಿಂಹಗಡದಲ್ಲಿ ಅವಳ ಸೋದರಣ್ಣ ಭಾವುಸಾಹೇಬ್ ಹೇಳುತ್ತಾನೆ: "ಸೋದರಸಂಬಂಧಿ, ಅರ್ಜಿ ತಾಂತ್ರಿಕವಾಗಿ ಮಾನ್ಯ. ಅರ್ಜಿ ತಪ್ಪು ಎಂಬುದನ್ನು ಅವಲಂಬಿಸದ ಒಂದು ಕಾರಣ ನೀನು ಕೊಡುವವರೆಗೆ ನಾನು ಈ ಕೋಟೆಯನ್ನು ನಿನಗಾಗಿ ಹಿಡಿಯುತ್ತೇನೆ." ಅವಳ ಬಳಿ ಆ ಕಾರಣ ಇನ್ನೂ ಇಲ್ಲ.

ನಾಲ್ಕನೆಯ ವಾರ. ಮುರುಡ್-ಜಂಜಿರಾ ಕರಾವಳಿ ಕೋಟೆ ರಾತ್ರೋರಾತ್ರಿ ವನರಕ್ಷನ ಪಕ್ಷಕ್ಕೆ ಸೇರುತ್ತದೆ. ಆ ಕೋಟೆದಾರ ದತ್ತಾಜಿ ಮೋಹಿತೆ ಶಿವಗತಿ ನಿರೀಕ್ಷಿಸದ್ದನ್ನು ಮಾಡುತ್ತಾನೆ. ಅವನು ಕೋಟೆಯ ಪಕ್ಕದ ಸಣ್ಣ ದ್ವೀಪ-ಸ್ಮಶಾನವನ್ನು ವಶಪಡಿಸಿಕೊಳ್ಳುತ್ತಾನೆ — ಶಿವಗತಿಯ ಹಿರಿಯ ಸೋದರಣ್ಣ ಭಾವುಸಾಹೇಬ್-ದ-ಫಸ್ಟ್ ಹೂಳಲ್ಪಟ್ಟ ಸ್ಮಶಾನ, ಮೊದಲ ಅಧ್ಯಾಯದ ವಸಂತ ಯುದ್ಧದಲ್ಲಿ ಹದಿನಾರನೆಯ ವಯಸ್ಸಿನಲ್ಲಿ ಸತ್ತವನ ಸಮಾಧಿ, ಶಿವಗತಿ ಹದಿನೈದು ವರ್ಷಗಳಿಂದ ಪ್ರತಿ ಹುಣ್ಣಿಮೆಗೆ ಭೇಟಿ ನೀಡಿದ ಸಮಾಧಿ. ಆ ಶುಕ್ರವಾರ ಹುಣ್ಣಿಮೆ ಬರುತ್ತದೆ. ಹದಿನೈದು ವರ್ಷಗಳಲ್ಲಿ ಮೊದಲ ಬಾರಿಗೆ, ಶಿವಗತಿ ತನ್ನ ಸೋದರಣ್ಣನ ಸಮಾಧಿಗೆ ಭೇಟಿ ನೀಡಲಾಗುವುದಿಲ್ಲ.

ಅವಳು ಆ ಶುಕ್ರವಾರ ರಾತ್ರಿ ಪ್ರತಾಪಗಡದ ಗೋಡೆಯ ಮೇಲೆ ಕೂರುತ್ತಾಳೆ. ಅವಳು ಶಾಂತವಾಗಿ ಹೇಳುತ್ತಾಳೆ: "ಅವನು ಕಾನೂನು ಮತ್ತು ಪ್ರೀತಿ ಒಂದೇ ಜಾಗದಲ್ಲಿರುವ ಸ್ಥಳವನ್ನು ಕಂಡುಕೊಂಡಿದ್ದಾನೆ. ಅಲ್ಲಿ ನಾನು ಅವನೊಂದಿಗೆ ಹೋರಾಡಲಾರೆ. ಅವನು ಗೋಡೆಗೆ ತರಬೇತಿ ಪಡೆದಿಲ್ಲ." ಆ ಕೊನೆಯ ವಾಕ್ಯದ ಅರ್ಥ ಅವಳಿಗೆ ಇನ್ನೂ ಗೊತ್ತಿಲ್ಲ. ಅದು ನಂತರ ಬರುತ್ತದೆ.

ಅವಳು ತನ್ನ ಅತ್ಯಂತ ಕೆಳಗಿನ ಕಾವಲುಗೋಪುರದಲ್ಲಿ ಕೂತಿರುವಾಗ, ಕೆಳಗಿನ ಕಾನನದ ಜಾಡಿನ ಮೇಲೆ ಒಂದು ಬೃಹತ್ ಆಕೃತಿ ಚಲಿಸುವುದನ್ನು ಕಾಣುತ್ತಾಳೆ — ಆನೆಯ ಗಾತ್ರ, ಆದರೆ ತಪ್ಪು ಆಕೃತಿ. ಆರು-ಮೀಟರ್ ಕಾಡುಕೋಣ. ಹರಿಯಾಲ್ ಹಾಡುತ್ತದೆ. ಪಾರಿವಾಳ ರಾತ್ರಿಯಲ್ಲಿ ಹಾಡುವುದಿಲ್ಲ. ಅದು ಒಂದು ಸಾಗರ-ವಂಶದ ಬಂಧ ಪರ್ವತವನ್ನು ಸಮೀಪಿಸುತ್ತಿರುವಾಗ ಮಾತ್ರ ರಾತ್ರಿಯಲ್ಲಿ ಹಾಡುತ್ತದೆ. ಅವಳು ಇಳಿಯುತ್ತಾಳೆ. ಕಾಡುಕೋಣ ಉಪ್ಪು-ಕಲೆಯಿಂದ ಕೂಡಿದೆ. ಅದರ ಬೆನ್ನ ಮೇಲೆ ಗೋವಾದ ಕಪ್ಪು-ಜುಟ್ಟಿನ ಬುಲ್‌ಬುಲ್ ನಿದ್ರಿಸುತ್ತಿದೆ.

ಶಿವಗತಿ ಜಾಡಿನ ಅಂಚಿನಲ್ಲಿ ಮಂಡಿಯೂರುತ್ತಾಳೆ. "ನೀನು ಇಲ್ಲಿಗೆ ನಡೆದು ಬಂದೆ. ನನ್ನ ಚಿಕ್ಕಪ್ಪ ಸತ್ತಿದ್ದಾನೆ." ಕಾಡುಕೋಣ ತಲೆ ಎತ್ತಿ, ತನ್ನ ಮುಂಗಾಲುಗಳ ಮೇಲೆ ಭಾರ ಸರಿಸಿ ತಲೆ ಇಳಿಸುತ್ತದೆ. ಆ ಸನ್ನೆ ಸ್ಪಷ್ಟ. "ನೀನು ನನ್ನ ಬಳಿಗೆ ಬಂದೆ ಏಕೆಂದರೆ ನಾನು ನಡೆದು ಬರಬಹುದಾದಷ್ಟು ಹತ್ತಿರದ ಮುಂದಿನ ಸಾಗರ-ವಂಶ ವಾರಸುದಾರೆ. ನೀನು ಗಡಿಯಲ್ಲಿ ನಿಲ್ಲಲಿಲ್ಲ. ನೀನು ಮುಂಬೈಯಲ್ಲಿ ನನ್ನ ಅಣ್ಣನನ್ನು ಭೇಟಿಯಾಗಲಿಲ್ಲ. ನೀನು ಇಲ್ಲಿಗೆ ಬಂದೆ. ನಾನು ಒಪ್ಪುತ್ತೇನೆ." ಕಾಡುಕೋಣ ಮಂಡಿಯೂರುತ್ತದೆ. ಬುಲ್‌ಬುಲ್ ಎಚ್ಚರಗೊಂಡು ಹರಿಯಾಲ್ ಹಾಡಿದ ಅದೇ ಗಂಟೆಯ ಹಾಡನ್ನು ಹಾಡುತ್ತದೆ. ಶಿವಗತಿ ತನ್ನ ಬೆನ್ನ ನಡುವೆ ಹೊಸ ಹಚ್ಚೆ ರೂಪುಗೊಳ್ಳುವುದನ್ನು ಅನುಭವಿಸುತ್ತಾಳೆ — ಕಾಡುಕೋಣದ ಗೊರಸಿನ ಗುರುತು, ಮೊದಲ ಗೆರೆ. ಅವಳಿಗೆ ಈಗ ಎರಡು ಪ್ರಾಣಿಗಳು. ಪರ್ವತ ಎರಡನ್ನೂ ಒಪ್ಪುತ್ತದೆ.

ಮುಂಜಾವಿನ ಹೊತ್ತಿಗೆ ಸುದ್ದಿ ಹರಡುತ್ತದೆ. ಇಪ್ಪತ್ತೇಳು ಕೋಟೆಗಳು ಕಾಡುಕೋಣದ ಆಗಮನವನ್ನು ಆಶೀರ್ವಾದ ಎಂದು ಕರೆಯುತ್ತವೆ. ಇಪ್ಪತ್ತು ಅದನ್ನು ಶಾಪ ಎಂದು ಕರೆಯುತ್ತವೆ. ವನರಕ್ಷನ ಕಾನೂನು ತಂಡ ವಾದಿಸುತ್ತದೆ: ಕಾಡುಕೋಣ ನಡೆದದ್ದೇ ಸಾಗರದೇವ ವಿಫಲನಾದದ್ದರಿಂದ. ಕರಾವಳಿಯಲ್ಲದ ವಾರಸುದಾರೆಗೆ ನಡೆಯುವ ಬಂಧ ಸ್ಥಳಾಂತರಗೊಂಡ ಬಂಧ, ಕೊಡಲ್ಪಟ್ಟ ಬಂಧವಲ್ಲ. ಶಿವಗತಿ ಕದಿಯುತ್ತಿದ್ದಾಳೆ. ಶಿವಗತಿ ವರದಿ ಓದುತ್ತಾಳೆ. ಅವಳು ಯೋಚಿಸುತ್ತಾಳೆ: ಅವನು ಸಂಪೂರ್ಣ ತಪ್ಪಿಲ್ಲ. ಬಂಧ ನನ್ನದು ಏಕೆಂದರೆ ರಕ್ತ-ಹಕ್ಕಿನ ಬೇರೆ ಯಾರೂ ಸಾಕಷ್ಟು ಹತ್ತಿರವಿರಲಿಲ್ಲ. ಅದು ತೆಳು ಹಕ್ಕು. ಆದರೆ ಅದೇ ನನ್ನ ಬಳಿಯಿರುವ ಏಕೈಕ ಹಕ್ಕು.

ಎಣಿಕೆ ಮತ್ತೆ ವನರಕ್ಷನೆಡೆಗೆ ಜಾರಲಾರಂಭಿಸುತ್ತದೆ. ಭಾವುಸಾಹೇಬ್ ಅವಳಿಗೆ ಹೇಳುತ್ತಾನೆ: "ನೀನು ಚುರುಕಾದ ಮಧ್ಯಸ್ಥಳಾಗಿ ಉಳಿಯಲಾಗದು. ಈಗ ಪ್ರಶ್ನೆಗಳು ಎರಡೇ. ನೀನು ಬದ್ಧಳಾಗಬೇಕು. ಒಂದು ಕೋಟೆಯನ್ನು ಆರಿಸು. ಸ್ನೇಹಿಯಲ್ಲ, ಶತ್ರುವಲ್ಲ. ಮೌನವಾದದ್ದು. ಯಾರೂ ಎಣಿಸದ ನಲವತ್ತಾರನೆಯ ಕೋಟೆ. ತಾನಾಜಿಯ ಕೋಟೆ. ಒಬ್ಬಂಟಿಯಾಗಿ ಹೋಗು. ಗೋಡೆ ಏರು. ರಸ್ತೆ ಬಳಸಬೇಡ. ಮೇಲೆ ವಾನರ ಎತ್ತಬೇಡ." ಅವಳು ಉತ್ತರಿಸುವುದಿಲ್ಲ. ಅವಳಿಗೆ ಗೊತ್ತು. ತನಗೆ ಇನ್ನೂ ಹೆಸರೇ ಗೊತ್ತಿಲ್ಲದ ಉತ್ತರಕ್ಕಾಗಿ ಅವಳು ಇಡೀ ಜೀವಮಾನ ತರಬೇತಿ ಪಡೆದಿದ್ದಾಳೆ.

ನಲವತ್ತಾರನೆಯ ಕೋಟೆ. ಮುಂಜಾವಿನ ಮೊದಲ ಬೂದು ಹೊತ್ತು. ಪೂರ್ವ ಗೋಡೆ ಅತ್ಯಂತ ಕಡಿದಾದದ್ದು — ಐವತ್ತು-ಮೀಟರ್ ನೇತಾಡುವ ಬಂಡೆಯ ಮೇಲೆ ಮೂವತ್ತು ಮೀಟರ್ ಒಣ-ಜೋಡಣೆಯ ಸಹ್ಯಾದ್ರಿ ಬಸಾಲ್ಟ್. ಮೆಟ್ಟಿಲಿಲ್ಲ. ಹಗ್ಗವಿಲ್ಲ. ಈ ಬದಿ ಏರಲಾಗದಂತೆ ಕೋಟೆಯನ್ನು ಕಟ್ಟಲಾಗಿತ್ತು. ಶಿವಗತಿ ನಾಲ್ಕೂ ಮುಕ್ಕಾಲಕ್ಕೆ ಬಂಡೆಯ ಬುಡದಲ್ಲಿದ್ದಾಳೆ. ಕವಚವಿಲ್ಲ. ಶ್ವೇತ ಪ್ರತಾಪಗಡದಲ್ಲಿ — ಅಳಿಲು ತೋರಿಸಲಲ್ಲ, ಇರಲು. ಅವಳು ಏರಲಾರಂಭಿಸುತ್ತಾಳೆ. ಅಳಿಲು-ಬಂಧ ರೆಕ್ಕೆ ಕೊಡುವ ಮಾಯೆಯಲ್ಲ. ಅದು ಒಂದು ಸಮತೋಲನ — ದೈತ್ಯ ಅಳಿಲು ತೊಗಟೆಯ ಘರ್ಷಣೆಯನ್ನು ತನ್ನ ದೇಹ ತೂಕದಿಂದ ಓದುವ ರೀತಿ. ಅವಳ ಬೆರಳುಗಳು ಅತ್ಯಂತ ಸಣ್ಣ ಸಂದಿಗಳನ್ನು ಕಂಡುಕೊಳ್ಳುತ್ತವೆ. ಒಮ್ಮೆ ಅವಳು ಜಾರುತ್ತಾಳೆ. ನಾಲ್ಕು-ಮಿಲಿಮೀಟರ್ ಅಂಚಿನ ಮೇಲೆ ಎಡಗೈಯ ಮೂರು ಬೆರಳುಗಳಿಂದ ಆ ಜಾರುವಿಕೆಯನ್ನು ಹಿಡಿಯುತ್ತಾಳೆ. ಅವಳು ಬೀಳುವುದಿಲ್ಲ. ಅವಳು ಐದೂ ನಲವತ್ತಕ್ಕೆ ಗೋಡೆಯ ಮೇಲೆ ತಲುಪುತ್ತಾಳೆ.

ತಾನಾಜಿ-ದ-ಸೆಕೆಂಡ್ ಆಜ್ಞಾ ಕೋಣೆಯಲ್ಲಿ ಚಹಾ ಕುಡಿಯುತ್ತಿದ್ದಾನೆ. ಅವಳು ಬಾಗಿಲು ತಟ್ಟುತ್ತಾಳೆ. ಒಳಗೆ ಬಂದು, ವಾನರವನ್ನು ಬೆನ್ನಿನಿಂದ ಬಿಚ್ಚಿ ಬಾಗಿಲಿಂದ ಮೂರು ಹೆಜ್ಜೆ ದೂರ, ತನ್ನ ಕೈಗೆಟುಕದಂತೆ ಗೋಡೆಗೆ ಒರಗಿಸುತ್ತಾಳೆ. ಕೈಗಳನ್ನು ತೆರೆದು ಕೋಣೆಯ ನಡುವೆ ನಿಲ್ಲುತ್ತಾಳೆ. "ನಾನು ಇಲ್ಲಿದ್ದೇನೆ. ನಾನು ರಸ್ತೆಯಲ್ಲಿ ಇಳಿದು ಬರಲಿಲ್ಲ, ಗೋಡೆ ಏರಿ ಬಂದೆ. ನನ್ನ ತಂದೆ ಯಾರೆಂದು ಅಲ್ಲ, ನಾನು ಏನು ಮಾಡಿದೆ ಎಂದು ನನ್ನನ್ನು ಆರಿಸು." ತಾನಾಜಿ ಅವಳ ಕೈಗಳನ್ನು ನೋಡುತ್ತಾನೆ — ನಾಲ್ಕು-ಮಿಲಿಮೀಟರ್ ಗಾಯಗಳು. ಅವಳ ಬೆನ್ನಿನ ಹೊಸ ಕಾಡುಕೋಣದ ಗೊರಸನ್ನು ನೋಡುತ್ತಾನೆ. ಅವನು ಚಹಾ ಲೋಟ ಕೆಳಗಿಟ್ಟು, ಎದ್ದು, ಉತ್ತರಾಧಿಕಾರದಲ್ಲಿ ಕೋಟೆದಾರರು ಬಳಸುವ ಅರ್ಧ-ಮಂಡಿಯೂರುವಿಕೆ ಮಾಡುತ್ತಾನೆ. "ನಾನು ಗೋಡೆ ಏರುವವನನ್ನು ಹಿಂಬಾಲಿಸುತ್ತೇನೆ. ಬಾಗಿಲನ್ನು ಬಳುವಳಿಯಾಗಿ ಪಡೆಯುವವನನ್ನಲ್ಲ. ನಲವತ್ತಾರನೆಯ ಕೋಟೆ ನಿನ್ನದು." ಅವನು ವಾನರವನ್ನು ಎತ್ತಿ ಅವಳಿಗೆ ಮರಳಿ ಕೊಡುತ್ತಾನೆ. "ಮುಂದಿನ ಸಂಭಾಷಣೆಯಲ್ಲಿ ನಿನಗೆ ಇದು ಬೇಕು, ಈ ಸಂಭಾಷಣೆಯಲ್ಲಿ ಅಲ್ಲ." ಅವನು ಅವಳಿಗೆ ಚಹಾ ಬಗ್ಗಿಸುತ್ತಾನೆ.

ಆ ಸಂಜೆಯ ಹೊತ್ತಿಗೆ ಇನ್ನೂ ಮೂರು ಕೋಟೆಗಳು ಶಿವಗತಿಯ ಪಕ್ಷಕ್ಕೆ ಬರುತ್ತವೆ. ಎಣಿಕೆ ಇಪ್ಪತ್ತೇಳು-ಇಪ್ಪತ್ತಕ್ಕೆ ನೆಲೆಗೊಳ್ಳುತ್ತದೆ. ಶಿವಗತಿ ಒಂದೇ ದೇಹದ ಮೇಲೆ ಎರಡು ಪ್ರಾಣಿ-ಗುರುತುಗಳನ್ನು ಹೊರುವ ಗಾಥೆಯ ಮೊದಲ ವೀರೆ. ನಂತರ ವನರಕ್ಷನ ನೆರಳು-ಜಾಲ ಒಂದು ಕಾವಲುಠಾಣೆಗೆ ಬೆಂಕಿ ಹಚ್ಚಲು ಯತ್ನಿಸುತ್ತದೆ, ವಿಫಲವಾಗುತ್ತದೆ. ಆದರೆ ಮರುದಿನ, ನಿಜವಾದ ಚಿಹ್ನೆ ಬರುತ್ತದೆ: ಒಂದು ಸಹ್ಯಾದ್ರಿ ಮರದ ತುದಿಯಲ್ಲಿ ಸುಟ್ಟ ಅಳಿಲು-ಗೂಡು, ಕೆಳಗಿನ ಮರ ಮುಟ್ಟದೆ. ಗಾಳಿಯಲ್ಲಿ ಹದಿನೇಳು ಮೀಟರ್ ಎತ್ತರದಲ್ಲಿ ತೇಲುತ್ತಿರುವ ಬೂದಿ. ಆ ಗೂಡಿನಲ್ಲಿ ವಾಸಿಸಿದ ಅಳಿಲು ಹೋಗಿದೆ. ಶವವಿಲ್ಲ. ಜಾಡಿಲ್ಲ. ಶಿವಗತಿ ಮರ ಏರಿ ಬೂದಿ ಮುಟ್ಟುತ್ತಾಳೆ. ಅದು ಕೋಣೆಯ ಉಷ್ಣತೆಯಲ್ಲಿದೆ. ಅದರಲ್ಲಿ ಶಾಖವಿಲ್ಲ.

ಅದರ ಅರ್ಥ ಅವಳಿಗೆ ಗೊತ್ತು. ಶೂನ್ಯ-ಮಾವ್ ಅವಳ ಒಂದು ಅಳಿಲನ್ನು ತೆಗೆದುಕೊಂಡಿದೆ. ಹಿಂಸೆಯಿಂದಲ್ಲ. ನೆನಪಿನಿಂದ ತೆಗೆಯುವ ಮೂಲಕ. ಅಳಿಲಿನ ಉಪಸ್ಥಿತಿಯನ್ನು ಯಾವುದೂ ಎಂದೂ ಇರಲೇ ಇಲ್ಲದಂತೆ ತೆಗೆದಾಗ ಉಳಿಯುವುದೇ ಆ ಸುಟ್ಟ ಗುರುತು. ಅವಳು ಯಾರನ್ನೂ ಕೊಲ್ಲುವುದಿಲ್ಲ. ಕರ್ನಾಟಕದ ನಿಯಮ ಸಹ್ಯಾದ್ರಿಯನ್ನು ತಲುಪಿದೆ. ತಾನಾಜಿ ಆ ಮರದ ಬುಡದಲ್ಲಿ ಒಂದು ಸಣ್ಣ ಗುಡಿ ಕಟ್ಟುತ್ತಾನೆ — ಸಹ್ಯಾದ್ರಿ ಕಲ್ಲು, ಒಂದು ಕೆತ್ತಿದ ಅಳಿಲು-ಬಾಲ. ಅದು ಋತುವಿನೊಳಗೆ ಯಾತ್ರಾಸ್ಥಳವಾಗುತ್ತದೆ.

ಏಳನೆಯ ವಾರದ ಸಂಜೆ, ಹಳದಿ-ಪಾದದ ಹಸಿರು ಪಾರಿವಾಳ ಪ್ರತಾಪಗಡಕ್ಕೆ ಎರಡು ಮಡಚಿದ ಚೀಟಿಗಳನ್ನು ಹೊತ್ತು ಬರುತ್ತದೆ. ಮೊದಲನೆಯದು ಈಗ ಕೇರಳದಲ್ಲಿ ೧೦೯ನೆಯ ಪಟ್ಟು ಕಲಿಯುತ್ತಿರುವ ಕೃಷ್ಣದೇವರಾಯ-ಭ್ರಾತನಿಂದ: ಸೋದರಸಂಬಂಧಿ, ನೀನು ಹೊರುವುದಕ್ಕೆ ಯಾವ ನಿಯಮಗಳೂ ಇಲ್ಲ. ಅವನ್ನು ಸೃಷ್ಟಿಸು. ಬಾಯಿ ತೆರೆದಿದೆ. ಪರಿಷತ್ ಕೂರಬೇಕು. ಎರಡನೆಯದು ಗೋವಾದ ಫೆರೇರಾ-ದ-ಸಂಗಮ್‌ನಿಂದ: ಶಿವಗತಿ — ಸಾಗರ-ವಂಶ ಉತ್ತರಕ್ಕೆ ವಲಸೆ ಬರುತ್ತಿದೆ. ನೀನು ಕಾಡುಕೋಣವನ್ನು ಒಪ್ಪಿದರೆ, ನನ್ನನ್ನು ನಿನ್ನ ಒಕ್ಕೂಟ ಪಾಲುದಾರನಾಗಿ ಒಪ್ಪುತ್ತೀಯೆ — ಸಾಮಂತನಲ್ಲ, ಪಾಲುದಾರ. ಶಿವಗತಿ ಎರಡನೆಯದನ್ನು ಎರಡು ಬಾರಿ ಓದುತ್ತಾಳೆ. ಅವಳು ಅದೇ ಸಂಜೆ ಫೆರೇರಾನ ಸ್ವಂತ ಕಾಗದದ ಹಿಂಭಾಗದಲ್ಲಿ ಉತ್ತರಿಸುತ್ತಾಳೆ: ಹೌದು. ಪಾಲುದಾರ. ಪರ್ವತಗಳು ಮತ್ತು ಕರಾವಳಿ ಒಂದೇ ಬಂಧವಾಗಿತ್ತು. ನಮಗೆ ಸಾಧ್ಯವಾಗದವರೆಗೆ ನಾವು ಆ ಬಂಧವನ್ನು ಒಟ್ಟಿಗೆ ಹಿಡಿದಿಡುತ್ತೇವೆ.

ಸಂಜೆ ಸೂರ್ಯ ಮುಳುಗುತ್ತಿರುವಾಗ ಶಿವಗತಿ ಸಹ್ಯಾದ್ರಿ ಶಿಖರದ ಅತ್ಯುನ್ನತ ಕಾವಲುಗೋಪುರದ ಮೇಲೆ ನಿಲ್ಲುತ್ತಾಳೆ. ಅವಳ ಪಕ್ಕದಲ್ಲಿ ತಾನಾಜಿ. ಶ್ವೇತ ಕೂರುತ್ತಿದ್ದ ಜಾಗದಲ್ಲಿ ಒಂದು ಎಳೆಯ ಅಳಿಲು. ಏಳು ಮಹಡಿ ಕೆಳಗೆ ಪರ್ವತ ಹುಲ್ಲುಗಾವಲಿನಲ್ಲಿ ಆರು-ಮೀಟರ್ ಗೋವಾ ಕಾಡುಕೋಣ ಮೇಯುತ್ತಿದೆ. ನಲವತ್ತೇಳು ಕೋಟೆಗಳು ಈಗ ಅವಳಿಗೆ ಇಪ್ಪತ್ತೇಳು, ಅವಳ ವಿರುದ್ಧ ಇಪ್ಪತ್ತು. ಮುಂದಿನ ಸುತ್ತು ಕಠಿಣ ಎಂದು ಅವಳಿಗೆ ಗೊತ್ತು. ತನ್ನ ಅಣ್ಣ ಇನ್ನೂ ಮುಗಿಸಿಲ್ಲ ಎಂದು ಅವಳಿಗೆ ಗೊತ್ತು. ಆದರೆ ಅವಳಿಗೆ ಗದೆ ಎಂದೂ ಉತ್ತರವಾಗಿರಲಿಲ್ಲ ಎಂದೂ ಈಗ ಗೊತ್ತು — ಗೋಡೆ ಏರುವುದು ಉತ್ತರ. ಅವಳು ವಾನರದ ಪಟ್ಟಿಯನ್ನು ಬಿಗಿಗೊಳಿಸಿ, ಸದ್ಯಕ್ಕೆ ತನ್ನೊಂದಿಗೆ ಒಪ್ಪಿರುವ ಪರ್ವತದ ಮೇಲೆ ಮುಸ್ಸಂಜೆ ಇಳಿಯುವುದನ್ನು ನೋಡುತ್ತಾಳೆ.

The Forty-Seven Forts — A Film in Two Halves
═══ FIRST HALF ═══ OPENING — THE SAHYADRI MORNING Pratapgad. Five-thirty a.m. The mountain is breathing — the upward draft of the Konkan plateau-edge meeting the lateral wind of the ridge. The clouds beneath the fort move in a different direction than the clouds above. The woman who stands on the parapet has noticed this every morning of her life and considers it a private privilege. Her name is Shivagati. She is thirty-one. She is wearing a sleeveless cotton tunic over leggings of brown leather, a wide leather belt, and the iron staff Vanara strapped diagonally across her back. The staff was forged from a single piece of Sahyadri rock-iron by her grandfather’s smith. The iron came from a meteorite that fell in 1893 on this same fort’s western slope. She is running through the morning fort-net — the encrypted radio relay that connects the forty-seven forts of her federation. The Pratapgad commander reports clear weather. The Raigad commander reports a market dispute. The Sinhagad commander reports nothing because the Sinhagad commander reports nothing every morning — this is a famous joke among the federation. The Lohagad commander reports a request for a hearing on something he will not name. She acknowledges them in turn. She does not stop running. Her squirrel — the Indian Giant Squirrel of the Sahyadri, slate-grey and rust-bellied, the bond-animal of her line — is on her shoulder. He is named Shvet, which means “white,” which is the colour his coat is not. Her grandfather named him. The Yellow-footed Green Pigeon — Hariyal — flies over the parapet at six exactly, the way Hariyal flies over the parapet every morning at six exactly. The pigeon’s flight is the kingdom’s clock. Shivagati is happy in the way that people who have done a difficult thing well are happy. The forty-seven forts are federated. The squirrel-bond is intact. The brother she defeated in spring is silent. The mountain agrees with her. The mountain has been wrong before. This is one of those mornings. THE PETITION Nine a.m., same day, two hundred kilometres south-east. Pune. The Maharashtra Heritage Court, civil bench. A lawyer in a black robe over a charcoal-grey suit walks up the courthouse steps carrying a binder one hundred and thirty-two pages thick. His name is Adv. Kelkar. His client’s name is Vanaraksha. The binder is the cleanest primogeniture petition the post-1947 court has ever received. Adv. Kelkar files it at 9:47. The clerk timestamps it. The clerk is a man named Mhatre who has worked at the court for thirty-one years and has never seen a petition that thorough in a succession case. The petition cites: the Bloodline-E elder law (1664), the Maharashtra Succession Act (1957), the Heritage Court Bloodline Jurisdiction Statute (1989), and seven sub-clauses across four state-government memoranda. It cites four expert affidavits. It cites the head of the University of Pune Bloodline-E succession-law chair. It is correct. The petition’s relief sought is one sentence: that the Maharashtra Heritage Court issue an order recognising Vanaraksha, son of [the same parents] and elder of two children, as the rightful succession-holder of the Sahyadri federation, and directing the respondent Shivagati to withdraw her federation administration in favour of regent-shared governance under the petitioner’s line. By ten a.m. the news is in every fort-commander’s morning briefing. By eleven a.m. it is in Shivagati’s. She reads it twice. She does not curse. She does not throw anything. She walks to the parapet and watches the clouds move below the fort and above the fort in different directions. She says aloud, to Shvet on her shoulder: "He is not coming to fight. He is coming to file. I have never trained for this." Shvet does not have an answer. THE FORTS BEGIN TO CHOOSE By the third day three forts have publicly endorsed the petition. By the seventh day eight have. By the second week fourteen forts have signed nominal allegiance to Vanaraksha’s succession claim. By the end of the third week the count is twenty-four. Twenty-four of the forty-seven forts have decided, on paper, that they prefer the predictability of inheritance to the messiness of federation. Shivagati holds her remaining fourteen. The nine that are uncommitted refuse to talk to her or to her brother. She tours her remaining forts personally. She climbs each one because climbing is faster than driving the ridge-roads. The squirrel-bond gives her impossible balance — she can move along ledges no climbing-rated mountaineer would attempt without three pieces of protection. She is the only person in the Sahyadri who can reach Pratapgad and Raigad and Sinhagad in a single day on foot, and she does this twice in the first month after the petition is filed. At every fort she asks the commander: "What is making you stay?" The answers are not flattering. Several commanders say I have not yet been bought. One says I would have left except I owe you a personal debt from the Ch 1 spring battle. Most say I do not love Vanaraksha and I do not yet trust the courts. One commander — her cousin Bhausaheb, at Sinhagad — says Cousin, the petition is technically valid. I will hold this fort for you until you give me a reason to hold it that does not rely on the petition being wrong. She does not have that reason yet. She knows it. He knows it. He stays anyway. THE LOSS — MURUD-JANJIRA The fourth week. The Murud-Janjira coastal fort — the island fortress on the Arabian Sea, the one Shivagati trusted because its commander had served her father — defects to Vanaraksha overnight. The commander’s name is Dattaji Mohite. His defection is filed at the Heritage Court at 8:15 a.m. on a Tuesday. By 8:30 he has issued a public statement endorsing Vanaraksha’s claim. By 9:00 he has barred Shivagati’s supply boats from the fort’s harbour. By noon he has done the thing she did not see coming. He has seized the small island-cemetery on the fort’s landward side — the cemetery where Shivagati’s elder cousin Bhausaheb-the-First is buried, who died at sixteen at the same Ch 1 spring battle, and whose grave Shivagati has visited every full moon for fifteen years. Dattaji Mohite has declared the cemetery property of the heritage estate pending resolution of the succession claim and posted guards. The full moon arrives the following Friday. For the first time in fifteen years, Shivagati does not visit her cousin’s grave. She cannot. The supply boats are barred. The cemetery is guarded. To force the issue would mean armed conflict, which would be exactly what the petition needs to be granted on emergency grounds. She sits on Pratapgad’s parapet that Friday night. Shvet is on her shoulder. Hariyal sits on the railing. She has not slept. She is not crying. She says, quietly: "He has found the place where the law and the love are the same place. I cannot fight him there. He has not trained for the wall." She does not yet know what she means by the last sentence. It will arrive later. ═══ INTERVAL ═══ The thirty-first night after the petition was filed. Three nights after, somewhere unknown to Shivagati, Sagaradeva has died in Mormugao Bay holding a cyclone. She is at her lowest outpost — not a fort, an old goat-shed converted into a watch-post above the Konkan plateau-edge. She has come here because she wanted a place no fort-commander would interrupt her at. She has been sitting on the rim of the cliff for two hours. The moon is not yet up. The Konkan forest below is black under black sky. She sees movement on the forest trail far below. A massive shape. Slow. Walking through canopy gaps as if the canopy did not exist. The shape is the size of an elephant but the wrong silhouette — lower at the shoulder, more horizontal in the back. A bovine. A six-metre wild gaur. Hariyal sings. The pigeon does not sing at night. She has never heard the pigeon sing at night. The sound is the same as the morning sound but lower, longer. She has read about this in her grandfather’s commentary on the bond-laws: Hariyal sings at night only when a Bloodline-E sea-bond is approaching the mountain. The last recorded instance was 1873. She stands. She begins the descent. She does not climb — descending is slower — she takes the goat-trail. She is at the canopy edge in twenty minutes. The gaur is on the trail there, motionless, head lowered. The gaur is salt-stained. Her hide carries the sea, not in moisture but in texture — the way Konkan wood that has been used for fifty years to build fishing boats carries the sea in its grain. The black-crested bulbul of Goa is on her back, sleeping. Shivagati does not approach further. She kneels at the trail-edge. "You walked here." The gaur lifts her head. She is six metres long from shoulder to rump. Her horns are old and pitted. Her eyes are the colour of monsoon stone. She looks at Shivagati for a long, exact amount of time. "My uncle is dead." The gaur does not nod the way humans nod. The gaur shifts weight onto her front legs and lowers her head. The gesture is unmistakable. "You came to me because I am the next Bloodline-E heir close enough to walk to. You did not stop at the border. You did not visit my brother in Mumbai. You came here." Shvet on her shoulder is awake and is not afraid. The squirrel bond does not contest the gaur bond. They are different bonds. They can coexist, because they have to. "I accept." The gaur kneels. The bulbul wakes and sings the same song Hariyal sang from the parapet. The two birds’ songs are not the same song, but they are the same hour’s song. Shivagati feels it the way Sagaradeva once felt his original bond — not in her head but in her back, mid-back, where the new tattoo begins to form. The hoofprint, first stroke. The gaur’s left forehoof. She turns her shoulder; the squirrel-tail mark on her right shoulder pulses once in acknowledgement. She has two animals now. The mountain agrees with both. ═══ SECOND HALF ═══ THE BLESSING AND THE CURSE Word spreads through the fort-net by dawn. Twenty-seven of the forty-seven forts call the gaur’s arrival a blessing. Twenty call it a curse. The Blessing-faction’s reasoning: the Sagaravamsha-line itself chose her. The gaur did not go to Vanaraksha. The bond’s own answer is the answer. Four forts that had been blood-leaning switch on this argument within the first week. The Curse-faction’s reasoning, supplied within forty-eight hours by Adv. Kelkar’s legal team and reinforced by the University of Pune chair: the bond walked because Sagaradeva failed. A bond that walks to a non-coastal heir is a displaced bond, not a given bond. Shivagati is technically stealing what is not hers. The squirrel-bond remains valid; the gaur-bond is theft. Vanaraksha publishes a scholar’s report — thirty-four pages, footnoted, citing bond-law precedent back to 1664 — arguing the gaur should be returned to Goa under federation custody and a new bonding-rite held with a properly coastal Bloodline-E heir. The scholar’s report names a candidate for the rite: Simhavikrama of Gujarat. Shivagati reads the scholar’s report. She thinks: he is not entirely wrong. The bond did walk because Sagaradeva failed. The bond is mine because no one else with bloodline-claim was close enough. That is a thin claim. It is the only claim I have. The federation count holds at 27-20 for two weeks. Then it begins to slide back toward Vanaraksha. Three forts publicly question their alignment. The fort-net begins to feel like a meeting nobody wants to attend. THE PLAN — STORMING TANAJI’S FORT Bhausaheb — her cousin at Sinhagad — visits her at Pratapgad. He is older than her by twelve years. He is, in his own way, the closest she has to a brother who is not Vanaraksha. "You cannot keep being the agile mediator," he says. "The mountain needed a mediator when there were forty-seven equal questions. Now the questions are two. You have to commit." "To what." "Pick one fort. Not a friendly one. Not a hostile one. A silent one. The forty-sixth fort. The one nobody has counted in their arithmetic." "Tanaji’s." "Tanaji’s." The forty-sixth fort is a small mid-ridge garrison commanded by a fifty-year-old man whose family has held it for nine generations. The commander’s name is Tanaji-the-Second — his great-great-great-grandfather’s name was Tanaji, and the name has skipped six generations and returned. He has been on every council vote without speaking. He is the saga’s most famous neutral. He does not love Shivagati. He does not love Vanaraksha. He has never said why. Shivagati nods. "Go alone," Bhausaheb says. "I know." "Climb the wall. Do not use the road." "I know." "Do not raise Vanara at the top." She does not answer this. She knows. She has been training her whole life for the answer she does not yet have a name for. THE WALL-CLIMB The forty-sixth fort. The grey hour before dawn. The fort is on a ridge-top that drops away on three sides to scree and acacia. The eastern wall is the steepest — thirty metres of dry-laid Sahyadri basalt over a fifty-metre overhanging cliff. No stair. No rope-line. The fort was built for this side to be unclimbable. Shivagati is at the base of the cliff at four-thirty. She is wearing the same sleeveless tunic and leather leggings, no armour, Vanara strapped to her back. Shvet is at Pratapgad — she has left him because the squirrel is not for showing, the squirrel is for being. She has eight men with her at the base. She tells them to wait. "If I am not on the parapet by dawn, climb the road." She begins the climb at four-forty. The cliff is fifty metres of overhanging basalt, then thirty metres of dry-laid fort wall. The squirrel-bond is not a magic that gives her wings. The bond is a balance — the way the Indian Giant Squirrel reads the friction of bark with its body weight, the way it knows which branch will hold and which will not. She has the same reading. She moves up the cliff in a rhythm she has practiced for fifteen years and never used in the open. Her fingers find the smallest seams. Her toes find rugosities of rock that are smaller than a fingernail. Once, halfway up the cliff, she slips. She catches the slip with three fingers of her left hand on a four-millimetre lip of basalt. She does not fall. She continues. She reaches the fort wall at five-thirty. The wall is dry-laid stone — no mortar — and the gaps between stones are wider than the cliff seams. She climbs the wall faster. She reaches the parapet at five-forty. She steps over the parapet, onto the rampart, and walks to the command-room door. Tanaji-the-Second is in the command room. He has been awake for an hour because he sleeps four hours a night. He is sitting at his desk drinking tea. He hears the parapet steps. He does not stand up. He waits. She knocks. He says "Come in." She comes in. She unstraps Vanara from her back and leans it carefully against the inside wall, three steps from the door, well out of her reach. She stands in the middle of the room with her hands open at her sides. She says: "I am here. I have come up the wall, not down the road. Choose me by what I did, not by who my father was." Tanaji-the-Second studies her for a long, exact amount of time. He looks at her hands — the four-millimetre cuts and abrasions across the fingertips. He looks at her back, where the new gaur-hoofprint can be seen at the top edge of the tunic’s shoulder cut. He looks at the squirrel-tail mark on her right shoulder. He looks at her face. He puts down his teacup. He stands. He walks around the desk. He kneels — not all the way to the floor, but the half-kneel of recognition that fort-commanders use in formal succession. "I follow the one who climbs the wall. Not the one who inherits the gate. The forty-sixth fort is yours." He stands. He picks up Vanara from where she leaned it. He hands it back to her. She takes it. He says: "You will need this in the next conversation, not this one." He pours her tea. THE CONVERSION By that evening three more forts have moved to Shivagati’s side: Lohagad, Tikona, and Visapur. Their commanders all served alongside Tanaji-the-Second in the same Ch 1 spring engagement; they have followed his example before. Within a fortnight the federation count has settled at 27-20 in Shivagati’s favour, with three independents and two permanent dissidents. The Murud-Janjira coastal fort remains under Vanaraksha’s flag. The cemetery remains guarded. The squirrel-foreleg-first-stroke completes on Shivagati’s shoulder. The gaur-hoofprint at the top of her back stabilizes — a single full hoofprint, the gaur-bond at Stage I, marking the start of a new ladder she will not finish. She is the first hero of the saga to carry two animal-marks on the same body. THE BURNT NEST Two weeks after Tanaji-the-Second’s conversion. Vanaraksha’s shadow-network attempts a counter-move: a saboteur is paid to set a fire at one of Shivagati’s high observation posts during the dry pre-monsoon. The fire does not take — the wind is wrong, the kerosene leaks — and the saboteur is caught by a Lohagad watchman and turned over to the local magistrate. The next morning, a Lohagad fort-runner brings Shivagati the actual sign. A Sahyadri ironwood tree, four kilometres from the failed-fire post, has a burnt squirrel-nest at the top of its canopy. The tree below is untouched. The grass beneath has no scorch. There is no fire-line, no smoke-stain on the bark, no carbonised approach-path. The carbonisation is upper-canopy-only — the nest itself, and a one-metre radius of ash around it, suspended in mid-air seventeen metres above an untouched forest floor. The Indian Giant Squirrel that lived in the nest is gone. No body. No feathers. No claws. No drag-marks. No predator-prints in the grass below. Shivagati climbs the tree. She does this herself because the climb is short and because she needs to read what the sign is saying through the bond. At the top, she touches the ash. The ash is room-temperature. There is no heat in it. She knows what it means. The squirrel-bond is partly hers, partly the species’. The Void Maw has taken one of her squirrels. Not by violence. By removal from memory — the way the Karnataka Third Herald appears with a wrong-direction shadow, the way Goa’s Second Herald walks out of the surf in clothes from a century that no longer holds him. The Maw has read the Sahyadri and has reached up and erased one squirrel from the record. The carbonisation is what is left when the squirrel’s presence is removed without anything ever having been there to remove. She does not kill anyone. The Karnataka rule has reached the Sahyadri. She marks the spot. She sends a runner for Tanaji-the-Second. By the end of the week Tanaji-the-Second has built a small shrine at the base of the ironwood tree. Sahyadri rock, packed clay, a single carved squirrel-tail in low relief. The shrine becomes a pilgrimage site within the season. People come from Pune and Mumbai. The Heritage Court takes notice. The petition continues. The shrine grows. THE BIRD-MESSAGE The Yellow-footed Green Pigeon arrives at Pratapgad on the evening of the seventh week. She is carrying two folded notes — the same pigeon, the same trip. The first note is from Krishnadevaraya-Bhrata, who is now in Kerala studying the 109th form under Old Parashurama. The handwriting is tight, economical: Cousin (and yes, I will call you that — the bond is my permission). I am studying. The bond is settling. Your gaur — Sagaradeva’s gaur — is now yours. There are no rules for what you carry. Make them. The mouth has opened. The Council must sit. — K-B. The second note is from Ferreira-de-Sangam in Goa. The handwriting is rounder, more deliberate: Shivagati — the Sagaravamsha-line is migrating north. I have your uncle’s second herald in my house. I have the Old Fishermen. I have the mesh-network. If you accept the gaur, you accept me as your federation partner — not vassal, partner. Sona Tendulkar sends her father’s blessing. Padre Cordeiro has resigned the consortium. The coast is waking up to itself. Write back if you mean it. — F. Shivagati reads both notes. She reads the second twice. She does not answer the first. She does not need to. She answers the second the same evening, with the same pigeon, on the back of Ferreira’s own paper: Yes. Partner. The mountains and the coast were one bond. We will hold the bond together until we cannot. — S. She gives the note to Hariyal. Hariyal flies south at dawn.
The Pilot’s Closing Image
Shivagati on the highest watchtower of the Sahyadri ridge at dusk. Tanaji-the-Second at her left elbow. A young Indian Giant Squirrel — the new one, the replacement, four months old — on her shoulder where Shvet used to sit. The six-metre Goan gaur grazing in a mountain meadow seven storeys below. The Yellow-footed Green Pigeon flying south with a folded note in her beak. The black-crested bulbul of Goa, still on the gaur’s back, sings once at sunset. The forty-seven forts are now twenty-seven for her, twenty against. She knows the next round will be harder. She knows her brother is not done. She knows the gaur came to her for a reason that has not yet been explained. She tightens the strap of Vanara across her back and watches the dusk settle on the mountain that has, for now, agreed with her.
