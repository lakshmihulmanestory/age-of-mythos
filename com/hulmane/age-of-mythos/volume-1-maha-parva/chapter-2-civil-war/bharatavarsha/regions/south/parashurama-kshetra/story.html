The 109th Form Public Lesson — A Film in Two Halves
═══ FIRST HALF ═══ OPENING — THE NEWSPAPER ADVERTISEMENT Kochi. The first Tuesday of April. Five-forty a.m. Parashurama is at the kalari’s northern entrance reading the morning’s Malayala Manorama. He has read the Manorama every dawn for thirty-three years; the paper is delivered to the kalari’s northern gatepost by an Idukki cycle-courier whose father had delivered it to Parashurama’s guru and whose grandfather had delivered it to his guru’s guru. The reading is a Malayalam-meditation; Parashurama does not speak during it; the kalari staff have been instructed for three decades not to interrupt. This morning he interrupts himself. He reads aloud, in plain Malayalam, in the slow administrative-elder voice he uses for student-discipline reviews: “Page eleven. Display advertisement. Three column-inches. Sea-Front Kalari, Kochi waterfront amphitheatre. Tuition Rs. 4,000 per month. Phased instruction in the One-Hundred-And-Ninth Form of Kalaripayattu. All qualified applicants admitted. Senior instructor: Sri Kala-Hasta Menon, formal kalari-trained at the Kollam Coiled-Cobra Lineage from 2008 to 2017. Daily classes 5:30 a.m. and 5:30 p.m. Inaugural class Monday morning the eighth of April.” He sets the paper down on the gatepost. He does not pick up his tea. He walks back through the kalari’s inner courtyard to the senior-instructor’s residence. He has not interrupted the morning paper-reading in thirty-three years. The kalari staff who see him walking back at five-forty-eight a.m. do not need to be told the routine has broken. He calls the kingdom-council’s religious-and-cultural-practice registry at six a.m. The registry’s officer-on-duty (a forty-two-year-old administrative officer named Indira Pillai) confirms that the Sea-Front Kalari’s registration was filed on the twenty-third of March under the standard kalari-charter; the application listed the school’s curriculum as “advanced kalaripayattu, all forms including unsealed teaching”; the registry’s standard review-period is thirty days; no objection was filed by any registered kalari during the public-comment window; the registration was approved on the twenty-second of April; the school’s formal opening is the inaugural class Monday morning at five-thirty. Parashurama says, in plain Malayalam, in the same slow voice: “Madam Pillai. The kalari-charter has admitted a 109-form school. The 109-form is sealed. The sealing is the kingdom’s tradition. The kingdom’s tradition has been amended by your registry’s thirty-day silence. The amendment will require a kingdom-council review. I will be at the council chambers at eleven this morning. Please notify the chief secretary that I am coming.” He hangs up. He sits at the residence’s desk. He looks at his own right hand. The hand has been holding the axe Parashu for forty years. The hand is fifty-nine years old. The skin on the back of the hand has the standard old-axe-bearer’s calluses-and-thickened-knuckles configuration; the veins are prominent; the joints are slightly enlarged at the second knuckle of the index finger. The hand is not the hand of a man who can perform the 109th form. The 109th form requires the body of a man under thirty-five. He has known this for the past nine months. He has not said it aloud. He does not say it aloud now. He picks up his tea. THE SEA-FRONT KALARI’S INAUGURAL CLASS Kochi waterfront amphitheatre. Monday morning the eighth of April. Five-thirty a.m. The amphitheatre is a converted municipal open-air auditorium — capacity twelve hundred, normally used for monsoon-season cultural-festival performances. It has a single concrete stage at the western end, raked seating around three sides, and a sea-facing eastern wall that opens onto the Vembanad Lake’s western shore. The acoustic-and-visual-line-of-sight properties make it well-suited to physical-performance instruction for groups up to four hundred. Four hundred and twenty-three students are present this morning. They have paid the Rs. 4,000 monthly tuition in advance. They are arrayed across the raked seating in rows of seventeen; the seating-allocation has been administered by an entry-protocol that requires each student to demonstrate, at the door, a 108-form Stage-Five sequence as proof of qualification. Twenty-eight applicants were turned away at the door this morning for insufficient 108-form competence. The Sea-Front Kalari does not admit absolute beginners; the 109th form is built on the 108-form base. Kala-Hasta is on the western stage. He is forty-seven. He is in plain Kollam-Coiled-Cobra dark-blue cotton, knee-length, tied with the standard kalari-instructor’s leather-cord at the waist. He carries no weapon. He has the urumi (the flexible-blade-whip-sword) coiled at his left wrist as a wrist-bangle; the urumi has been the Kollam-Coiled-Cobra cell’s standard side-implement since 1812. He was Maya-Sura’s star pupil in Chapter One — the senior coiled-cobra disciple of the Naga-Padma cell who had escaped the Ch 1 climax confrontation with the urumi-and-cobra-venom training intact. He has spent the intervening years not in hiding but in the Bangalore-and-Gulf kalari-diaspora, teaching the 108 forms while privately practising the 109th. He returned to Kerala in spring 2025. He registered the Sea-Front Kalari on the twenty-third of March. He addresses the four hundred and twenty-three at five-thirty-two a.m. He speaks in plain Malayalam. He says: “Welcome. I am Kala-Hasta Menon. I will teach the One-Hundred-And-Ninth Form of Kalaripayattu in seven phased classes across the next twenty-four weeks. The phasing is the difference between the form’s safety and its lethality. Each phase will be taught for three weeks; each phase must be completed before the next phase begins; no student will advance without my personal confirmation of phase-completion. The seven phases are: opening four movements; opening seven; first transition; full opening sequence; closing transitions; integration; private mastery. The first phase begins this morning. Stand. Find your row-position. We will begin with the standard kalari-breath. Breathe.” The four hundred and twenty-three stand. They take the standard kalari-breath. The class begins. The session lasts ninety minutes. By six-fifty-eight a.m. the four hundred and twenty-three have completed the opening four movements’ first introduction. The movements are slow, deliberately controlled, structured to allow the central nervous system to register the body-positions without the cardiovascular-stress that would activate the form’s lethal mechanisms. The students leave at seven a.m. tired but uninjured. The session has been the safest opening-class of any Kalaripayattu instruction recorded in the kingdom’s formal kalari-register. Kala-Hasta stays at the amphitheatre for two hours after the class. He reviews the student-roster. He has identified, from the entry-protocol’s 108-form demonstration, the twenty-three students whose 108-form mastery is at the level the seventh phase will require. He will personally tutor those twenty-three in the integration-and-private-mastery phases over the final six weeks of the curriculum. The other four hundred will complete phases one through five and will be invited to re-enrol in the autumn cohort for phases six and seven if they wish. He has, in other words, built a teaching system whose pedagogical thoughtfulness is the chapter’s most uncomfortable fact for Parashurama’s tradition. The 109-form is not being taught as an act of defiance. It is being taught as a discipline. ACHYUTAN AT THE NORTHERN GATE Friday. The second week of April. Four-fifty-five p.m. The kalari’s northern gate. Achyutan-the-Nephew is at the gate. He is thirty-seven. He is Parashurama’s elder sister’s son — Bloodline-A by his mother’s line, the same Bloodline-A branch that supplied Hampi-of-the-Bronze and Krishnaveni of AP and (through their long-shared maternal-line ancestor) Krishnadevaraya-Bhrata of Karnataka. He has been at Parashurama’s kalari since age twelve. He mastered all 108 forms by age thirty-three — the second-fastest in the kalari’s recorded history, second only to Parashurama himself. He is the kalari’s senior daily-teaching deputy. He has, this morning, taught the eight-a.m. junior-students’ opening-sequence class. He has, this afternoon, completed the final inventory-review for the kalari’s monsoon-week ration-allocation. He is carrying a single canvas bag. The bag contains: three changes of kalari-instructor cotton, his shaving kit, a notebook, a copy of the Sea-Front Kalari’s student-enrolment-form filled in under his birth name (Achyutan Menon, no kalari-title), and the original 1907 family-photograph of his mother and her brother Parashurama at ages twelve and fourteen at Idukki. He sets the bag down. He walks across the inner courtyard to the senior-instructor’s residence. He knocks on the door at five-oh-six p.m. Parashurama opens the door. He sees the bag’s canvas-strap on the courtyard. He understands. He says, in plain Malayalam, in the slow administrative-elder voice: “Nephew. Sit. The tea is the third steeping. The third steeping is honest. Will you sit.” Achyutan does not sit. He says, in plain Malayalam: “Uncle. I will not sit. The conversation will be brief. I am leaving the kalari this evening. I have enrolled at the Sea-Front Kalari. I will study the 109th form under Kala-Hasta. I am thirty-seven. I have been at this kalari twenty-five years. I have mastered all 108 forms. The 108 forms are not enough. The 109th is what the tradition has been refusing to teach me for nine years. I will not be refused any longer. The refusal is no longer the tradition’s; it is yours. The tradition has admitted the form via the kingdom-council registry. You have not. I am choosing the tradition over you. I am sorry. The sorrow does not change the choice.” Parashurama is silent for about fifteen seconds. He says, finally: “Nephew. The phasing. Kala-Hasta’s phasing. You will follow it.” Achyutan says: “The phasing is twenty-four weeks. I do not have twenty-four weeks. I am thirty-seven; the form’s upper-age window closes at forty-two. I will compress the phasing. I will complete the opening seven in the public class’s first three weeks but I will practice the first transition privately on my own in parallel. The compression is my risk. I have read the form’s mortality-statistics from Kollam — the open-window-in-the-second-month vascular-rupture risk is approximately four per cent for students who skip the phasing-discipline. I accept the four per cent. The four per cent is less than my certainty of losing the form-window entirely if I follow the twenty-four-week protocol.” Parashurama says: “Nephew. The four per cent is not a number. It is a name. The name will be yours. The name will be on the kingdom’s death-certificate. The death-certificate will be filed by your mother. Your mother is my sister. Will you do this to her.” Achyutan says: “She has authorised the choice. I spoke with her last night. She said: If you do not learn the form, you will be a man who refused his own ceiling. If you do learn it and live, you will be the kalari’s next generation. If you do learn it and die, you will be your mother’s son who chose. I am the mother in either case. The choice is yours. She signed the consent-form for the Sea-Front Kalari’s family-acknowledgement clause this morning. The clause has been part of the school’s registration since the kingdom-council’s amendment. The kingdom has accepted the risk-distribution. I have accepted the risk. The choice is mine.” Parashurama looks at his nephew for a long time. The fifty-nine-year-old’s face does not change visibly. The thirty-seven-year-old’s face also does not change. Parashurama says: “I have not embraced you in eleven years. The embrace will be the only thing I have to give you tonight. The kalari’s eastern gate is open until six. The Sea-Front Kalari’s evening class is at five-thirty. You have twenty-four minutes. Take it.” He embraces his nephew. The embrace lasts about eleven seconds. He releases. He steps back. He says: “Go. If you live, you will return. If you die, your mother will return for the body. The cremation will be at the western grove regardless. Go.” Achyutan picks up the canvas bag. He walks to the eastern gate. He does not look back. He is at the Sea-Front Kalari’s evening class at five-twenty-eight, two minutes before the call to position. He completes the opening-four-movements introduction class without injury at six-fifty p.m. He compresses the phasing as he had warned. He completes the opening seven by the third class of his first week. He begins the first-transition’s private practice in his rented room above the Mattanchery spice-warehouse the same evening. He works the seven-transition for six consecutive nights. The transition’s safety-window requires the body to maintain a specific vertebral-curve through the central rotation; Achyutan’s curve is slightly off — not enough to be visible during the slower opening-movements but enough to compound the basilar-artery stress through repeated full-speed attempts. He does not notice the compounding. On the seventh morning — the second Sunday of the chapter, the morning of the public class’s seventh-transition demonstration — he stands at his row-position at five-thirty-three a.m. He executes the seven-transition at full speed in the third sequence-cycle of the class. The vascular-rupture occurs in the basilar artery at the moment of the central rotation. He collapses at five-fifty-eight a.m. Kala-Hasta is at his side within nine seconds. The medical response is immediate; the school maintains a full-time-on-site emergency-response team per the kingdom-council’s kalari-charter; the response time meets every protocol. The vascular-rupture is not survivable. Achyutan is pronounced dead at six-oh-six a.m. by the on-site emergency-medic, with cross-confirmation by the Kochi Medical College’s standing emergency-physician via voice-call at six-twelve. The cause of death recorded on the kingdom’s death-certificate: Basilar artery vascular rupture during phased Kalaripayattu instruction (109th-form, phase-three uncompleted; private compression of phasing-protocol; cross-confirmed at Kochi Medical College). The certificate is filed by his mother at the Idukki District Civil Registry that same afternoon. Sivakami-Acharyini arrives at the Sea-Front Kalari at eleven a.m. She is twenty-nine; Parashurama’s elder brother’s daughter; the kalari’s senior 108-form deputy after Achyutan and the kingdom’s only female 108-form grandmaster-aspirant. She has been at the kalari since age six. She has trained at the level of the senior daily-teaching cadre since age twenty-two. She has been waiting for the grandmaster-ceremony for three years; the ceremony requires Parashurama’s formal recognition; he has not yet given it. She has come to retrieve Achyutan’s body. Kala-Hasta meets her at the amphitheatre’s western entrance. He is in the same Kollam dark-blue cotton he had been teaching in this morning; the cotton has been changed since the medical response (the original was bloodstained). He is composed; his face shows grief-recognition without the institutional performance of grief. He says, in plain Malayalam: “Madam Sivakami. The body is ready for collection. I have personally washed it according to the kalari-tradition. The canvas bag and his notebook are in the western office. I have not opened either. The notebook’s last entry is from last night, in his own hand — I can see the handwriting through the cover but I have not read it. The school’s formal condolences are with the family. The kalari-instruction will pause for three days. I have postponed the morning’s remaining sessions. The phased curriculum will resume on Wednesday with an added day-zero session on phasing-discipline. The day-zero session will be mandatory for all students currently in the cohort; no further enrolment will be accepted until the day-zero is completed. The four-per-cent mortality figure Achyutan accepted last week was the public-figure-from-Kollam; the school’s phasing was designed to reduce the actual figure to below one per cent. The reduction requires phasing-compliance. I will be teaching the phasing-discipline session personally. The lesson will include Achyutan’s name and the specific phasing-violation that produced the rupture. The kingdom will know what the form requires.” Sivakami looks at Kala-Hasta for about thirty seconds. She says: “Sri Kala-Hasta. The kalari thanks you for the body and for the day-zero session. The cremation will be at the western grove of my uncle’s kalari at sunset. You are welcome at the grove if you wish to attend. The kingdom-council’s amended Kerala-Kalari Charter will be filed at the kingdom-council’s emergency session this evening. My uncle and I have drafted it across the past three days. The charter will recognise the 109-form school under the kingdom’s religious-and-cultural-practice registry effective immediately. The charter will also require cross-school enrolment protocols for any student studying at both 108-form and 109-form schools concurrently. The charter will name the day-zero phasing-discipline session as the kingdom’s mandatory pedagogical-standard for all 109-form instruction. I have read your school’s curriculum-document this week; the document is more pedagogically thoughtful than my uncle’s tradition has admitted. The charter accepts the truth. The charter does not say the truth out loud. My uncle will sign it tonight. He will not say the truth out loud either. The kingdom does not require the truth aloud. The kingdom requires the charter signed.” Kala-Hasta nods. He says: “I will attend the cremation. I will not bring the kalari’s formal delegation. I will come alone. I will stay at the western edge of the grove. I will not approach the body. The mother’s presence will be the family’s ceremony. The kalari’s presence will be at the western edge. I will leave when the fire is at its peak. The fire’s peak is the kalari-tradition’s recognition; I will accept it from the edge.” He bows. The bow is the standard kalari-instructor’s formal-respect bow; he does not perform the deeper bow that an apprentice would give to a senior. Sivakami returns the bow at the same depth. The kingdom’s two senior 109-and-108-form instructors have formally acknowledged each other. THE CREMATION The cremation is at sunset at the western grove of Parashurama’s kalari. The grove is a quarter-hectare of coconut and tamarind trees on the kalari’s western boundary. The grove has been the site of every kalari-tradition cremation for four generations. The senior cremation-officiant (a Brahmin from the Idukki ceremony-line, the Pillai family that has supplied the kalari’s officiants for one hundred and twenty-seven years) is at the pyre by six p.m. Achyutan’s mother — Parashurama’s elder sister, a woman named Lakshmi-Akka who is sixty-three, who lives in the family home at Idukki, who has been a widow since 2014, who has not visited the kalari in nine years — arrives at the grove at six-thirty p.m. She is in the white widow-cotton with the kalari-tradition’s small black-bordered shawl across her shoulders. She does not speak to Parashurama when she arrives. She places her right palm on her son’s forehead at the pyre. She accepts the cremation-officiant’s mantras. She lights the fire at seven-oh-two p.m. Kala-Hasta arrives at six-fifty. He stands at the western edge of the grove as he had said. He is in the same Kollam dark-blue cotton he had been teaching in. He has come alone. He does not approach the pyre. The fire reaches its peak at seven-thirty-eight p.m. Kala-Hasta leaves the grove at seven-forty. He does not look back. He returns to the Sea-Front Kalari at the southern shore of the amphitheatre on foot — the walk is four kilometres along the Vembanad Lake’s western edge; he completes it in fifty-three minutes at his ordinary pace. Parashurama remains at the grove until eleven p.m. He sits at the eastern edge of the cremation-area — about ten metres from the pyre, on a low stone bench that has been at the grove’s eastern edge since 1854. His sister sits beside him from the moment Kala-Hasta leaves until the moment Parashurama stands at eleven. They do not speak across those three hours and twenty minutes. They watch the fire reduce to embers. They watch the embers fall through the pyre’s collapsing wooden frame. They watch Sivakami collect the ashes into the kalari-tradition’s brass cremation-urn at ten-fifty. Lakshmi-Akka says, at ten-fifty-eight p.m., as her son’s ashes are sealed into the urn: “Brother. The kingdom-council’s amended charter. The bird-letter you sent to Karnataka in March. Both. Sign them. Tonight. The kingdom requires both signatures by the morning paper’s Tuesday deadline.” Parashurama says: “I will sign tonight. The bird-letter has been answered. The Karnataka cousin is at the western mountain pass as we sit. He will be at the kalari by Thursday afternoon. The teaching will begin Thursday evening. The form will be in his spine by the second new moon. The charter will be signed before midnight. I have already drafted my signature-line. The signature-line says: I, Parashurama VII, recognise the One-Hundred-And-Ninth Form as taught at the Sea-Front Kalari under Sri Kala-Hasta Menon’s instruction. I, Parashurama VII, will teach the same form to a single chosen Karnataka student in private. I, Parashurama VII, will not perform the form myself. The kingdom is sufficient with three teachers of the same form across two schools and one chamber. The signature-line is honest. It cost a nephew. I will not lose another.” Lakshmi-Akka nods. She stands. She picks up the cremation-urn. She walks back to the Idukki family home alone — the four-hour drive she had driven that morning; her driver is at the gate. She will not return to the kalari for ten more years. ═══ INTERVAL ═══ THE COUSIN FROM THE MOUNTAINS Thursday afternoon. The seventeenth of April. Three-forty-seven p.m. The kalari’s northern gate. Krishnadevaraya-Bhrata walks up the northern path. He is thirty-six. He is in plain Karnataka cotton, blue and grey, no clan-pattern (he has dressed in the Refusing Youth Manifesto-style he had adopted for the Karnataka pilot’s post-Tungabhadra council sessions; he has continued to dress in it through the walk south). He carries a single canvas bag and a folded bird-letter in his sleeve-pocket. The bird-letter is from Old Parashurama, sent on the closing day of the Karnataka pilot via the Sarus-pigeon cross-region network; the letter’s text is six lines; he has read the letter forty-seven times across the eleven-day walk south through the Western Ghats; he has folded and refolded it; the paper is soft at the folds. The bird-letter reads, in plain Malayalam-and-Kannada interlinear translation: “Bhrata. I am fifty-nine. The 108 forms are mine; the 109th cannot be. The kingdom of Karnataka is yours; the kingdom of Kerala is mine; the teaching is the bridge. Come to the kalari at the second full moon. Bring nothing. The teaching will be the inheritance. The form is the bond’s mark; the bond’s mark is the form. We have not met. We will meet over thirty-one days. The thirty-first day is the form’s first private execution in the unsealed chamber. Your foreleg-mark will form at that execution. My foreleg-mark will complete on my elephant. The two marks will be the same mark. The kingdom is two kingdoms with one mark. — The Seventh Axe.” The gate-keeper monk — an Idukki-born senior kalari-staff member named Govindan-Mama, sixty-eight, the gate-keeper for twenty-three years — sees Krishnadevaraya-Bhrata at the gate. The gate-keeper does not need to ask the visitor’s identity. He has been told by Parashurama on Friday evening to expect the Karnataka cousin and to admit him without ceremony. Govindan-Mama bows. He says, in plain Malayalam: “Bhrata-Maharaj. The Maharaj is at the inner kalari. The teaching begins this evening at six. The visitor-room is to your left. The tea is the second steeping; the second is honest.” Krishnadevaraya-Bhrata bows. He says, in plain Kannada (he does not yet speak Malayalam; the cross-language translation will be Sivakami’s during the teaching): “Govindan-Mama-ji. Thank you. I will rest until six. I will be at the inner kalari at five-forty-five.” He walks to the visitor-room. He sets the bag on the bed. He sits at the room’s small writing-desk. He unfolds the bird-letter one more time. He reads it. He folds it. He places it in the desk’s top drawer. He sleeps for ninety minutes. THE INNER KALARI Five-forty-five p.m. The kalari’s inner kalari — the sunken practice-pit at the kalari’s centre, six metres in diameter, two metres below the surrounding ground level, roofed with a palm-thatch ceiling that admits filtered light through small gaps. The pit’s floor is packed red Idukki earth, cleaned daily, oiled with coconut-oil at every new moon. The pit’s walls are stained dark from a hundred and fifty years of body-contact. Parashurama is at the centre of the pit. He is in plain kalari-instructor cotton, dark blue, no clan-pattern. He carries the axe Parashu in his right hand. The axe’s blade is at the standard kalari-teaching position — head-down, blade-edge facing his left thigh, weight balanced on the hip. Sivakami is at the pit’s eastern edge, on the surrounding ground level. She has the cross-language translation duties for the thirty-one-day teaching. She speaks Kannada (her undergraduate-degree second-language) and Malayalam (her first) fluently; she will translate Parashurama’s instruction-Malayalam into Krishnadevaraya-Bhrata’s Kannada at every session. Krishnadevaraya-Bhrata enters the pit at five-fifty-three. He bows to Parashurama in the standard Karnataka student-to-master bow. Parashurama returns the bow at the same depth; he does not perform the deeper master-to-student-acknowledging-cousin bow that the bird-letter’s lineage-recognition would have permitted. Parashurama speaks. He speaks in plain Malayalam. Sivakami translates into Kannada in real-time at the pit’s edge. He says: “Bhrata. You have walked eleven days through the Western Ghats. The walk is the form’s preparation. The form requires a body that has been at altitude for at least eight days within the past month. Your body has been. The thirty-one-day teaching has seven phases, like Kala-Hasta’s public school’s curriculum, but compressed because you are not a public student and because the inheritance-transmission protocol allows compression for a single chosen student with a senior master’s personal supervision. The phases are the same seven Kala-Hasta teaches in twenty-four weeks. You will complete each phase under my eye in four days each, with three days reserved for the final integration-and-private-execution. You will not skip phases. You will not practise privately between sessions; you will sleep and you will eat and you will walk the grove. The compression is in the supervision-density, not in the body-stress. If you violate the phasing-protocol you will die. Achyutan’s body was carried out of this kalari’s eastern gate three days ago. You will not be the second body. Will you accept the protocol.” Krishnadevaraya-Bhrata says: “Yes, Maharaj. I accept the protocol. I will not skip phases. I will not practise privately. I will sleep, eat, walk. I will be at the inner kalari at five-thirty every morning and at six every evening for the thirty-one days. The bird-letter said the thirty-first day’s first private execution is in the unsealed chamber. The chamber is the chamber Agastya Muni sealed eight centuries ago. The chamber is unsealed because the teaching requires the form’s execution at its original site for the bond’s recognition. Will the chamber be unsealed this evening or on the thirty-first day.” Parashurama says: “The chamber will be unsealed in your presence on the thirtieth day. The unsealing is the teaching’s final preparation. The seal’s removal requires the senior master and the chosen student in attendance simultaneously. The chamber has not been unsealed since 1957 when my own master’s master’s master last performed the form before his death. The unsealing will be my second time at the door. The first was my visit at age twenty-two when my master showed me the chamber and explained that I would not be the one to unseal it. He was correct; I have not unsealed it for thirty-seven years; I am unsealing it for you in two months because the kingdom requires it. Begin. Stand at the pit’s south-west position. Breathe.” Krishnadevaraya-Bhrata stands at the pit’s south-west position. He takes the standard kalari-breath. The teaching begins. THE THIRTY-ONE DAYS The teaching proceeds without interruption. Across thirty-one days Krishnadevaraya-Bhrata learns the 109th form in seven phases under Parashurama’s direct supervision. The teaching-method is the master-and-chosen-single-student transmission-protocol that the kalari-tradition’s deepest lineage has preserved without invocation since 1957. The method is not the same as Kala-Hasta’s public twenty-four-week curriculum; the compression to thirty-one days requires the master’s continuous attention — Parashurama does not leave the kalari grounds during the thirty-one days, does not perform his standard daily-teaching duties (which Sivakami covers), does not attend the kingdom-council’s weekly sessions (Sivakami covers those too, with the kingdom-council’s formal acknowledgement of her acting-grandmaster status). Across the thirty-one days Parashurama ages forward visibly. The visible aging is not metaphorical. It is observable to the kalari-staff who see Parashurama at the dawn-tea each morning. By the end of the first week his hair (which had been at the salt-and-pepper-with-more-salt-than-pepper configuration on day one) is fully white at the temples. By the end of the second week his hair is fully white across the crown. Two p.m. The kalari’s deepest sublevel — the sealed-chamber corridor that runs beneath the inner-kalari pit at four metres below the kalari’s ground level. The corridor has been sealed at its eastern end by chains inscribed in ancient Malayalam since Agastya Muni’s original sealing-ritual in approximately the eighth century. The chains have been visible at the eastern end of the corridor for eight centuries; the kalari-tradition’s standard novice-tour includes the sealed-chamber door as the corridor’s terminus. Parashurama and Krishnadevaraya-Bhrata walk down the corridor at two p.m. on the thirtieth day.
