═══ FIRST HALF ═══ OPENING — THE PRAKASHAM BRIDGE AT EIGHT-FORTY-SEVEN A.M. Vijayawada. The second Monday of August. Eight-forty-seven a.m. The Prakasham Barrage Bridge across the Krishna river — the kingdom’s primary administrative-and-commuter crossing between the western Krishna district and the eastern Guntur district. The bridge is one-point-two kilometres long, four-lane, with pedestrian walkways on both sides; standard commuter-time traffic; approximately fourteen thousand vehicle-crossings per hour at the peak. Three hundred fishermen are walking north across the bridge from the western Krishna-district end. They are in plain delta-fisherman cotton, off-white, with the standard Krishna-and-Godavari delta’s blue-bordered shoulder-cloth. They are barefoot. They carry no weapons. They carry no banners. They carry no signs. They walk at the standard delta-fisherman pace — one metre per second — in a loose column of four-abreast, occupying the bridge’s eastern pedestrian walkway and the rightmost vehicle-lane. The Krishna District Bridge Police constable on duty at the western entry-checkpoint — a thirty-six-year-old officer named Sub-Inspector Pavan Reddy — sees the procession approaching from the western embankment at eight-forty-three. He has been on bridge-duty for nine years. He has never seen a fisherman-procession on the bridge that was not part of an authorised festival-permit. He radios the central control-room. The control-room confirms that no festival-permit has been filed for this date. The procession is unauthorised. He approaches the lead-fisherman at eight-forty-six. The lead-fisherman is a woman. She is thirty-five. She is in the same plain delta-fisherman cotton with the blue-bordered shoulder-cloth. She is barefoot. She carries no weapon. She carries no banner. She carries, in her left hand, a forty-seven-page folded document tied with a brown cotton string. The document’s top sheet is visible at the fold. The sheet is letterhead-paper from the Eluru District Sub-Registrar’s Office, dated 16 June 1990, with a sub-registrar’s stamp and the signature of a sub-registrar named B. Surya Prakasham (now deceased; retired 1998; died 2014). The lead-fisherman stops at three metres from Pavan Reddy. She bows the standard delta-fisherman bow — palms together at the chest, slight forward inclination, the standard greeting between equals. She speaks in plain Telugu. Her voice is calm; her diction is the careful diction of someone who has rehearsed. She says: “Officer Reddy. We are the Krishna-and-Godavari Delta Fishermen’s Mutual-Aid Council. We are walking north to Vijayawada Town Hall to file a kingdom-council petition. The petition has been on our council’s drafting-table for two years. The petition’s lead-signatory is myself. My name is Kalaniksha. I am the elder twin of the kingdom’s state hero Krishnaveni. I have the family-archive document confirming the relationship in my left hand. I am here to claim the river-throne by birth-order. The procession is unarmed. The procession will disperse the moment Town Hall accepts the petition. We require the bridge’s eastern pedestrian walkway for the next eleven minutes. We do not require the bridge’s vehicle-lanes. The vehicle-traffic may continue unobstructed. May we proceed.” Pavan Reddy looks at her for about fifteen seconds. He has been told nothing about a Kalaniksha. He has been told the kingdom’s state hero Krishnaveni was an only child. He has, however, received the family-archive document’s top-sheet’s sub-registrar’s stamp’s scanned-image at his bridge-duty briefing this morning — the brief had said the document was being investigated by the AP State Civil Service’s Bloodline-A Authenticity Cell and that any procession claiming the document’s lineage-evidence should be permitted to proceed if unarmed and if the procession does not obstruct vehicle-traffic. He radios the control-room. The control-room confirms the permission-protocol. He says, in plain Telugu: “Madam Kalaniksha. The procession may proceed on the eastern pedestrian walkway. The vehicle-lanes must remain unobstructed. The Town Hall reception has been notified by my radio-call. The procession will be received by the Town Hall’s reception-cell at nine-eleven if your pace holds at one metre per second. The kingdom-council’s emergency-petition reception is open until five p.m. today. Please proceed.” Kalaniksha bows. The three hundred fishermen behind her bow in unison. The procession resumes. She reaches the Town Hall at nine-eleven a.m. exactly. The Town Hall has been alerted; the kingdom-council’s emergency-petition cell’s senior officer (a forty-four-year-old administrator named Indira Krishnayya) is at the eastern reception-desk to receive her. The reception takes fifteen minutes. The petition is filed at nine-twenty-six. The petition’s number is AP-EP-2026-0847. It is the kingdom’s 847th emergency-petition filing of the year and the first by a lineage-rivalry claimant in the kingdom’s post-Narakasura twelve-year administrative history. The three hundred fishermen disperse from the Town Hall plaza at nine-thirty. Kalaniksha walks back across the bridge alone at ten a.m. She is at the western embankment by ten-twelve. She continues south along the riverbank toward the Krishna delta’s upstream entry-point at the Vijayawada-Eluru road. She walks for the next four hours. She is at the delta’s northern boundary at two-fifteen p.m. She crosses into delta-territory by foot. She does not look back at the city.
