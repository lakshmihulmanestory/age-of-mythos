Heaviest Cost • This Chapter
Forty-seven thousand people die of famine in central India while the twelve-court system deliberates which court has jurisdiction. Dvadashashringa survives Chapter Two and earns his Stage III mark — but the mark forms cracked down the centre, a visible scar through the antler-curve. He dissolves the twelve courts himself by striking the floor of the assembly with the Dwadash twelve-pronged staff — the staff breaks on the eighth strike; he keeps striking; by the twelfth strike the staff is in three pieces. The dharma is in the breaking. He carries the broken staff into Chapters Three and Four as the saga’s loudest reminder that the cost of late action is paid in bodies.

The Hero (Survives, Cracked)

Dvadashashringa — The Twelve-Antlered Who Broke His Own Twelve

Forty-six. Yudhishthira-paralysed. Founded the twelve-court system at the start of his reign. Dissolves it in the middle of a famine because his twelve-year-old daughter reads the death-toll arithmetic better than his entire court. The Stage III mark forms cracked — survival earned at the cost of his own founding principle.

The Child Who Read the Numbers

Sutrakara — The Twelve-Year-Old Arithmetician

Dvadashashringa’s daughter. Mathematics prodigy. Has been sitting in court galleries since age eight. Tracks the famine death-count correctly when the courts cannot. Speaks at the assembly. Is ignored. Walks out. Her father follows her to the corridor — the conversation in that corridor is the chapter’s real climax.

The Catalyst Villain

Adharma-Kendra — The Thirteenth Voice Who Voted Abstain

Maternal uncle of Dvadashashringa and Gangaputra both. Returns not as attacker but as a neutral thirteenth arbitrator-judge whom all twelve courts trust. Votes “abstain” on every issue, weaponising neutrality. Exposed when Drona-of-the-Plain testifies. Flees to Bhopal and disappears. Survives the chapter.

The Twelve Courts — What Was Dissolved

Madhya Pradesh’s twelve-court system was Dvadashashringa’s founding governance design — a court for each dharmic sub-domain, each presided over by a senior judge, deliberating in parallel. It worked for two decades. It fails in one famine.

After the dissolution: a single emergency court convenes, with Dvadashashringa as sole judge and Sutrakara as official advisor. Vidura-Babu of UP sends two Awadhi administrators on day three. Food convoys begin moving within forty-eight hours.

The Cross-Kingdom Refugee

Mahaguru Drona-of-the-Plain — the senior dharmic scholar whose Imambara testimony in Lucknow last month broke his forty-year secret debt to Adharma-Kendra — arrives at Dvadashashringa’s court secretly, hunted by Adharma-Kendra’s agents. He brings two pieces of news: (1) Adharma-Kendra is engineering the twelve-court paralysis on purpose; (2) the same coordinating network funded Bhoomi-Takshaka’s Karnataka Restoration AND the Mormugao port consortium AND now MP’s thirteenth-voice scheme. The villains of Volume I are coordinating. Drona is the saga’s first confirmation of that fact.

The Void Maw Sign

Two weeks after the court-dissolution, a herd of sixty barasingha in Kanha is reported standing motionless in a clearing. They do not graze. They do not move. They face east. They stand for eleven days. On the twelfth day they begin grazing again. None of them remember the eleven days. Dvadashashringa reads what the sign means through the bond: the herd was caught in the gap between knowing-the-question and answering-it. The Maw lives in deliberation that does not end. The herd-sign is the chapter’s first multi-creature Maw event — sixty animals at once, not one. He marks the clearing. The Kanha forest guards become the shrine’s keepers.
