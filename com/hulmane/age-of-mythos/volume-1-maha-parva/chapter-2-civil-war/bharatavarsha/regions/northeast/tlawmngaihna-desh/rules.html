The Serow-Bond — Stage III on the Mountain (Hero-Death)

Tlangvala’s Stage III serow-foreleg mark does not form on his body. The body is part of the mountain at the moment of completion. The mark forms instead on the eastern granite face of Phawngpui Mountain as a forty-seven-metre living-rock relief — the bull-serow’s right forelimb in mid-stride, rendered in the same ink-density as a body-mark, structurally integrated into the granite’s recomposed mineral state. Visible from twenty-three kilometres away; permanent for at least eight hundred years per the Forest Department’s geological assessment. This is the saga’s twelfth bond-mechanic: the mountain-mark — the first hero-death bond-mark to be carried by the landscape rather than by the hero’s body or by a successor’s body.

Saga-Rule • The Mountain-Mark Mechanic
A hero whose Stage III bond-trial requires the hero’s death by physical-incorporation-into-a-landscape may earn the Stage III mark on the landscape itself rather than on a body. The mechanic requires: (1) the hero’s death must be by physical incorporation (not by combat-death; not by sacrifice-by-burning; not by drowning) — the hero must walk into the landscape and be physically absorbed into its substance, (2) the absorption must be voluntary and witnessed by the kingdom’s assembled crowd at the threshold-distance the kingdom-council has approved, (3) the bond-bearer’s succession must be confirmed within seventy-two hours of the absorption by a separate witness-event (Hmangaihi’s Stage I hoofprint forms within 48 hours; the two-headed serow speaks her name within 72 hours), (4) the landscape’s mark must be structurally permanent — not painted, not carved, but emerging from the landscape’s own substance reorganisation. The mountain-mark is the saga’s first inheritance of Stage III by landscape-substitution; the bond stays at full Stage III continuously, displayed by the mountain forever, while the successor begins at Stage I on her body. The two-stage configuration (full Stage III on landscape + Stage I on successor’s body) is the saga’s first split-mark distinct from the UP split-bond (which split the same animal across two bodies) and from the mirror-bond (which mirrored the same mark across two sisters). The mountain-mark is the saga’s first split-MARK.

The Tattoo Ladder — Tlangvala & Hmangaihi (Split Across Two Bearers)

Four stages. The Mizoram split-mark is unique: Stages I and II are on Tlangvala’s body (completed and deceased); Stage III is on the mountain’s eastern granite face (permanent, landscape-borne); Hmangaihi begins her own Stage I on her right shoulder forty-eight hours after the eastern mark completes.

Rendered in the same ink-density as a body-mark would be; the hoof-impression at the lower edge, the foreleg arching upward, the shoulder-curve, the head-in-profile at the topmost section with the curved horn cresting at the upper-left. The Maw subsequently confirms the bond-transfer via the two-headed serow that speaks her name at 23 hours’ age. Hmangaihi’s Stage II and III will form across Ch 3 and Ch 4 by the standard ladder.

Sacred Weapons — The Hnam-Tuk and the Bent Bier-Bamboo

The chapter’s two bamboo-implements are not opposites — they are inherited and ceremonial. The Hnam-Tuk passes from father to son. The bier-bamboo is six metres long and bent at its upper-third to thirty-five degrees, left at the base of the mountain-mark as the kingdom’s permanent memorial.

Ancient Weapon • Inherited to Lalruatpuia
Hnam-Tuk — The Nation’s Spine
Type
A bamboo staff-spear two metres in length, single-stalk hill-bamboo cut in 2005 from the Phawngpui southern grove, hardened with twenty-one years of seasonal pine-resin treatments, the blade-end fitted with a small steel point and the grip-end wrapped in serow-hide leather. Flexible yet unbreakable. Tlangvala carried it for twenty-one years as Mountain Guardian.
Status This Chapter
Carried by Tlangvala into the eastern granite at five-thirty-one a.m. on the entry-morning. The spear is the only physical object that does not become part of the mountain — it is recovered from the southern fissure’s exit-path by the locked youth’s lead-walker (an eighteen-year-old Saiha refugee named Lalpianhlira) and returned to Ramdinmawia at the western trailhead by ten a.m. Passes formally to Lalruatpuia at the funeral procession by Ramdinmawia’s spoken declaration. Lalruatpuia carries the spear into the kingdom-council’s emergency ratification session on the Friday following the funeral. The spear’s daily seasonal pine-resin treatment continues under Lalruatpuia’s discipline; the spear will be lighter the more he uses it.
New Implement • The Bent Bier-Bamboo
The Six-Metre Bier — The Kingdom’s Bend Made Permanent
Type
A single length of bamboo, six metres long, cut from the standing-stem in the bamboo grove behind Tlangvala’s house on the night before the funeral procession by Hmangaihi and Lalruatpuia together (the parent plant was not felled). The bamboo’s upper third bends naturally to about thirty-five degrees from the weight of its own crown — the standard Mizo bamboo-bend. The bend was left in the bier.
Use This Chapter
Carried up the southern approach to the eastern mark’s base by Hmangaihi and Lalruatpuia at the funeral procession; left at the base of the mark as the kingdom’s permanent memorial. Vanlalruati refreshes the bend with a small saffron cord each morning, adjusting the tension to keep the bend at the standard thirty-five degrees. The bend is the procession’s answer to the mountain — the bamboo bends, the bamboo absorbs, the bamboo springs back; the mountain holds what the bamboo cannot become. By chapter end the bier has been visited by ~52,000 Mizos (about 30% of the kingdom’s adult population).

The Saga-Rules This Chapter Established

Saga-Rule • The Two Correctnesses
A kingdom under simultaneous short-term and long-term pressure faces a two-correctness dilemma: the short correctness (the locked must be released this week) and the long correctness (the inheritance-logic of the creed must be reformed across the next ten generations). The two correctnesses are not the same correctness; they cannot be performed by the same body. The kingdom divides the responsibility — the senior generation pays the short correctness (Tlangvala’s entry into the mountain), the next generation administers the long correctness (Lalruatpuia and Hmangaihi’s joint-signature reformed Manifesto). The division is the kingdom’s, not the individual’s. The division is honest. The two correctnesses can both be done. They cannot both be done by the same person at the same time. This rule applies to every kingdom whose Chapter Two crisis has a short-term ransom-equivalent demand (Mizoram, Sikkim, Karnataka) and a long-term creed-administration demand (Mizoram, Sikkim, Arunachal, Karnataka).
Saga-Rule • The Sacrifice-Acceptance Registry
A kingdom whose institutional charter has historically required, encouraged, or implicitly inherited a culture of self-sacrifice must, on the occurrence of a chapter-level sacrifice-event, establish a public registry of acceptances. The registry: (i) names each citizen who has volunteered the sacrifice, (ii) records the alternatives the citizen knew about at the time of volunteering, (iii) records the kingdom-council’s formal vote accepting or declining the offer, (iv) commits the kingdom to ten-year care for the citizen’s family following acceptance. The registry is the kingdom’s evidence that sacrifice is acknowledged publicly and borne institutionally — not absorbed silently into the kingdom’s culture. The Mizoram registry is the saga’s first such institution; the saga’s Volume Two and Three will see similar registries established at Yavana-Khanda coastal cities where citizen-soldier-sacrifice has been institutionally normalised.
Saga-Rule • The Future-Naming Maw Event (Heralds Are Named, Not Killed — extended to Bond-Bearers)
The Karnataka rule (Heralds are named, not killed) extends now to future bond-bearers via the Mizoram precedent. The two-headed serow at Phawngpui spoke Hmangaihi’s name with both heads in synchrony at twenty-three hours’ age. The Maw has demonstrated the capacity to name the next bond-bearer aloud through a creature that should not have existed and should not have spoken. The kingdom does not interfere with the experiment. The kingdom does not silence the speaking. The kingdom hears the Maw’s naming, logs it in writing, and continues the bond-transfer to the named successor as the kingdom would have done regardless. The Maw’s naming does not confer or increase authority on the named. The naming is the kingdom’s notice that the Maw is reading the saga’s bond-architecture aloud; the kingdom’s answer is to continue speaking the architecture back, more clearly, at every subsequent kingdom-council session.
Void Maw Sign • Catalogue Entry • Mizoram • The Two-Headed Serow (First Future-Naming Event)
A wild serow doe at the southern Phawngpui meadow gives birth at three-oh-two a.m. on day four after Tlangvala’s death. The kid is born with two fully-formed heads on a single body. Both heads breathe. Both heads open their eyes. The body is otherwise normal — one set of four legs, one torso, one tail, standard serow-kid colouring and proportions. The two heads share a single neck-base; they branch at approximately the level of the kid’s shoulder-blades into two distinct skull-and-jaw structures. The kid lives for approximately twenty-three hours. The mother grooms it. Both heads nurse simultaneously from the single mother. Both heads look in different directions independently; they blink in synchrony; they breathe in synchrony. At one-fifty-eight a.m. on the death-day, both heads speak together in plain Mizo, a single word: “Hmangaihi.” The voices are sharply distinct (one slightly higher-pitched, one slightly lower), neither resembling a standard serow vocalisation; the synchrony is within fifty milliseconds, consistent with two distinct vocal-tracts speaking in coordinated production rather than a single tract producing a doubled effect. The kid dies four minutes after speaking. Witnessed by Vanlalruati (senior shrine-keeper, Mizoram Forest Department), two junior rangers, Hmangaihi, and Lalruatpuia at the six-metre observation distance; audio confirmed on Vanlalruati’s body-camera recording. Mechanism (through the serow-bond): the Maw has experimented on the serow-bond’s succession; the experiment’s result is the audible confirmation of Hmangaihi as the next bond-bearer. The Maw has demonstrated the capacity to name the next bond-bearer aloud through a creature that should not have existed and should not have spoken. The sign is the saga’s first future-naming Maw event. Significance: Mizoram’s sign is the twelfth rung of the escalation pattern — the first future-naming experiment. The Maw has shifted from experimenting on memory (Arunachal’s face-erasure) to experimenting on the saga’s succession itself.

Cross-Kingdom Cascade-Alert
Hmangaihi sends the cross-kingdom Sarus-pigeon cascade-alert at three p.m. on the death-day. The alert reads: “The Maw has experimented on the Mizoram serow-bond. A two-headed kid was born on the Phawngpui southern slope four days after my Stage I hoofprint formed. The kid lived twenty-three hours. Before dying, both heads spoke together, in plain Mizo, a single word — my name. The naming is the saga’s first future-naming Maw event. The Maw has demonstrated the capacity to name the next bond-bearer aloud through a creature that should not have existed and should not have spoken. Watch your own bonded species for dual-body experiments and for instances of vocal-production by species that do not vocalise.” Krishnadevaraya-Bhrata at the buried-temple chamber in Karnataka sends the saga’s first cross-region reply (Vol I’s only inter-region cascade response so far), confirming that the Karnataka Third Herald’s Bronze-Dust Child had remained mute through the entire chapter and that the muteness now reads as a complementary signal.
