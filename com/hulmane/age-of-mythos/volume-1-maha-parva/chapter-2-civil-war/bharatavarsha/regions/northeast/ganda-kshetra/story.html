The Suppressed Coven — A Film in Two Halves
═══ FIRST HALF ═══ OPENING — THE KAMAKHYA AT DAWN The Kamakhya temple. Nilachal hill above Guwahati. Five-forty a.m. The mist is on the Brahmaputra below. The temple’s squat dome — carved sandstone, painted ochre and red, the shape that has not changed since the eighth century — catches the first pale-rose light. Kamalavarna is awake at the dome’s eastern threshold. She has been awake since four. She has performed the lower morning rites: the Kalika invocation, the salt-water purification, the lighting of the inner lamps. She has done them correctly. She has done them every morning for twelve years. She has not performed the Mahavidya rite. She has not authorised the Mahavidya rite. The Kalika Purana’s footnote that names the rite has been kept in the temple’s locked manuscript-case for as long as she has been High Priestess. She holds the key on a leather cord around her neck. She has not opened the case. Her sacred animal — the Indian one-horned rhinoceros — is in Kaziranga, three hours east. She does not see the rhino in person more than twice a year. The bond is constant. The White-winged Wood Duck flies past the temple at six a.m. exactly. The duck is the kingdom’s clock the way the Sarus Crane is UP’s. She watches the duck disappear over the Brahmaputra. She turns to go inside. As she turns she sees, across the temple courtyard at the outer gate, a woman in dust-red robes standing motionless on the threshold. The woman has been standing there since before dawn. She has not entered. She has not knocked. She has not called out. She is simply waiting. Kamalavarna does not yet know who the woman is. She nods. She goes inside. She does not think about the woman again until evening. THE AUNT-MOTHER That afternoon. The matriarch’s cell in the temple’s second tier. Bhairavi-Naam — eighty-seven years old, the woman Kamalavarna calls Aaee, the woman who has been her mother for as long as she remembers having one — is found dead in her sleeping mat. She is found by Lakshmi-priya, the youngest of the temple’s acolytes. Lakshmi-priya screams — the scream is brief, professional, the kind of scream a tantric acolyte is trained to allow herself and then immediately contain. She runs for Kamalavarna. Kamalavarna comes. She kneels. She closes her aunt-mother’s eyes. The eyes are clear — not glazed, not contorted. The matriarch died sleeping. She is also — Kamalavarna sees this within ninety seconds — arranged. The body lies in the eastern-facing posture of the Bhairavi-cycle’s seventh sub-rite. The arms are crossed at the wrists. The breath-mantra position. The matriarch did this herself. She knew she was dying. She arranged her own body in the position. The position is from the Mahavidya cycle. The locked-manuscript cycle. The rite Kamalavarna has not authorised in twelve years. She sits with the body for a long time. THE INVESTIGATION Two days later. Kamalavarna has had the temple’s cremation arranged at the Brahmaputra ghat. The matriarch’s ashes are scattered. The temple women have grieved openly — the matriarch was loved. Kamalavarna’s grief is private. She does her morning rites the next day exactly as she has done them for twelve years. The duck flies past at six. The temple opens for the public at seven. But she investigates. She talks to Lakshmi-priya. To the cremation-attendant. To the senior priestesses. To the temple gatekeeper. To the night-shift acolytes. The picture assembles slowly. The matriarch had been leaving the temple at night. Three nights a week. For the past four months. Walking down Nilachal hill to the Brahmaputra ferry-crossing at Saraighat. Crossing to the eastern bank. Walking into the clearing where Kama-Mara’s Open Coven meets. Participating in their rites. Returning before dawn. The matriarch — Kamalavarna’s adoptive mother, the High Priestess’s own teacher — had been secretly attending the suppressed coven’s Mahavidya-fragment cycles. The cause of death is in the Kalika Purana’s footnote on the rite. Cumulative rite-incompletion. When a tantric practitioner performs fragments of the Mahavidya cycle without ever completing the central invocation, the body accumulates the partial-cycles. After enough cycles — usually months, sometimes years — the body can no longer hold them. The accumulation collapses inward. The death is peaceful but it is a death by suppression. The matriarch died of the rite Kamalavarna would not perform. She closes the file. She walks the Brahmaputra ghat alone for three hours that evening. She does not cry. She does not curse. She walks. KAMA-MARA AT THE FERRY Kamalavarna crosses to the eastern bank that night. The Saraighat ferry runs until midnight. The boatman is the same boatman who has rowed her great-aunt across in secret three times a week for four months. He does not look at her face. He does not need to. The Open Coven’s clearing is a flat space among teak and bamboo half a kilometre inland from the ferry-landing. There is a small fire-pit at the centre. There are mats arranged in a circle. There are about thirty women present — they were expecting Kamalavarna. The news of the matriarch’s death and the matriarch’s secret participation has reached the Coven by yesterday. Kama-Mara is at the centre. She is forty-one. She is wearing a deep-burgundy cotton sari without ornament. Her hair is long and loose. Her tantric-tilak is on her forehead in dark vermillion. She and Kamalavarna look at each other for the first time in fourteen years. Kama-Mara says, in clear Assamese: "You came." "She was attending your rites." "She was. For four months. I did not invite her. I did not refuse her. She walked in on her own." "You knew her cycles were incomplete." "I knew. I did not have the authority to close them. The central invocation requires a sister-pair of the proper line. You and I are the only two who could have closed her cycles. You would not authorise the rite. I am not High Priestess. The matriarch knew this. She chose to keep practising anyway." "You let her die." "I did not let her die. Her own body chose. I will not lie to you about my part in this, sister. I taught her the fragments. The fragments alone are dangerous. The completion is what makes them safe. We could have completed them together — you, me, her — for any of the four months. We did not." The word sister hangs in the air. Kamalavarna does not address it. The Open Coven’s women do not move. Kamalavarna says, finally: "I will come back. I do not know when. I do not yet know what for." She walks back to the ferry. The boatman rows her across the Brahmaputra. The river is wide and quiet. The moon is half-full. THE WANDERER ENTERS The next morning. Six-fifteen a.m. The temple’s outer gate. The woman in dust-red robes is still standing on the threshold. She has been standing on the threshold for three days. The temple acolytes have brought her water; she has accepted the water without speaking. The acolytes have brought her food; she has refused the food. Kamalavarna walks to the gate. She stops three paces from the woman. She looks at her. The woman is sixty-eight years old. She is tall — nearly as tall as Kamalavarna. Her hair is white at the temples and grey through the centre. Her face is the most familiar face in the world to Kamalavarna and also the face of a stranger. She has Kamalavarna’s exact jawline. She has Kama-Mara’s exact eyes. She says, in soft Assamese: "Maa. I am Bhairavi-Aaee. May I come in." The honorific Maa is the High Priestess’s daughterly form — the word a daughter uses for a temple-mother. The wanderer is calling Kamalavarna daughter. Kamalavarna says, very quietly: "Come in." ═══ INTERVAL ═══ THE LINEAGE REVEAL The small east room of the temple complex. The room used for private dharmic conversation. Kamalavarna sits on one cushion. Bhairavi-Aaee sits on the cushion opposite. There is a clay pot of tulsi-tea between them. The wanderer has accepted the tea. Bhairavi-Aaee says: "The Kalika Purana has a footnote in the third manuscript at the back of your locked case. The footnote names the three-sister-line-with-one-wanderer pattern. The footnote is the reason I have come back. Read the footnote first. I will wait." Kamalavarna leaves the room. She returns ten minutes later with the third manuscript open at the footnote. She reads it aloud. The footnote is in old-Sanskrit hand. It describes a pattern of Chandravamsha matrilineal succession in which three sisters share the line — one to political seat, one to temple seat, one to wandering ascetic — and in which the Mahavidya rite’s central invocation can only be performed by two practitioners who are same-mother sisters of different fathers. The footnote names the configuration. The footnote does not say what to do if the configuration does not exist. The footnote assumes the configuration exists somewhere in the line at any given hour. Kamalavarna closes the manuscript. She looks at Bhairavi-Aaee. She says: "Tell me." Bhairavi-Aaee tells her. The telling takes nineteen minutes. The Chandravamsha line in this generation has three sisters — Devi-Maa (who took the political seat in the 1970s and died in 1991), Bhairavi-Naam (who took the temple seat and raised Kamalavarna and died last week), and Bhairavi-Aaee (the wandering ascetic, who has been wandering the Brahmaputra valley for thirty-five years and is sitting in this room). Bhairavi-Aaee had two daughters by two different partners in her wandering. The first daughter was given to Bhairavi-Naam to raise at the temple. That daughter’s name was changed when she came to the temple from her original name to Kamalavarna. The second daughter was raised by her own father, a Tibetan-Buddhist tantric scholar in a hill village above Guwahati. That daughter’s name was Kama-Mara. "Kama-Mara is my sister." "Kama-Mara is your sister. Same mother. Different father. Eighteen months apart. You were the elder." "The matriarch knew." "Your aunt-mother knew from the day I handed you to her. She honoured the secret because I asked her to. I asked her to because I did not want you raised in the wandering. I did not want either of you raised in the wandering. The wandering is my dharma. It is not a dharma for daughters." "You let me grow up not knowing who my mother was." "Yes." "For thirty-eight years." "Yes." Kamalavarna is quiet for a long time. She is not crying. She is not angry. She is reading the footnote of her own life with a tantric scholar’s patience. She says, finally: "The Mahavidya rite must be performed." "Yes." "By me and her." "By you and her. Together. Within the next thirty days. The matriarch’s death has opened the cycle-window. After thirty days the window closes for another forty-seven years. There will not be another sister-pair of the proper line in this kingdom within forty-seven years. If you do not perform the rite now, the suppression you have practised for twelve years becomes permanent." "And the women who have been dying from incomplete cycles in the Open Coven?" "They will continue to die. As they have. As your aunt-mother did." Kamalavarna sits with this. She finishes her tulsi-tea. She says: "I will send a runner to my sister tonight." THE BRAHMAPUTRA AT MIDNIGHT That night. Kamalavarna walks to the Brahmaputra alone. She carries the prayer-beads her aunt-mother gave her at age fifteen. She walks down to the ghat. She stands at the edge. She drops the prayer-beads into the river. The beads sink slowly. The river has carried six millennia of beads exactly like these. She speaks aloud, to nobody in particular: "Aaee. I will perform the rite. I will perform it with the daughter you raised differently. I will not be angry with you for the thirty-eight years. I will be angry with myself for the twelve." The rhino-bond pulses. The mark on her chest — Stage II from Chapter One, the rhino-shoulder mark across her collarbone — warms faintly. The bond is approving the decision. A White-winged Wood Duck flies past the ghat at the wrong hour — midnight, not six a.m. The duck’s flight is the kingdom’s clock saying the hour has changed. ═══ SECOND HALF ═══ THE MESSAGE The runner is the Saraighat ferry-boatman who has carried the matriarch three times a week for four months. He takes the letter from Kamalavarna’s hand. He does not read it. He rows it across the river. Kama-Mara reads the letter at her Open Coven hut on the eastern bank. The letter says, in Assamese: Sister, The matriarch knew. The wandering ascetic at our gate is your mother and mine. The Mahavidya rite must be performed within the cycle-window the matriarch’s death has opened. I am no longer suppressing the rite. I am asking you to perform it with me at the temple at the new moon. Bring your twelve senior coven women. Bring nothing else. I will meet you at the temple steps. — K. Kama-Mara reads the letter three times. The third time she reads it aloud to her twelve senior coven women. The senior women listen without interrupting. When she finishes, Tara-priya — the oldest of them, sixty-one years old, who had been Bhairavi-Naam’s student before being expelled with Kama-Mara in Chapter One — says: "Sister-priestess. We will come. We will not bring our blades." Kama-Mara nods. The coven women begin preparations. THE NEW MOON The Kamakhya temple at new moon. No moonlight. The temple lamps are lit — one hundred and eight oil lamps inside the dome, twelve at the outer altar, three at the inner altar. The inner altar is where the Mahavidya rite must be performed. Kama-Mara arrives at the temple steps at dusk. Twelve senior coven women in burgundy saris walk behind her. They have left their blades at the Saraighat ferry-landing as Tara-priya said they would. They wear no ornaments. Their tantric-tilaks are fresh in dark vermillion. Kamalavarna meets her at the steps. She is wearing the High Priestess’s ceremonial red-and-gold sari. Her tilak is the same dark vermillion. She does not embrace her sister. The two of them have not been raised together. The embrace is a thing for the rite to earn. She says: "Sister." Kama-Mara says: "Sister." They walk into the temple together. Bhairavi-Aaee is in the doorway of the inner altar chamber. She is still in her dust-red robes. She has not slept since the day she entered. She nods once at her daughters and stands aside. The Temple Order’s senior priestesses are in the outer dome — twelve of them, in a circle, with the twelve Open Coven women interleaved. The combined twenty-four form the surrounding mandala. They will chant. Kamalavarna and Kama-Mara walk past Bhairavi-Aaee into the inner altar chamber. They sit on the two cushions at the inner altar — facing each other across a low stone slab carved with the ten Mahavidya yantras. The rite begins. THE FIRST SIX HOURS The Mahavidya rite is not theatrical. It does not involve loud chanting or dramatic gestures. It is small and slow and very precise. The two sisters speak the ten goddess-mantras alternately — Kamalavarna takes the odd-numbered (Kali, Tara, Tripura-Sundari, Bhuvaneshvari, Chinnamasta), Kama-Mara takes the even-numbered (Bhairavi, Dhumavati, Bagalamukhi, Matangi, Kamala). Each mantra has nine repetitions. Each repetition has nine breath-cycles. The whole sequence is ninety repetitions per goddess, nine hundred repetitions in total for the central sequence alone. The pace is one breath every four seconds. The first hour is slow. Kamalavarna’s tongue has not spoken these mantras in a non-private setting in twelve years. She is rusty. The second hour she finds the cadence. So does Kama-Mara. The third hour the temple’s walls begin to glow faintly — the ochre-and-red paint catching some inner light that is not the lamps. The surrounding mandala chants without faltering. The Temple Order priestesses and the Open Coven women have begun the rite at opposing sides of the circle; by the third hour they are chanting in unison. The fourth hour Kamalavarna feels the tantric power for the first time in her life. Not a fragment. The whole. It is large. It is not human. It is also not destructive — she has been wrong about the rite all twelve years. The rite is not what she feared. The rite is what she has been afraid of without knowing what she feared. The fifth hour Kama-Mara begins to weep silently while continuing the mantras. The weeping does not interrupt the cadence. The weeping is part of the rite. The sixth hour the Brahmaputra at Saraighat — observed by the Saraighat ferry-boatmen, three news-channel boats already filming an unrelated story, and a Geological Survey of India monitoring station — begins to slow. THE SEVENTH HOUR The seventh hour the river reverses. The Brahmaputra at Saraighat — which has flowed west-to-east for forty-five million years — flows east-to-west. The ferry-boatmen feel the current first. They report it on radio within two minutes. The news-channel boats turn their cameras to the water. The Geological Survey monitoring station’s telemetry shows the reversal in real time. The reversal will be recorded in their public-archive dataset within twenty-four hours. The reversal lasts exactly one hour. In Kaziranga National Park — one hundred and seventy kilometres east of Kamakhya — every one-horned rhinoceros (one hundred and fourteen animals across the park’s recorded census) is found kneeling. Heads lowered. Facing west toward Kamakhya. The park-rangers on the morning patrol-route find them and at first think the herd has been poisoned. The animals are breathing normally. The animals are not in distress. The animals are simply kneeling and they will not move. Inside the Kamakhya inner altar chamber, Kamalavarna and Kama-Mara speak the final goddess-mantra. The tenth. Kamala. The lotus-goddess. The goddess of the rite’s own completion. They speak the mantra nine times. On the ninth repetition the tantric power that has been gathering through six hours fully fills the chamber. Kamalavarna feels the rhino-bond’s Stage III mark complete on her chest — the rhinoceros head and shoulder spreading across her collarbone and upper torso, the single horn pointing toward her right clavicle. The mark forms cleanly. No crack. Not a surrender-mark like Gangaputra’s, not a cracked-mark like Dvadashashringa’s. A clean Stage III. She looks at her sister. Kama-Mara’s sari has fallen open at the shoulder where the rite’s movements have loosened it. The same mark is forming on Kama-Mara’s shoulder. Identical. Mirror-position. The rhinoceros head, the shoulder, the single horn pointing toward the left clavicle — the exact mirror. The first sister-bond in the saga. Two living humans, two identical Stage III marks, one rite. They look at each other across the stone slab. Neither of them speaks. The mandala continues chanting. THE RITE’S END The rite ends at dawn. The surrounding mandala falls silent. The temple-walls’ inner glow fades. The Brahmaputra at Saraighat returns to its normal westward flow at exactly the moment the rite ends. The rhinos at Kaziranga begin grazing within five minutes. Bhairavi-Aaee, in the doorway, has not spoken for seven hours. She is older now — visibly older, in a way that is not a trick of the light. The rite has cost her something too. She says, in soft Assamese: "It is done. The Kamakhya seat is full. I will leave." Kamalavarna says: "Will you stay." "I am a wandering ascetic. The wandering is my dharma. But I will return to Kamakhya at the new moon every year while you both live. You have my word." She bows once to each daughter. She walks out of the temple. The temple gatekeeper sees her leave. The Saraighat ferry-boatman, returning across the river at first light, does not see her at the ferry-landing. The dust-red robes are not seen again in Guwahati that day, or that week, or that year. THE NEW ARRANGEMENT Kamalavarna and Kama-Mara sit on the temple steps at sunrise. The senior priestesses and the Open Coven women have gone to rest. The temple is quiet. Kamalavarna says: "We will be co-High Priestesses. The Open Coven’s clearing at Saraighat will be a formal second temple-site — supervised, sanctioned, the rites authorised. The Mahavidya rite will be performed annually at new moon by the two of us. The women who have been dying from incomplete cycles will not die anymore." Kama-Mara nods. Kamalavarna says: "I am yielding the political throne of the Northeast." Kama-Mara looks up. "To whom." "To Moirangthem of Manipur. He is our cousin. He is Bloodline C still — through the Sangai-deer line that is the second-oldest in the matrilineal tree. He will hold the political throne. We will hold the cosmic throne. The Northeast will have two seats." "He will accept?" "I have sent a Sarus Crane to him this morning. He will accept." THE CROSS-KINGDOM LETTER Mid-morning. A Sarus Crane arrives at the Kamakhya temple from the southwest — not from Manipur, which would be northeast; from Madhya Pradesh, far southwest. The crane carries a folded letter in her beak. The handwriting is small and precise. Kamalavarna takes the letter. The handwriting is a twelve-year-old’s. Great-aunt — my father has dissolved the courts. The Drona who came south told us about the villain-network. They are coordinating — Karnataka and Goa and now MP and probably also Banaras and probably Assam too. Be watchful. We have a small shrine in Kanha where sixty rhinos forgot eleven days. I think your rhinos will know what mine forgot. — Sutrakara, age twelve, MP. Kamalavarna reads it twice. She smiles. She passes the letter to Kama-Mara. Kama-Mara reads it and smiles too — the second time the two sisters have smiled at the same time in fourteen years. Kamalavarna writes the reply on the back of the same letter. She uses her sister’s coven-ink, which is darker than the temple’s. Sutrakara — I have just performed the Mahavidya with my sister. The Brahmaputra ran backward for one hour. One hundred and fourteen rhinos kneeled. Your father’s herd in Kanha and my herd in Kaziranga have spoken to each other across the Maw’s gap. Let them come. We are not what they think we are. Send word again when your calligraphy advances; mine has not improved in a decade. — Kamalavarna. The Sarus Crane carries the reply south. ═══ EPILOGUE ═══ THE KNEELING HERD Three days later. Kaziranga National Park. Kamalavarna and Kama-Mara have driven east together to visit the kneeling-meadow where the herd remained kneeling for seven hours during the rite. The park-rangers have established a small perimeter. The meadow itself is unaltered — the grass is short where the rhinos grazed yesterday morning, slightly flattened in the centre where the kneeling herd’s bodies pressed down for seven hours. The two sisters walk to the centre of the meadow. A single rhinoceros — the herd’s alpha female, approximately twenty-eight years old, the size of a small car — is grazing twenty metres away. The rhino looks up at the sisters. The rhino walks slowly toward them. The rhino stops three metres away. The rhino bows her head — not the kneeling-bow of the rite, but the lower bow of acknowledgement. Kamalavarna’s rhino-bond pulses through both her chest and through her sister’s chest simultaneously. Kama-Mara feels it. The bond is double now — mirrored across the two sisters. The rhino returns to grazing. Kamalavarna says: "We will build the shrine at the eastern edge of this meadow. Sandstone and packed earth. A single rhinoceros-horn carved in low relief on the western face — the direction the herd faced during the rite. The Kaziranga forest-rangers will be the shrine’s keepers." Kama-Mara says: "Yes." They drive back to Guwahati at dusk. THE TWO TILAKS The temple steps. Sunset. Kamalavarna and Kama-Mara are in the High Priestesses’ double-throne arrangement — two stone seats side by side at the inner courtyard, never used before because the temple has not had two High Priestesses in its eight-century history. Both sisters apply their tantric tilaks — the dark vermillion forehead-mark of the Kamakhya priestesshood — in the same gesture at the same moment. They have not coordinated. The gesture is the same. Tara-priya, the oldest of Kama-Mara’s former Open Coven women now serving at the formal Saraighat second site, watches from the courtyard edge. She is sixty-one. She has been waiting forty-three years for some version of this moment. She says, to nobody in particular but loud enough for the courtyard to hear: "The Chandravamsha line is whole." The White-winged Wood Duck flies past the temple at sunset — the wrong hour, again, the bird’s clock for the kingdom rearranged. The duck’s flight passes between the two sisters’ thrones and continues west toward the Brahmaputra. In the manuscript-case, the third manuscript’s footnote on the Mahavidya rite remains. Beside the footnote, Kamalavarna will write — the next morning, in her own hand — a single small note in the margin: Performed by Kamalavarna and Kama-Mara, daughters of Bhairavi-Aaee. New moon, 2026. The river reversed. The rhinos kneeled. The line was whole. She closes the manuscript. She does not lock the case.
The Pilot’s Closing Image
Two sisters in the High Priestesses’ double-throne arrangement at the Kamakhya temple. Both with the mirror rhinoceros-mark across the chest in identical mirror-position. Both wearing the dark vermillion tilak applied in the same gesture at the same moment. A White-winged Wood Duck flying past at the wrong hour of the day for the second time this week. A dust-red robe walking somewhere along the Brahmaputra at a distance no one in Guwahati can quite estimate. A locked manuscript-case now unlocked. A footnote with a marginal annotation that was not there before. One hundred and fourteen rhinos grazing in Kaziranga without memory of seven hours. A river running west to east as it has flowed for forty-five million years — with one hour of exception now in the Geological Survey of India’s public-archive dataset that will be cited in a hundred peer-reviewed papers over the next century.
