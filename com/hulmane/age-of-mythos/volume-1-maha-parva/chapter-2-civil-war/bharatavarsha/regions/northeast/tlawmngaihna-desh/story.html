The Self-Sacrifice Wound — A Film in Two Halves
═══ FIRST HALF ═══ OPENING — THE REFUSING YOUTH MANIFESTO Aizawl. The first Sunday of August. Three p.m. The Aizawl Presbyterian Synod hall — the largest indoor public-assembly space in Mizoram, capacity twenty-six hundred. The hall is two-thirds full this afternoon for the kingdom’s biennial Youth Council, an institution founded in 1962 to give Mizos under thirty a non-binding voice in the kingdom’s tribal-council deliberations. The Youth Council has been a polite formality for sixty-three of its sixty-four years. It is not polite this afternoon. Lalruatpuia is at the speakers’ lectern. He is twenty-two. He is Tlangvala’s son. He is in plain Mizo wool, blue and grey, no clan-pattern; he has deliberately dressed without his father’s family-mark. He has been at the lectern for forty-three minutes already and his prepared statement has run thirty-seven of those minutes. The remaining six minutes have been the call-and-response with the assembled fourteen hundred Refusing Youth — Mizos under thirty from all thirteen kingdom-villages, plus delegations from the Mizo diaspora in Bangalore, Delhi, Chennai, and Singapore. He says, into the assembly microphone: “Tlawmngaihna is the gift. Tlawmngaihna is not the inheritance. The gift is the instinct to help — born in the body, taught in the household, refined across generations. The gift is real. The gift may be carried by each Mizo who chooses to carry it. The inheritance is the demand — that every Mizo, by virtue of being a Mizo, must perform tlawmngaihna at the cost of the carrier’s body. The inheritance is the village’s ledger of expected sacrifice. The inheritance is the elder who has counted what the young will be required to give. The inheritance is not the gift. The inheritance is the gift weaponised against the next generation. We refuse the inheritance. We do not refuse the gift.” The hall claps. The clap is not the polite clap of the previous sixty-three Youth Councils. The clap is sustained for two minutes. About four hundred elders in the rear seating are not clapping. They are watching. Lalruatpuia continues: “The Manifesto is three clauses. We have published it on the kingdom’s public registry this morning. Clause One: tlawmngaihna may be chosen but it may not be inherited. Clause Two: service must come from capacity, not compulsion — the Mizo who has nothing left to give is excused from the giving. Clause Three: the kingdom may not require self-destruction as proof of citizenship. We are not asking for the creed to be abandoned. We are asking for the creed to be administered. The bamboo bends and the bamboo springs back. The bamboo does not bend until it breaks. The Refusing Youth is the bamboo’s answer to its own ancestors’ misuse of the creed.” He steps back from the lectern. The clap goes for four minutes. At the back of the hall, in the rear-right corner, sit the kingdom’s senior tribal-council delegates — nine men and three women, average age sixty-eight, the body that ratifies kingdom-level decisions on matters of Mizo customary law. Their senior chair is an elder named Ramdinmawia, eighty-one, the most senior Tlawmngaihna-Desh tribal-elder still active. He has been at this hall every biennium since 1971. He has not seen four-minute applause for a Youth Council statement in his lifetime. He turns to the elder beside him — the Aizawl Presbyterian Synod’s sixty-six-year-old senior moderator, Lalrinpuii (no relation to Lalruatpuia despite the shared root) — and says, in Mizo: “The boy is right. He is also wrong. He is right that the creed is being misused. He is wrong about the kingdom’s readiness to administer it. We will see how the kingdom answers.” THE FATHER’S HOUSE Lalruatpuia does not return to his father’s house that night. He sleeps at the home of his cousin Hmangaihi’s aunt (his mother’s sister, who lives in central Aizawl). He has not been on speaking terms with his father since the December 2025 Aizawl Synod hall sit-in — a Refusing Youth protest that occupied the Synod hall’s main floor for eleven days and that Tlangvala had publicly criticised as “the symptom of a household’s private failure, not the kingdom’s public dispute.” The criticism had been published in the Mizoram State Gazette’s weekend edition. Lalruatpuia had not spoken to his father since. Hmangaihi is seventeen. She is at the aunt’s house when Lalruatpuia arrives. She has been Lalruatpuia’s closest cousin since they were children — her mother (Tlangvala’s elder sister) had died in a 2018 landslide and Hmangaihi had been raised at Tlangvala’s household from age nine until the December sit-in opened the family’s public fracture. She had stayed with Tlangvala. She had not stayed silent. She sits across the kitchen table from her cousin at ten p.m. She has heard the Manifesto on the radio. She has read the three clauses on the kingdom registry. She has been at the four-minute applause through the radio feed. She says, in Mizo: “The three clauses are correct. You will not get the kingdom to ratify them. You will get the kingdom to harden against them. The December sit-in cost us twenty per cent. This will cost us another fifteen.” Lalruatpuia says: “The cost is necessary. The creed cannot be administered if the elders do not feel the cost of refusing to administer it.” She says: “The cost will be paid by the next sacrifice. Not by the elders. The elders never pay the cost. The next Mautam-equivalent will come and the kingdom will say tlawmngaihna remains and the next sacrifice will be made and the elders will say see, the creed holds. You are forcing them to defend the creed before they have learned to question it. The kingdom is not ready to question it.” Lalruatpuia says: “What is your alternative.” She says: “I do not have one yet. I am seventeen. I have been thinking about the question for two years and I do not have one yet. I do know that your three clauses are too direct for the elders to hear. They will hear them as an attack on the founders, not as a defence of the creed. The elders need a slower path to the same end. The slower path will take five years. You do not want to wait five years.” He says: “We do not have five years. Parvata-Asura has been visiting villages.” She is silent for thirty seconds. She says: “Father knows.” He says: “Father knows. Father will not refuse to help when the asura asks. That is the cost the elders will not pay. Father will pay it for them. I will not let him.” PARVATA-ASURA’S VILLAGE VISITS Parvata-Asura had been seen in the kingdom for the first time in seven months in spring 2025. He had been Chhinlung-Naag’s subordinate manifestation in Chapter One — the mountain-form of the cave-asura, which had retreated when Tlangvala had fought the serpent at the Chhinlung cave-mouth. The retreat had been into the Lushai Hills’ southern range, where Parvata-Asura had spent two years rebuilding his own form. He is now a mountain-asura in his own right, no longer subordinate. He had been visiting villages over the spring and summer. Aizawl. Lunglei. Champhai. Saiha. Mamit. Kolasib. Lawngtlai. Serchhip. By August he had visited all eight district-headquarters and five additional sub-village clusters across the state. His visits had been informal. He had not spoken at public assemblies. He had come to individual households — mostly households with sons or daughters in the 15-22 age bracket — and had sat at the kitchen table for an hour or two, drinking tea, asking about the young person’s plans, listening. His pitch, repeated in roughly the same form at each household, was: “The mountain is open. Phawngpui’s eastern face has a fissure that I have been clearing. The fissure leads to a chamber large enough to hold six hundred. The chamber is the refuge from the inheritance. The young who wish to refuse tlawmngaihna may come to the chamber and sit. The chamber will protect them while their kingdom argues. The protection will end when the kingdom has resolved the argument. I will provide rations — rice, dal, tea, fresh water from the mountain’s southern spring. I am not asking for a fee. I am offering refuge. Your son may come if he wishes. Your daughter may come if she wishes. The mountain is open until the new-moon week. The new-moon week is approximately four weeks from today.” The pitch was articulate. It was, in plain political-language terms, the most generous offer any Mizo asura had ever made in the kingdom’s recorded history. It was also strategically perfect — Parvata-Asura had read the Refusing Youth Manifesto’s clauses three weeks before they were published; he had calibrated the offer to attract the exact cohort the Manifesto would politicise. Six hundred and twenty-nine Mizos between 15 and 22 had accepted the offer by the new-moon week of week four. They had walked to Phawngpui Mountain from all thirteen villages over the preceding nine days, in small family-supervised groups, with their parents’ permission in approximately seventy per cent of cases and against their parents’ wishes in the remaining thirty. They had entered the eastern-face fissure on the night of the new-moon at the asura’s invitation. The fissure closed at dawn. THE LOCKING The first morning of week five. Six-fourteen a.m. The Phawngpui Mountain Sanctuary’s eastern ridge access-point. The Mizoram Forest Department’s dawn-patrol ranger, an Mizo woman named Kima-Lalremruati, has been on the standard pre-monsoon patrol route since five a.m. She walks up to the eastern face at six-fourteen a.m. and sees that the fissure that had been an open vertical crack three metres high and one metre wide the previous day — the crack she had logged in the Forest Department’s weekly register for the past seven months — has closed. The granite is now continuous, undifferentiated, with no visible seam. The eastern face looks exactly as it did before Parvata-Asura began clearing the fissure in spring 2025. She approaches the granite. She places her right palm on the closed surface. She feels heat — the granite is warm to the touch, approximately thirty-eight degrees Celsius, which is anomalous for granite at this altitude in this season. She knocks. The knock produces a hollow resonant sound consistent with a substantial cavity behind the granite, not with solid stone. She walks back down the ridge to her patrol-station at six-thirty a.m. and radios in the observation. The radio operator at Aizawl District Forest Office logs the call at six-thirty-three. The District Forest Officer reaches Tlangvala at his Aizawl residence by telephone at six-fifty-one. Tlangvala is at the kingdom-council emergency meeting by seven-thirty. THE KINGDOM-COUNCIL EMERGENCY MEETING Seven-thirty a.m. The Mizoram State Secretariat’s tribal-council chamber. The chamber holds twenty-four seats around an oval table. Twenty-two are filled this morning — the eight District Tribal Council Chairpersons, the four Aizawl Presbyterian Synod senior moderators, the Mizoram Forest Department’s senior officer, the State Civil Service’s chief secretary, the eight Refusing Youth representatives (admitted by emergency vote on day one of week five over Ramdinmawia’s formal objection), and the kingdom-hero Tlangvala. Two seats are empty: the Bangalore-diaspora Mizo representative is in transit and the Champhai District Chair is at his daughter’s wedding in Singapore. Lalruatpuia is one of the eight Refusing Youth representatives. He has been admitted as the Aizawl-district representative on the strength of his Manifesto-author standing. He sits at his father’s left side at the council table. They have not spoken since December. They do not speak now. They look at the surveyor’s diagram of Phawngpui’s eastern face that the Forest Department has projected on the chamber’s far wall. Kima-Lalremruati the dawn-patrol ranger is at the meeting in observer-capacity. She presents the morning’s patrol-log at seven-forty-three a.m. The presentation takes about eight minutes. She concludes with the count: “Six hundred and twenty-nine. The intake-registers I have been keeping at the eastern-face access-point since spring confirm the count. The youngest is fifteen. The oldest is twenty-two. The locked include forty-seven from Aizawl-Lalruatpuia’s own constituency, eighty-three from Lunglei, fifty-one from Champhai, thirty-eight from Saiha, twenty-nine from Mamit, forty-four from Kolasib, sixty-eight from Lawngtlai, seventy-seven from Serchhip, plus the rest from sub-village clusters and the Bangalore-Delhi-Chennai-Singapore diaspora. The intake-registers have been kept under the standard Forest Department voluntary-camping-permit framework. No one has been compelled to enter. All have entered under their own signatures. The locking has occurred after their entry; the locking is unilateral on Parvata-Asura’s side; the locked have not signed any consent-form authorising the closure of the fissure.” The chamber is silent for about twenty seconds. Ramdinmawia, the senior tribal-council chair, speaks first. He says, in Mizo, in his slow administrative-elder voice: “The asura has demanded one perfect sacrifice. I have received the demand at four a.m. this morning by a Mizo-language Sarus-pigeon. The Sarus-pigeon is unconfirmed as Parvata-Asura’s — we do not yet know if the asura has the institutional standing to operate a pigeon-network — but the message itself is unambiguous. The fissure will remain closed until the kingdom delivers one perfect sacrifice. The sacrifice must enter the eastern face alone. The sacrifice will become part of the mountain. The locked will be released at the moment the sacrifice completes. The kingdom may select the sacrifice. The asura specifies only that the sacrifice must be voluntary, must be adult, must have completed a recognised Mizo bond-mark of any stage. The asura recommends the sacrifice be the kingdom’s most recognisable carrier of tlawmngaihna. The asura’s recommendation is a name. The name is Tlangvala.” The chamber is silent for about ten seconds longer. Tlangvala says, in plain Mizo, in his ordinary speaking voice: “Yes. I will go.” Lalruatpuia stands up at his chair. He does not speak. He walks out of the chamber. The door closes behind him at seven-fifty-three a.m. THE COUNCIL’S DELIBERATION The deliberation that follows takes seven days. The chamber meets twice daily, three hours each session. The Refusing Youth representatives raise the objection that the asura’s demand is itself the trap — that delivering Tlangvala validates the inheritance-logic the Manifesto rejects. The senior tribal-council delegates raise the counter-objection that refusing the demand leaves six hundred and twenty-nine youth locked indefinitely. The Aizawl Presbyterian Synod moderators raise the third-position objection that the kingdom must refuse both alternatives and instead find a way to break the locking by counter-action. The counter-action options are explored in detail. They are: (1) physical excavation of the eastern face (rejected on day three; the Forest Department’s geological survey confirms the granite is now bonded to the mountain’s deeper plutonic mass; excavation would require explosives at a scale that would kill the locked); (2) negotiation with Parvata-Asura through the four other northeast catalyst-villains (rejected on day four; the asura is not in coordination with Tamasa-Nrita, Megha-Daitya, or Naga-Rakshasa — he is operating as Chhinlung-Naag’s standalone successor); (3) cross-kingdom request to the saga’s First Herald via the Roing Dawn-Shadow Temple (raised on day five by the Lawngtlai District Chair; rejected on day six because the Herald-naming protocol explicitly forbids invoking a Herald to break a Maw-aligned action); (4) the kingdom-hero’s entry, the asura’s demand fulfilled, the locking ended. The vote on day seven is twenty-to-two in favour of option four. The two dissenting votes are Lalruatpuia’s and the Refusing Youth’s Lunglei representative. The vote is closed at five p.m. The kingdom-council then takes a second vote — the unprecedented vote that has not been taken in any Mizo tribal-council in the kingdom’s recorded history — on whether to formally request that Tlangvala not enter the mountain. The vote is taken at Ramdinmawia’s motion, seconded by the Aizawl Presbyterian Synod moderator Lalrinpuii. The vote is fifteen-to-seven. The kingdom-council formally requests that Tlangvala not return from the mountain. The formal request is given to Tlangvala at six p.m. He receives the written document. He reads it. He says, in plain Mizo: “The council has done what the council must do. I will go anyway. The going is mine. The request is yours. We can both have what we have asked for.” THE FATHER-AND-SON CONVERSATION That evening. Tlangvala’s house in the eastern Aizawl hills. Eight p.m. Lalruatpuia has not been at the house since December. He returns this evening because Hmangaihi has telephoned him from the aunt’s house and told him to come. She has not given him a reason. He has come because she has not given a reason; the absence of reason has been a sufficient summons. Tlangvala is in the kitchen. He is making tea. He does not turn when Lalruatpuia enters. He says, into the kettle: “The tea is the second steeping. The first steeping is too strong this evening. Will you sit.” Lalruatpuia sits. Tlangvala pours two cups of tea. He sets one in front of his son. He sits across the table. He says: “The council has voted. The vote is twenty-to-two. The kingdom has also formally requested that I not return. The two votes are honest. The going is honest. I will go at first light on Tuesday.” Lalruatpuia says: “The Manifesto is correct. You are validating the inheritance-logic by going. The locking will end. The next asura will lock the next generation and demand the next perfect sacrifice. The pattern will repeat. The kingdom will never reach the administration of the creed because the creed will keep being weaponised for its inheritance.” Tlangvala says: “The Manifesto is correct. You are correct. The going is also correct. The two correctnesses are not the same correctness. The Manifesto is the kingdom’s long correctness — the answer to the next ten generations. The going is the kingdom’s short correctness — the answer to the six hundred and twenty-nine locked this week. You are responsible for the long correctness. I am responsible for the short correctness. We have divided the responsibility. The division is the kingdom’s, not ours.” Lalruatpuia says: “You are leaving me the kingdom.” Tlangvala says: “I am leaving you the kingdom’s political seat. I am leaving Hmangaihi the bond. The two of you will share the kingdom — she carries the serow, you carry the council. You will need each other. You have not always agreed; you have always understood. You will understand each other now.” Lalruatpuia says: “Why Hmangaihi.” Tlangvala says: “Because the serow chooses her, not me. The bond moves to whoever the line offers. The line offered her at her mother’s landslide-death eight years ago; I did not honour the offer at the time; the bond stayed with me because I refused to refuse. The bond has been waiting eight years to move. The bond will move when I become the mountain. Hmangaihi will be at the western trailhead on Tuesday morning. The serow will walk down to her after I am gone. The Stage I hoofprint will form within two days. She will understand it before I do not exist any more — her understanding is the bond’s acceptance. The bond does not need my permission. The bond is honest. I am also honest. The going is honest.” Lalruatpuia is silent for about a minute. He says: “I do not forgive you for going.” Tlangvala says: “I do not require forgiveness. I require that you do the kingdom’s long correctness with Hmangaihi’s bond at your right side. You will. The Manifesto will be administered, not enforced. The clauses will be rewritten by the joint-signature you and Hmangaihi will compose on the day after my funeral. You will pass the clauses by the kingdom-council’s ratification vote within six weeks. The vote will be eighteen-to-six. Ramdinmawia will vote in favour; he will retire from the senior chair the following week. Lalrinpuii will succeed him. The kingdom will be ready to administer the creed by the new year. The going has bought you that.” Lalruatpuia does not respond. He drinks his tea. The tea is the right strength. He says, finally: “Tuesday morning. I will be at the eastern ridge.” Tlangvala says: “No. You will be at the western trailhead with Hmangaihi. The eastern ridge is mine alone. The western trailhead is the kingdom’s. The kingdom watches from the west. The asura takes the east. The bond walks down between the two. That is the geography. The kingdom-council has approved the geography by unanimous vote. Lalrinpuii drafted it last night.” Lalruatpuia nods. He stands. He walks to the door. At the door he turns and says, in plain Mizo: “Father. The bamboo bends.” Tlangvala says: “The mountain does not bend. The mountain holds the bamboo’s shape inside its own. We have chosen which we are. Go and sleep, my son. Tuesday is the day after tomorrow.” ═══ INTERVAL ═══ THE WALK INTO GRANITE Tuesday. The third day of week eleven. First light at the Phawngpui Mountain’s eastern face is timed for five-thirty-one a.m. The kingdom has assembled at the western trailhead since four a.m. — approximately thirty-eight thousand Mizos, drawn from all thirteen villages, the Refusing Youth’s full membership, the senior tribal-council delegates, the Aizawl Presbyterian Synod, the Mizoram Forest Department’s full staff, the State Civil Service, the locked six-hundred-and-twenty-nine’s families. The trailhead opens onto a long ridge that runs west of the mountain at approximately five hundred metres lower elevation; the eastern face is visible from the ridge at a distance of about three kilometres horizontally. Tlangvala arrives at the eastern face at five-twenty-eight a.m. He has walked the four-kilometre trail from the eastern access-station alone, in plain Mizo wool, blue and grey, no clan-pattern (he has dressed in his son’s Manifesto-style); he carries the bamboo staff-spear Hnam-Tuk in his right hand. He carries nothing else. He has eaten a single bowl of rice at three a.m. He has drunk one cup of tea at four a.m. He has had three hours’ sleep. He stops at the granite face. He places his right palm on the warm granite. The granite is now forty-one degrees Celsius — warmer than the previous week’s thirty-eight, consistent with the asura’s readying-of-the-chamber for the sacrifice. The fissure is still closed. He says, into the granite, in plain Mizo: “I have come. I am the one perfect sacrifice. The locked will be released at the completion. The kingdom watches from the west. I do not ask for the kingdom’s permission. I have the kingdom’s request that I not return. I accept the request. The going is mine.” The granite warms further. At five-thirty-one a.m. — the moment of first-light at the eastern face — the granite opens. The fissure forms as it did in spring 2025: a vertical crack three metres high and one metre wide. Inside the fissure is darkness. Tlangvala steps through. The fissure closes behind him at five-thirty-one-twelve a.m. The granite is continuous again. The kingdom watches from the western trailhead. Approximately thirty-eight thousand Mizos see the fissure open, the man step through, the fissure close. The seeing is at three kilometres distance through high-power binoculars and through the naked eye. The seeing is reported in the same way by every observer: the man entered the granite and the granite closed. For the next forty-three minutes the eastern face is unmarked. THE MARK ON THE MOUNTAIN At six-fourteen a.m. the eastern face begins to change. The change starts at the upper-left section of the face, approximately seventy metres above the closed-fissure location. A pattern begins to form in the granite — not painted, not carved, but emerging from within the granite itself, as if the rock’s mineral composition were rearranging. The pattern is recognisable to the kingdom’s senior tribal-council elders within ninety seconds: it is the bull-serow’s right forelimb in mid-stride. The pattern grows. Across the next eighteen minutes the full Stage III serow-foreleg mark forms on the eastern granite. The mark is forty-seven metres tall when complete. It is rendered in the same ink-density as a body-mark would be on a hero’s skin — the serow’s hoof-impression at the lower edge, the foreleg arching upward, the bull’s shoulder-curve at the upper edge, the head-in-profile at the topmost section with the curved horn cresting at the topmost-left point. The mark is fully visible from the western trailhead and from the twenty-three-kilometre satellite-imagery range. The Mizoram Forest Department’s drone-survey at seven a.m. confirms the mark is structurally part of the granite — not surface, not paint, not relief; the rock itself has been recomposed. At six-thirty-two a.m. the southern fissure opens. The southern fissure is at the opposite end of the mountain from the closed eastern fissure — approximately one and a half kilometres around the mountain’s circumference. It opens as a wide horizontal mouth-shape, twelve metres wide and four metres high. The locked six hundred and twenty-nine youth begin to walk out of it at six-thirty-five. The exit takes eleven minutes. None of the locked is harmed. Forty-seven of them are dehydrated and three are mildly hypothermic from the mountain’s interior cold; none requires hospitalisation. The Mizoram State Civil Service’s emergency-medical convoy is at the southern exit by six-fifty a.m.; the medics process the released youth at the rate of one every seven minutes; by ten a.m. all six hundred and twenty-nine are accounted for and being escorted back to their families on the western ridge. Parvata-Asura does not emerge through the southern fissure. He is partially dissolved by the same dissolution that formed the eastern mark. The Forest Department’s subsequent thermal survey of the mountain over the next month detects faint residual mountain-asura presence in the deep southern range — the dissolved remainder of his form retreats toward the Chin-state border-zone (the same border-zone Tamasa-Nrita and Naga-Rakshasa crossed earlier in the chapter). Parvata-Asura survives in depleted form. Per the wider villain-network pattern he becomes Adharma-Kendra’s fourth NE field-operative in Chapter Three. THE SEROW WALKS DOWN Hmangaihi has been at the western trailhead with Lalruatpuia since four a.m. They have stood together at the ridge’s northern edge. She has been watching the eastern face through her father’s old Forest Department binoculars (her late father had been a deputy district forest officer; the binoculars are her inheritance). Lalruatpuia has been watching with his naked eye. They have not spoken much during the four-hour wait. At seven-forty-three a.m., as the kingdom’s assembled witness-crowd begins to disperse and the locked-youth-recovery convoy begins to descend, a wild serow walks down the western ridge from the mountain’s southern slope. The serow is a mature bull, approximately seven years old, weight estimated at ninety-two kilograms. It walks across the ridge at its standard pace — one and a half metres per second — in a direct line toward Hmangaihi’s position. It stops at her right side at seven-fifty-three a.m. It lowers its head. She places her right palm on its forehead. She does not say anything. The Stage I serow-hoofprint forms on her right shoulder over the next forty-eight hours. THE FUNERAL PROCESSION The funeral procession is held three days later at the Phawngpui Mountain Sanctuary’s southern approach — the same approach the locked youth had used for their exit. The body cannot be retrieved (the body is part of the mountain now; the eastern mark is the visible form of the body’s incorporation). The procession is therefore a sky-burial-equivalent procession — the kingdom processes a symbolic empty bamboo bier up the southern approach to the mark’s base, performs the standard Mizo Presbyterian funeral rites with the Synod’s senior moderator officiating, and leaves the bier at the base of the mark as a permanent memorial. The bier is a single length of bamboo, six metres long, cut from the bamboo grove behind Tlangvala’s house by Hmangaihi and Lalruatpuia together the night before the procession. They cut the bamboo from the standing-stem (they did not fell the parent plant). The bamboo’s upper third bends naturally to about thirty-five degrees — the standard Mizo bamboo-bend — from the weight of its own crown. They leave the bend in the bier. The bend is the procession’s answer to the mountain. The kingdom-council’s senior chair Ramdinmawia speaks at the bier’s base at the procession’s conclusion. His statement is brief. He says, in plain Mizo: “The mountain holds the man. The bend is the kingdom. The man chose the mountain. The kingdom chooses the bend. The Manifesto will be ratified at the kingdom-council’s emergency session at six p.m. on Friday. Lalruatpuia and Hmangaihi have my chair’s endorsement. I am retiring from the senior chair effective the moment of the ratification vote. Lalrinpuii of the Synod will succeed me. The kingdom will administer the creed from the new year. The bamboo bends and the bamboo springs back. The bamboo does not always become the mountain. Sometimes the mountain becomes the bamboo. The kingdom learns the difference at the next dawn.” The procession disperses by sunset. The bier remains at the mark’s base. The mark is visible from the western trailhead and from the twenty-three-kilometre satellite-imagery range; it will remain so as long as the granite holds its current composition, which the Forest Department’s geological assessment estimates at “not less than eight hundred years.” ═══ SECOND HALF ═══ THE RATIFICATION Friday evening. The kingdom-council’s emergency session at the Mizoram State Secretariat tribal-council chamber. The chamber is full — twenty-four of twenty-four seats. The two previously-absent representatives (Bangalore-diaspora and Champhai) are now present. Hmangaihi sits at the left side of the table’s northern end. She is in plain Mizo wool with the senior tribal-council’s neutral grey shawl over her shoulders. She has not previously held a council seat (she is seventeen; the senior council’s minimum age is twenty-one); she is admitted by emergency motion as the bond-bearer of the kingdom’s state-hero-line, a category that supersedes the age-rule under Mizo customary law. The motion passes by unanimous vote at six-oh-two p.m. Lalruatpuia sits at her right side. He is in plain Mizo wool, blue and grey, no clan-pattern. He carries the bamboo staff-spear Hnam-Tuk — the spear is his father’s inheritance, formally transferred at the funeral procession’s bier by Ramdinmawia’s spoken declaration. The spear is light in his hand. It will be lighter the more he uses it. The joint-signature Manifesto is on the table in front of them. Three a.m. The southern slope of Phawngpui Mountain. A wild serow has been at the same southern grazing-meadow for the past eleven days — the same area the locked youth had emerged from. The serow is a doe, approximately five years old, in her second pregnancy. She has been observed by the Mizoram Forest Department’s southern patrol-station’s rangers (the senior shrine-keeper for the Phawngpui Sanctuary, a Mizo ranger named Vanlalruati, plus two junior rangers) since her arrival at the meadow. She gives birth at three-oh-two a.m. The labour is normal up to the moment of expulsion. The kid that emerges has two fully-formed heads on a single body. Both heads breathe. Both heads open their eyes. The body is otherwise normal — one set of four legs, one torso, one tail, normal serow-kid colouring and proportions. The two heads share a single neck-base; they branch at approximately the level of the kid’s shoulder-blades into two distinct skull-and-jaw structures. Vanlalruati and the two junior rangers observe from approximately six metres distance through their night-vision optics. The doe grooms the kid in the standard serow post-natal manner. The kid attempts to stand at three-fourteen a.m. It succeeds at three-twenty-one. It walks at three-thirty. Its mother nurses it. Both heads drink simultaneously without interference. The kid’s presence at the meadow is logged in the rangers’ field-book at four a.m. The rangers do not approach. They radio the observation to the Aizawl District Forest Office at four-thirty. The District Forest Officer reaches Hmangaihi by telephone at five-fifteen. Hmangaihi and Lalruatpuia are at the southern patrol-station by ten a.m. They observe the kid from the same six-metre distance through the rangers’ optics. The kid is grazing alongside its mother. Both heads graze. The heads do not interfere with each other; they appear to coordinate their feeding-patterns through some mechanism the rangers cannot identify. The mother appears unbothered by the kid’s configuration. Lalruatpuia says, to Hmangaihi, in Mizo: “The Maw has experimented. The body is normal; the heads are doubled. The serow-bond is what is being tested.” Hmangaihi says: “Yes. Vanlalruati-la, how long does the rangers’ assessment expect the kid to live.” Vanlalruati says: “The veterinary-officer’s preliminary assessment is twelve to thirty-six hours. The two-head configuration in any large-mammal species typically fails at the level of the shared cardiovascular and digestive systems — the heads’ coordinated demands exceed the single body’s capacity within a day. The kid is unusually vigorous so far; if the heads are sharing systems efficiently it might live longer, but we have no precedent for serow two-head observations in the field.” The kid lives for approximately twenty-three hours. Throughout the daylight hours of the following day the rangers, Hmangaihi, and Lalruatpuia observe at the same six-metre distance. The kid grazes, walks, nurses, sleeps. Both heads remain conscious. They appear to be capable of looking in different directions independently — when one head turns south, the other can turn west. They do not appear to speak. They breathe in synchrony. They blink in synchrony when both faces are visible to the observers. At one-fifty-eight a.m. on the next morning — approximately twenty-three hours after birth — the kid stops grazing. It walks to the centre of the meadow. It stops. It lowers its body to a kneeling position. Its mother stays at its right side. The three rangers, Hmangaihi, and Lalruatpuia are at the meadow’s southern edge at this moment; they have been keeping the same six-metre vigil since the previous evening. Both heads raise. Both pairs of eyes open fully. Both heads face Hmangaihi’s position. Both heads speak together. They speak in plain Mizo. They speak a single word. The word is “Hmangaihi.” The word is audible to all five observers at the six-metre distance. Vanlalruati subsequently confirms (after multiple replays of her body-camera audio recording) that both voices were sharply distinct — one slightly higher-pitched, one slightly lower, neither resembling a standard serow vocalisation — and that the synchrony was within fifty milliseconds, consistent with two distinct vocal-tracts speaking in coordinated production rather than a single tract producing a doubled effect. The kid lowers its heads four minutes after speaking. It dies six minutes after that. Its mother grooms the body for an hour. She then walks down the southern slope toward Hmangaihi’s village at a steady pace. The mother does not acknowledge the rangers, Hmangaihi, or Lalruatpuia during her descent. She arrives at the village outskirts at six a.m. She stops at the edge of the village. She lies down. She stays at that position for three days. The villagers leave food and water at the edge of her resting-place. She does not eat or drink. On day four she stands, walks back up the southern slope toward the meadow, and is not seen in the village again. The kid’s body is recovered by the Forest Department’s veterinary team at three a.m. on the morning after the death. The body is preserved in formaldehyde at the Aizawl District Forest Department’s zoological-museum sub-collection, in the same configuration in which it died. The body is logged as “Two-headed serow kid, Phawngpui southern slope, post-natal death at 23 hours, observed speech at 23 hours minus 4 minutes, single word, ‘Hmangaihi.’” The body is not on public display. Only senior Forest Department staff, the senior tribal-council members, Hmangaihi, Lalruatpuia, and Lalrinpuii of the Synod have access to the sub-collection.
