The Epic Saga

The Patrilineal Storm — A Film in Two Halves
═══ FIRST HALF ═══ OPENING — SHILLONG IN AUTUMN RAIN Meghanadi’s ancestral house at Laitumkhrah. October. The early monsoon has tapered to long thin afternoon rain that the Khasi call u-ksing-snie — the rain that drums but does not flood. The house is the kind of Khasi matrilineal house where the youngest daughter inherits and where the oldest aunt holds the kitchen. There are five rooms. There is a fire in the kitchen at every hour of every day in the colder months. There is a brass jug of jadoh — rice steamed in pork-blood stock — on the hearth. Meghanadi is at the kitchen table. She is thirty-seven. She is wearing the storm-grey silk jainsem her grandmother wove forty years ago. Her hair is loose. She has been reading the morning mail. The morning mail today is a single letter. The letter is on the cream stationery of the Meghalaya High Court at Shillong. The seal is the state-government green. The letter informs Ka Meghanadi, head of the Megha-Maata kingdom under the Sixth Schedule Customary Authority Recognition Act 1962, that a writ petition has been filed against the kingdom by one Megha-Daitya, Advocate, of Guwahati. The writ is appended. The writ is forty-seven pages long. She reads the first paragraph twice. She reads the last paragraph. She sets the letter on the table. She picks up the jug. She pours herself jadoh. The hill myna in the sacred grove behind the house sings the long-call that means rain coming. THE WRIT The forty-seven pages are the cleanest legal argument the Meghalaya High Court has received in decades. Megha-Daitya’s thesis is that the Sixth Schedule’s protection of Khasi customary law does not override the Indian Succession Act on personal-property questions when the claimant is the patrilineal son of a Khasi woman who married outside the tribe and whose property — specifically her ancestral claim to her family’s Ka Khadduh inheritance — would have descended to her under Khasi customary law had she stayed and then to her son under the Indian Succession Act because she did not. The argument is internally consistent. The argument is also, to the extent the question is unsettled in Indian constitutional law, plausible. Three Meghalaya advocates have already declined to argue against it; two of them on the grounds that they think Megha-Daitya might win. Meghanadi reads the writ in full that evening. She does not weep. She does not curse. She closes the file. She walks to the room where her grandmother’s photographs are on the wall and stands in front of the photograph from 1973 of the household’s last formal Ka Khadduh ceremony. She stands there for an hour. She does not yet know what she will do. She does not yet know what she should do. She does what she has done for nine years — she does not decide. She waits for the council to decide. She is the kingdom’s protector by political seat; she is, in her own private estimation, the kingdom’s servant by dharma. THE WAVE Within four weeks Megha-Daitya files forty-six more petitions. Each is filed by a different patrilineal claimant. Each targets a different Khasi matrilineal household. The petitions are coordinated — same template, same exhibits-format, same lead counsel (Megha-Daitya himself for the lead petition; junior counsel from his Mumbai firm for the secondary forty-six). The Meghalaya High Court’s docket is overwhelmed within a fortnight. The petitioners are not all extremists. Some are middle-aged Assamese men who simply want recognition of their Khasi mothers’ line and a small share of the matrilineal property — honourable, articulable, dharmically defensible. Some are younger and angrier — men who feel they have been denied a tribal-identity claim and want full revocation of the matrilineal-inheritance custom. Megha-Daitya organises both wings. The honourable petitioners think they are filing for recognition. The angry petitioners think they are filing for revolution. Megha-Daitya knows what he himself is filing for. The Council of Matrilineal Elders convenes in special session. The fracture begins in the first half-hour. Ka Phidamon-the-Elder — ninety-four years old, the council’s longest-serving voice — refuses to engage the petitions at all. “The Sixth Schedule is the answer. The court will rule for us. There is nothing for this council to do.” The Garo-Hills representative U-Lyngdoh argues for engaging the petitions through a Mixed Council framework. Three other elders argue for various intermediate positions. Meghanadi sits at the head of the council table. She does not take a position. She listens. The council deliberates for nine days. The deliberation reaches no conclusion. THE BRIDGE AT NONGRIAT Week three. Sunday morning. Pre-dawn. The double-decker living root bridge of Nongriat — the famous Khasi engineered-tree-root bridge of the southern hills, the bridge built across the Umshiang gorge over a hundred and fifty years from interleaved ficus elastica roots, the bridge that every Khasi child crosses at age six as part of the kingdom’s informal coming-of-age. Ka-Sngi is on the upper deck. She is twenty-six. She is the Ka Khadduh of Meghanadi’s family’s main house. She has come to Nongriat at dawn because she has come to Nongriat every Sunday at dawn for nine years — she meditates on the bridge for thirty minutes, then walks back up the gorge to the village. The Khasi tradition of iaroh-snong — the dawn-walk at a living-bridge — is one of the matrilineal household’s gentler rituals. She is not alone this Sunday. Her half-brother Kong-Bah — petitioner number eleven on Megha-Daitya’s coordinated docket, the petitioner whose suit named the family’s ancestral house — is on the bridge too. He is fifty-one. He is half-Khasi by their shared mother’s side, raised by his Assamese father after their mother’s remarriage and death. He has been waiting for the right Sunday for two months. He pushes her into the gorge at 5:47 a.m. The fall is sixty-two metres. The investigation by the Mawkhanu police records the cause of death as “accidental fall, slippery monsoon root-surface.” The recording is in the standard police-form template. The half-brother walks back up the gorge by the same path the dead cousin would have walked. He returns to Shillong by mid-afternoon and files an additional brief amendment with his petition. Meghanadi is told at 7:30 a.m. by the Mawkhanu sub-divisional officer over the satellite phone. She has had an anonymous warning four days earlier — an unsigned note slipped under her door — that named Kong-Bah and named the Nongriat bridge. She did not act on the warning because she did not want to dishonour the council’s ongoing deliberation. She did not warn Ka-Sngi. She did not assign protection. She sat with the warning in her drawer for four days and read it twice each day and said nothing. At 7:31 a.m. she puts on the storm-grey jainsem. She walks to Ka-Sngi’s family’s house at the eastern Khasi Hills. She breaks the news to Ka-Sngi’s twelve-year-old sister Ka-Sienlyngdoh. She holds the girl while she cries. She does not cry herself. She walks back to her own house at dusk. She stands in front of her grandmother’s 1973 photograph again. She stands there for the entire night. She does not weep until first light. THE GARO ELDER AT THE DOOR The morning after Ka-Sngi’s funeral at Nongriat. Meghanadi’s house. Six a.m. A Garo elder is at the kitchen door. She is seventy-one. She is wearing a Garo nokma’s wrap in deep indigo. She is leaning on a walnut staff. She has walked from her cousin’s house in Cherrapunji where she has been staying for two weeks waiting for the right hour. Her name is U-Mawkynrew. Meghanadi opens the door. The two women have not met formally. The two women have known of each other for fifteen years. U-Mawkynrew says, in Khasi: "Ka Meghanadi. May I come in. I have a thing to tell you that I have been preparing to tell you for nine years." Meghanadi steps aside. U-Mawkynrew comes in. She sits at the kitchen table. She accepts a cup of red-tea with no sugar. She unrolls a fourteen-page paper from her shawl. The paper is a settlement-framework draft. The draft is in Khasi-Garo bilingual cross-column format. The draft has been worked on by U-Mawkynrew and her great-nephew U-Khrawkmen for the past two years — predating the patrilineal-petition wave, drafted in anticipation of it. She does not show the draft yet. She drinks her tea. She looks at Meghanadi. She says, in plain Khasi: "Ka, you think your silence is devotion. Your silence is just silence. The matrilineal line you protect does not need you to disappear into it. It needs you to speak for it. Devotion requires voice. Your cousin died because you would not speak." Meghanadi does not look away. She says, after a long silence: "Yes." U-Mawkynrew nods once. She unrolls the draft. She sets it on the table. She slides it across. "Read it. We have been waiting for you to be ready to read it. Today you are ready." ═══ INTERVAL ═══ THE READING Meghanadi reads the draft for the rest of the morning. U-Mawkynrew waits. The hill myna in the sacred grove sings twice. The afternoon rain begins. The draft is a three-part settlement framework. Part one preserves the Khasi matrilineal-property descent intact for all land-property and ancestral-house claims — the Sixth Schedule protection holds. Part two creates a new category of recognition-without-property for blood-Khasi patrilineal claimants whose mothers were Khasi by birth: they become honorary clan-members with restricted rights (clan-record inclusion, ritual-attendance, ceremonial-naming) but no inheritance. Part three creates a Mixed Council of Khasi and Garo elders to adjudicate any disputed personal-law cases — especially custody, marriage-out validations, and the question of movable patrilineal-claim wealth (which the Garo tradition already handles). The draft is not perfect. The draft is plausible. Meghanadi puts the draft down. She says: "Mawkynrew-Aaee. This will pass." "It will pass if you speak for it." "I have not spoken in council for nine years." "That is the problem we are solving today, Ka. Not the petitions. The petitions are the symptom." THE COUNCIL SPEECH The next morning. The Council of Matrilineal Elders convenes in special session at the State Central Library auditorium. Three hundred elders present — the largest Khasi-Garo council assembly since 1972. The press are present. Two national news-channels are present. The university constitutional-law department is present in a row at the back. Meghanadi enters in the storm-grey jainsem. She carries the Megha-Dand — the cloud-staff of her line, four feet of polished bamboo with the pale-jade hill-myna at the grip. She has not carried the staff in council in nine years. The Council elders who have served beside her for those nine years all see it at the same moment when she enters. She walks to the central speaker’s position. She sets the staff against the table. She speaks. The speech is twenty-one minutes long. It begins: “Honoured elders. I have not spoken in this council for nine years because I believed silence was the form of my devotion to the matrilineal line. My niece-cousin Ka-Sngi died seven days ago because of my belief. I will speak now. I will speak for the rest of my life. I will speak even on the days I would prefer not to. “The Khasi matrilineal line is older than the Indian Succession Act and older than the Constitution that conditionally protects it. We do not preserve our line by hiding from challenges to it. We preserve our line by answering challenges with the same patience and the same precision we used to build the line in the first place. The patrilineal petitioners have asked a question. We have a duty to give them an answer.” She reads the settlement framework. She reads all fourteen pages aloud. She does not paraphrase. She does not summarise. She reads each clause — the property-protection, the recognition-without-property, the Mixed Council jurisdiction — in the formal council-Khasi register her grandmother taught her. The council deliberates for two days. The settlement passes on the third morning. Eighty-six in favour, thirty-one opposed, four abstentions. Megha-Daitya, observing in the press gallery on the third morning, walks out of the auditorium when the vote is announced. He does not return to his Mumbai law-firm hotel. He goes directly to a safe-house on the outskirts of Shillong. ═══ SECOND HALF ═══ THE ORGANISING The two weeks after the council vote. Megha-Daitya makes his second move. He does not file an appeal. The settlement was a state-customary-council action, not a court ruling — appellate review would require a separate writ. He does not have time. He does not want time. He has been preparing the second move for months. He approaches twenty-three of the most radical patrilineal petitioners. He explains: the settlement gives them recognition-without-property. The settlement does not give them what they came for. The settlement is a delaying tactic. The matrilineal council will use the settlement to drag the property question into the Mixed Council indefinitely. By the time the Mixed Council rules on any individual patrilineal property claim, the original petitioner will be elderly or dead. The argument is partly true. The Mixed Council has not yet been constituted, and the constitution-process will take months. He proposes a faster path. He has weapons — modern submachine guns, two dozen, sourced through a Mumbai-Mizoram cross-border channel he has cultivated for two years. He has a target — the Meghalaya High Court at Shillong, where the original forty-seven writs are still pending and where the matrilineal-council settlement will need judicial ratification to bind future petitioners. The plan: attack the High Court during monsoon’s heaviest week, when the streets are flooded and the police presence is reduced. Seize the court’s docket-room. Burn the original writs. Burn the council’s settlement-document filed with the court. Force a new round of petitions under chaos conditions. Twenty-three petitioners agree. Twenty-four reject. The split is roughly proportional to age — the older claimants stay home; the younger ones come. THE STORM Monsoon’s heaviest week. The seventh week after the council vote. Late July. Shillong is at 1,400 mm of monthly rainfall and counting. Cherrapunji is at 2,800 mm. The streets of Shillong’s lower hills are flooded to thirty centimetres. The High Court compound is flooded to fifteen. Tuesday morning. 9:47 a.m. The patrilineal storm-faction — twenty-three armed petitioners under Megha-Daitya’s field command — arrives at the High Court compound in three vehicles. They breach the south gate within four minutes. The police presence is two constables, both of whom are immediately disarmed and bound. Meghanadi receives the alert at her ancestral house at 9:53 a.m. by satellite phone. The alert comes from the High Court’s under-clerk, a Khasi man named U-Banjop who has worked at the court for thirty-one years and who hides in the docket-room’s back stack while making the call. She does not call for police backup. She does not wait for the council-protection detail. She puts on the storm-grey jainsem. She picks up the Megha-Dand. She walks down the hill in the rain. The walk takes nineteen minutes. The rain is full-monsoon — the kind of rain that does not fall vertically because the wind drives it sideways. Her jainsem is soaked through within three minutes. She does not change her pace. The clouded leopard of her bond — in the eastern hills, two hundred kilometres away — is awake in her chest. The bond is pulsing. She arrives at the High Court compound south gate at 10:12 a.m. THE CHARGE Twenty-three storm-faction fighters in the court compound. Most are inside the building. Two are at the south gate, one inside the docket-room with the under-clerk U-Banjop still hiding in the back stack, and one with Megha-Daitya himself in the central courtyard. Megha-Daitya is in the courtyard. He has a submachine gun across his chest. He is reading aloud from the original writ to one of his lieutenants — the lieutenant is supposed to set the writ on fire, but Megha-Daitya wants the document read aloud first. He believes in the dignity of his own argument. He wants the document’s last hearing to be at his own voice. Meghanadi enters the courtyard at 10:15 a.m. She is alone. She is carrying the cloud-staff. Her hair has come loose; the storm-grey silk of the jainsem is plastered to her body. She looks like the cloud-mother carving on the temple wall at Mawphlang. She says, across the courtyard, in clear Khasi: "Daitya. Withdraw the storm. The settlement stands. You may petition the Mixed Council. You may not burn the document." Megha-Daitya looks up from the writ. He is forty-two and the rain has made his white shirt half-transparent. He has his mother’s Khasi-marriage photograph in his inside pocket — he has carried it for thirty-nine years. The photograph is sealed in plastic and is the only object on his person that the rain has not reached. He says: "Aunt. Last chance. Withdraw the settlement. Give us the full matrilineal property. The line is mine through my mother." She says, in the same plain Khasi tone she used at the council: "No." He raises the submachine gun. She charges. The charge is twenty-two metres of waterlogged courtyard. She moves through the rain in the long-stride storm-mother gait. The cloud-staff is held two-handed at chest height. The clouded leopard in the eastern hills is on its feet for the first time in twelve years — the bond is fully present now, the Stage III foreleg-mark forming across her chest in real time as she crosses the courtyard. She feels the mark complete at metre sixteen — the clouded-leopard’s curling tail across her right collarbone, the foreleg spreading down her chest, the head turned in profile across her sternum. He fires at metre eighteen. Four shots. All four hit her chest in a tight grouping above the new mark. She does not stop. She does not slow. The bond’s last function for her body is to keep the legs moving for four more metres. At metre twenty-two she reaches him. The cloud-staff enters his sternum at a forty-five-degree angle. The pale-jade hill-myna at the grip is the last point of contact between her hand and the staff — she is dead at that point but the body’s muscular memory of the storm-mother strike finishes the gesture. The staff passes through his sternum and exits his back. He is dead before the second second of her own dying. They fall together at the courtyard’s centre. The rain continues. The Megha-Dand stands upright in the courtyard floor, pinning his body to the stone, with the pale-jade hill-myna at its top and the storm-grey jainsem still fluttering in the rain at its base. The storm-faction’s remaining twenty-two fighters see what has happened from the courtyard’s upper windows. Three drop their weapons immediately. Eleven try to flee through the north gate — nine are caught by the police backup arriving at 10:24 a.m., two escape into the hills and are not found. Eight are caught in the docket-room with U-Banjop, who has emerged from the back stack with one of the dropped weapons and has held them at gunpoint for the eleven minutes between the courtyard-fall and the police-arrival. U-Banjop will be promoted to chief court-registrar within a month. His starting salary will be doubled. His name will be added to the Khasi clan-roll of the Mawkhar quarter, an honour he had not previously held. ═══ EPILOGUE ═══ THE RATIFICATION U-Khrawkmen arrives at the courtyard at 10:38 a.m. He has been at the Garo Hills District Council office in Tura when the storm broke; he has flown in by Pawan Hans helicopter on U-Mawkynrew’s order. He is twenty-eight. He is wearing the plain wool-and-cotton Mixed Council Mediator’s coat he has been wearing for six years. He kneels beside Meghanadi’s body. In the inner pocket of her jainsem is a sealed envelope addressed to him. The handwriting is hers. The seal is the Megha-Maata kingdom’s storm-grey wax. The contents are a single page. The page is dated three days earlier. U-Khrawkmen, If you are reading this, the storm has come and I have not survived it. The settlement we drafted together is the framework. The Mixed Council you have prepared for six years is the institution. The Khasi matrilineal line will continue because you will not let it stop. You are the inheritor of the political seat by my explicit wish, ratified under the Sixth Schedule customary procedure (witnessed: U-Mawkynrew, Vidura-Babu of UP via Sarus-pigeon, Kamalavarna of Assam via your great-aunt). You are not Khasi-matrilineal-bloodline. The clouded-leopard bond will not pass to you. It will stay with the line. It will settle on Ka-Sienlyngdoh when she is ready — she is ready before any of us know. Speak. Even on the days you would prefer not to. That is the whole of the inheritance. — M. U-Khrawkmen reads the letter. He folds it. He puts it in the inner pocket of his own coat. He stands up. By 11:30 a.m. the Council of Matrilineal Elders has been convened in emergency session at the State Central Library auditorium. By 12:15 p.m. U-Khrawkmen has been formally ratified as inheritor of the Megha-Maata political seat under a Sixth-Schedule-compliant customary-law procedure that U-Mawkynrew has been preparing alongside the settlement draft. Ka Phidamon-the-Elder, who had refused to engage the petitions in week one, casts her vote in favour. She says one sentence after her vote: “The line speaks now. The line continues.” THE FUNERAL Three days later. The Nongriat double-decker living root bridge. The same bridge where Ka-Sngi died ten weeks ago. Meghanadi’s body is wrapped in the storm-grey jainsem and laid on the bridge’s lower deck. The Megha-Dand — recovered from the High Court courtyard, the pale-jade hill-myna still intact — is laid beside her, wrapped in cloud-grey cloth. The clouded leopard of her line walks down out of the eastern hills at sunset. The leopard is twenty-eight years old — the kingdom’s elder bond-animal, the matriarch of the surviving Khasi-hills population. She walks across the upper deck of the bridge, looks down at the body, and lies down on the upper deck above. She stays there for the full ceremony. U-Khrawkmen reads the eulogy. He says: "Aunt-mother spoke at the end. She broke her silence. She broke it for the matrilineal line itself, not for any one woman, not even for herself. We will speak in her name from this day forward. The line that does not speak does not survive. We will speak." The eulogy is sixteen minutes long. The rain holds off for the full sixteen minutes — the only break in the monsoon-week’s rainfall. At the eulogy’s end the rain resumes. The clouded leopard rises, walks back across the upper deck, descends the gorge path, and disappears into the eastern hills before full dark. Ka-Sienlyngdoh, twelve years old, stands at the bridge’s eastern edge during the funeral. She does not yet carry the bond. She does not yet know she will carry it. The bond does not approach her yet. It is still on the upper deck where the leopard lay. It will descend to her in Chapter Three’s Beast-Rising at her own threshold — not at a funeral but at a doorway. THE DRY DAY The morning after the funeral. The first dry day in living-memory monsoon-season Cherrapunji. The rain stops at 5:47 a.m. — the exact minute Ka-Sngi died at Nongriat eleven weeks earlier. The rain does not resume for twenty-four hours. Cherrapunji’s rain-gauges record zero precipitation for July 30th, the only such reading in the gauges’ eighty-three-year history. Mawsynram records the same. Shillong records the same. The Mawphlang sacred grove’s hill mynas fall silent at 5:47 a.m. and remain silent for the same twenty-four hours. The locals name the day before noon. Ka-Rhang-Wat-Snie. The dry day of grief. By the evening the name has spread to every Khasi household in the state. U-Khrawkmen, in his first formal act as inheritor of the political seat, orders the day added to the state calendar as a permanent observance. The order is filed by 14:00. By 18:00 the order has been ratified by the Council of Matrilineal Elders, the Garo Hills District Council, and the Jaintia Hills District Council in concurrent emergency sessions. Three districts in unanimous concurrence on a single day — the first time in the state’s post-1972 history. The shrine is built at the Mawphlang sacred grove edge by week’s end. Sandstone, packed earth, a single cloud-pattern carved in low relief on the western face. The grove’s elder hill myna sings the dedication-song at the shrine’s opening — the first myna-song the grove has heard since the dry day began. THE CROSS-KINGDOM LETTER That same evening, at the Kamakhya temple in Assam, a hill myna arrives from Meghalaya. She carries a folded slip of paper in her beak. The handwriting is U-Khrawkmen’s. Kamalavarna-Aaee. Aunt-mother is dead. Megha-Daitya is dead. The storm broke at the High Court. I am the inheritor by Aunt-mother’s written wish. The clouded leopard did not take me — it stays with the line. Ka-Sienlyngdoh will carry the bond in time. I will hold the political seat until then. The settlement holds. The Mixed Council is forming. The patrilineal petitioners who did not join the storm have all accepted the framework. The state calendar has a new dry-day mark. I will need counsel. I will travel to Kamakhya before the next new moon to receive it. — U-K. Kamalavarna reads it at her co-priestess throne. Kama-Mara reads it over her shoulder. The mirror-marks on both sisters’ chests pulse once at the same beat — their cousin Meghanadi’s death registering in the Bloodline-C bond-network at a delay of three days. Kamalavarna lights a single oil lamp at the inner altar. She lights it for her cousin. The clouded leopard of the Kamakhya temple courtyard — a leopard not seen at Kamakhya for nine centuries — walks once around the lamp at midnight and disappears into the temple darkness. She writes the reply on the back of the same slip. She uses her sister’s coven-ink: U-Khrawkmen. Come at the new moon. Bring U-Mawkynrew. Bring the draft of the Mixed Council charter. Bring Ka-Sienlyngdoh if her family will allow her — the bond may settle sooner than you expect when she sees Kamakhya. We have a co-priestess throne at the temple now. We can offer counsel and shelter and a private library. The line is whole because your aunt-mother spoke. Speak. Even on the days you would prefer not to. That is the inheritance. — K. The hill myna carries the reply south at dawn.
The Pilot’s Closing Image
The Nongriat double-decker living root bridge at dusk. A twenty-eight-year-old man in a Mixed Council Mediator’s wool-and-cotton coat at the bridge’s apex with his great-aunt beside him. A twelve-year-old Khasi girl at the bridge’s eastern edge, not yet bonded. A clouded leopard’s warm-spot on the upper deck where she lay for the eulogy. The Megha-Dand cloud-staff wrapped in cloud-grey cloth set down at the bridge’s centre with the pale-jade hill-myna intact. The hill mynas in the Mawphlang sacred grove singing again after twenty-four hours of silence. In Cherrapunji, the rain returning at 5:47 a.m. exactly. In Kamakhya, a single oil lamp burning at the inner altar for a cousin three days dead. The line speaking. The line continuing.
