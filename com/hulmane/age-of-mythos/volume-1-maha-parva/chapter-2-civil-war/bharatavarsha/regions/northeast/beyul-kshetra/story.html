The Refugee Council — A Film in Two Halves
═══ FIRST HALF ═══ OPENING — THE INNER SANCTUM AT RUMTEK Rumtek Monastery, Gangtok district, twenty-four kilometres south-east of the city. The first week of October. The inner sanctum is a windowless stone chamber three metres by three metres at the back of the main prayer hall. Two butter-lamps burn at the north and south walls. A single low altar of black slate runs along the east wall. On the altar, in a square-cut wooden housing the size of a child’s hand, sits the Chintamani — the wish-fulfilling jewel of Avalokitesvara, the Beyul-Kshetra’s primary relic, the Hidden Land’s heart-stone, the thing that has remained inside this small chamber without a single hour’s absence since 1959. Kanchenjunga is at the altar at four-fifteen a.m. He is forty-one. He has been senior Beyul-guardian at Rumtek for nine years. He is in the dark-red guardian-robe with the seven-fold sash of the Karmapa lineage. His head is shaved. His hands are calloused. He has been in this chamber every dawn for three thousand two hundred and eighty-seven consecutive days. He sees the Chintamani in its housing. He places his right palm on the altar-edge in the morning’s first gesture of recognition. He does not touch the jewel. He has never touched it. No guardian has touched it in sixty-seven years. He turns and leaves the chamber. The day’s schedule is the same as every other day’s. Morning prayers at four-thirty. The refugee-blessing line at six. The Tuesday community-kitchen at ten. The Wednesday camp-rotation at the south camp at one. The Thursday and Friday camp-rotations at the central and west camps. The administrative council with the Sikkim state government at five on alternating weeks. At noon today the monastery will hold a Special Refugee-Blessing Ceremony for newly-arrived refugees from the autumn Tibetan-border surge. Two hundred and twelve refugees are scheduled to receive the blessing in three rotating batches. The Chintamani will be brought into the main prayer hall for the ceremony — not exposed, but carried in its housing on a small ceremonial palanquin from the inner sanctum to the central altar where it will sit visible (in housing) throughout the three-hour ceremony. This is the procedure. It has been the procedure for sixty-seven years. The Chintamani is brought out four times a year for major blessing-ceremonies. The autumn refugee-blessing is one of those four times. THE NUMBERS Twelve thousand. That is the count of refugees currently registered in the three south-Sikkim camps as of the morning of October the first. Approximately two thousand from the autumn Tibetan-border surge alone. Approximately five thousand long-standing Tibetan refugees from the 1959 wave and its descendants. Approximately two thousand from the 2008 Lhasa-unrest wave. Approximately one thousand from various Bhutan-border irregularities. Approximately one thousand identified as “ethnic-mixed” — refugees whose paperwork is unclear and who have therefore been registered as Beyul-Kshetra ward-residents under the monastery’s nine-century-old compassion-charter. The monastery has absorbed every one of them. The compassion-charter, originally written by Padmasambhava’s commentators in the eighth century and re-ratified by the Karmapa lineage in 1962, admits no refugee-rejection on any ground. Every applicant is welcomed. Every applicant’s family is welcomed if they arrive. Every applicant’s political enemy is welcomed too. The Beyul-Kshetra has no enemies. The Beyul-Kshetra is the absence of the question of enemies. Kanchenjunga has worked within the charter for nine years. He has welcomed two refugee Chinese-state-intelligence agents (identified by central Indian intelligence three months after arrival, then welcomed again as protected ward-residents). He has welcomed one former-Tibetan-monastery-corrupt-treasurer (who confessed to the corruption on his second week and was welcomed exactly as he had been welcomed the first week). He has welcomed a former-state-government Sikkim official accused of land-grab against the Hidden Land’s own forest-boundary (welcomed; reabsorbed; placed in the camp-administration cell where his expertise on Sikkim land-records made him the camp’s most effective ration-clerk). He has welcomed all of them. The charter requires it. The charter is not a guideline. The charter is the kingdom. THE REFUGEE FATHER In the spring of 2024, two and a half years before the chapter’s opening, a man in his forties arrived at the south refugee camp with a small backpack and a thirty-page Tibetan-language autobiography written in pencil in a school-exercise book. He gave his name as Krodha-Sherpa. He said he had been born in Pelliang Village in the Tibetan-occupied zone and that his entire family had been killed in a Chinese border-clearance operation in 2018. He had walked out across the Cho La pass alone in the winter of 2022. He had spent the intervening two years in Lhasa-region Buddhist monasteries doing manual labour and waiting for a route to the Indian side. He was welcomed. The intake clerk — a junior monk named Lobsang — logged him in the camp-administration database at intake-number 9,847. He was assigned to barrack 17-C, bed 3. His ration-card was issued the same afternoon. His autobiography was filed in the camp-archive in box 142 along with eleven hundred other recently-arrived testimonials. He was organised. He was articulate. He spoke Tibetan-fluent, Hindi-fluent, Nepali-fluent, Sikkimese-Bhutia-fluent. He read Sanskrit and English. He was not a monk, but he had a monk’s vocabulary. Within his first three months at the south camp he had started a Tibetan-language literacy class for refugee children whose schooling had been interrupted by the border crossing. Within his first six months he had organised a mutual-aid council across barracks 17 and 18, eight hundred refugees pooling ration-card supplements to support the most dependent among them. Within his first year he was running a weekly assembly for the south-camp’s twelve hundred adult-male refugees, called the Compassion-Reciprocity Council, that met every Sunday evening in barrack 17-C’s common room. The other refugees began to call him the Refugee Father. The phrase began informally and became official by the end of his second year. By the autumn of 2026 — the chapter’s opening — the Compassion-Reciprocity Council had grown to forty-seven hundred active members across all three south-Sikkim camps. The Refugee Father’s Sunday assemblies were attended by more refugees than the monastery’s own bi-weekly camp-administration meetings. Kanchenjunga had visited the Compassion-Reciprocity Council twice. The first visit, eight months earlier, he had sat at the back of the common room and listened to the Refugee Father explain the council’s ration-supplement allocation procedures. He had been impressed. The procedures were more efficient than the monastery’s. The Refugee Father’s administrative grasp was first-rate. The second visit, three months earlier, he had stayed for the Refugee Father’s closing-prayer. The closing-prayer was a Tibetan-language compassion-prayer attributed to the Sixth Karmapa, recited in the modern unision style. Kanchenjunga had joined the recitation. The Refugee Father had stood at the front and led it without faltering. Kanchenjunga had walked back to the monastery that evening reassured. The kingdom’s compassion-charter was producing its expected fruit. The most administratively-capable refugee in the camps was using his capability to multiply the camp’s self-care. The Beyul-Kshetra was working. He had not asked about the backpack. He had not asked about the autobiography. He had not asked the intake clerks for Krodha-Sherpa’s pre-2022 history. He had not, in nine years of guardianship, ever asked any refugee about his pre-arrival history. The charter forbade it. THE BLESSING-CEREMONY The Special Refugee-Blessing Ceremony begins at noon on the chapter’s opening day — the first Sunday of October. Two hundred and twelve new refugees are arranged in three rotating batches of seventy-one, seventy, and seventy-one. The Chintamani is brought into the main prayer hall in its ceremonial palanquin at eleven-forty-five a.m. and placed on the central altar. The housing is opened to half-visibility — the jewel’s side is exposed to the assembled refugees but not its top or its underside (the orthodox half-disclosure that has been the ceremonial standard since 1959). The hall holds about a thousand people. Two hundred and twelve refugees in the batch-area at the front. Six hundred and ninety long-standing camp residents and monastery monks at the rear. Approximately a hundred state-government and visiting-observer guests in a side gallery on the north wall. The Refugee Father is in the front row of the rear seating, beside the senior Compassion-Reciprocity Council delegates. Kanchenjunga performs the blessing in the standard procedure. Each refugee approaches the altar in turn, bows three times, receives the Chintamani-touched compassion-water from the senior monk’s ladle on the right of the altar, drinks it, returns to the seating area. The procedure takes about thirty-five seconds per refugee. Seventy-one refugees per batch. Three batches. The ceremony is scheduled to end at three p.m. At one-fourteen p.m., during the second batch, between refugee forty-three and refugee forty-four, the hall’s south-wall butter-lamps flicker. The flicker lasts about two seconds. Both lamps. Simultaneously. The hall’s ventilation does not normally produce flickers of this kind in the south-wall lamps; the prevailing draft at noon enters from the north door. Several of the monastery’s senior monks notice the flicker; none stops the ceremony; the flicker is logged later as “atmospheric.” At one-forty-seven p.m. the second batch concludes. The hall enters its standard fifteen-minute interval for the seating-rotation. Two senior monks remain at the central altar. The Chintamani is still in its half-opened housing. The Refugee Father stands and walks slowly toward the south-wall butter-lamp — the one that had flickered — ostensibly to adjust the wick. He bows to the senior monk at the south side of the altar in passing. The senior monk — Lobsang, the same junior monk who had logged Krodha-Sherpa’s arrival two and a half years earlier — bows back. The Refugee Father continues to the lamp. He adjusts the wick. The lamp resumes its normal burn. At one-fifty-three p.m. the third batch begins. The procedure continues. At three p.m. the ceremony concludes. The Chintamani is returned to its housing, the housing is sealed, the palanquin is carried back to the inner sanctum by the standard four-monk procession with Kanchenjunga at the head. Kanchenjunga opens the housing in the inner sanctum to perform the closing-of-day verification. He has performed this verification three thousand two hundred and eighty-seven consecutive times. The housing is empty. THE DAWN-DOOR Forty-seven days. The Chintamani is missing from the inner sanctum for forty-seven days. The monastery has not announced the theft. The state-government has not been informed. Two of the senior monastery monks have been informed. Lobsang the intake-clerk-and-senior-blessing-attendant is one. The senior administrative monk, Sonam, is the other. No one else. Kanchenjunga has decided to keep the absence private for as long as possible. The decision is not deception; it is triage. If the Chintamani’s absence is announced to the refugee population, the camps will riot. Twelve thousand refugees will read the absence as the end of their welcome. They will conclude that the monastery has lost the relic that anchored their compassion-charter. They will leave the camps in panic. Half of them will die in unprepared border-crossings. The math is concrete. He has done it. The decision is to keep the absence quiet and search the monastery, the camps, the state-government compound, the surrounding villages, in concentric rings, beginning with the closest. Lobsang and Sonam have searched the monastery building-by-building for nine days. They have found nothing. They have searched the south, central, and west camps barrack-by-barrack for another twelve days. They have found nothing. They have searched the surrounding villages with the cooperation of the Sikkim state-government chief secretary (who has been informed under strict confidentiality) for sixteen days. They have found nothing. At forty-six days, just before dawn on the forty-seventh day, a girl arrives at the monastery’s east gate. She is sixteen years old. She has walked the four kilometres from the south camp on foot. She is wearing the standard camp-issue brown sweater and dark trousers. Her hair is uncovered. She carries no bundle, no bag, no paper. She has come empty-handed. She is Tibetan. Her name is Pema-Chöden. She has been at the south camp for three months — arrived in the autumn surge, intake-number 11,438. Her parents and her older brother were killed in the same border-crossing she survived. Her brother was fifteen. He had been trying to help their mother across the third river-crossing on the western route. He had been shot by a Chinese border-patrol unit at the moment of helping her up. Their mother had been shot in the same burst. Their father had been shot at the same crossing on the way back trying to recover their bodies. Pema-Chöden had been on the eastern bank with their younger cousin Tashi. The two of them had walked the remaining sixty-four kilometres to the Sikkim border on foot over twelve days. She had taken her brother’s name as a middle-name on her camp-intake form. The form had her listed as Pema-Wangchuk-Chöden. Wangchuk was her brother’s name. She had explained the addition to Lobsang at intake by saying: “He should keep arriving in my paperwork. He did not make it past the river. He should arrive everywhere I arrive.” She had been a quiet camp-resident for three months. She had not attended the Compassion-Reciprocity Council’s Sunday assemblies. She had not attended the Tibetan-language literacy class. She had attended the monastery’s twice-weekly meditation hour and sat at the back. The senior monastery monks knew her by sight. Kanchenjunga had recognised her at the blessing-ceremony forty-seven days earlier — she had been at the front of the second batch, refugee number twenty-three of the second batch. She had received the compassion-water and bowed in the standard procedure and returned to her seat. She stands at the monastery’s east gate at five-twelve a.m. The gate-keeper monk asks her the standard pre-dawn entry question. She answers: “I need to speak with Kanchenjunga-la. Alone. Now. Before the dawn-prayer.” The gate-keeper hesitates. The dawn-prayer is at five-thirty. Kanchenjunga is in the inner sanctum. She says: “The Refugee Father is Krodha. I know because my brother went with him last week and came back wrong.” The gate-keeper opens the gate. PEMA-CHÖDEN AT THE ALTAR Kanchenjunga is at the inner sanctum altar when Lobsang brings her in. He is in his dawn-prayer position — right palm at the altar-edge, the absence of the Chintamani in front of him as it has been every dawn for forty-seven days. He has not slept the previous night. He has not slept properly in two weeks. He turns when Lobsang opens the door. He sees the sixteen-year-old refugee girl at the entrance. He registers her face from the blessing-ceremony forty-seven days earlier. He registers that she is empty-handed. He registers that she has come at five-twelve a.m. unannounced. He says, in his even guardian-voice: “Sit, daughter. The altar is your seat. Lobsang, the second tea.” She does not sit. She walks past the altar to the south wall and stops with her back to it. She faces him directly across the altar. The Chintamani’s empty housing is between them. She says, in Tibetan: “My brother Tashi — my cousin, not the one who died, the younger one, my mother’s sister’s son — went with the Refugee Father last week. The Refugee Father has been running a special evening sub-council for the new autumn boys for the past three months. The sub-council is at barrack 17-C, late, after the regular Compassion-Reciprocity Council closes. The new autumn boys are invited individually. The invitation is private. The boys who attend are told not to discuss it with the elders or the other refugees. Tashi attended for the first time twelve days ago. He came back to our barrack at three a.m. He did not speak for two days. On the third day he spoke. What he said was not Tashi.” Kanchenjunga says: “What did he say.” She says: “He said the Compassion-Charter was a Sikkimese lie. He said the Beyul was a Tibetan-fraud monastery built by collaborators with the 1959 expulsion. He said the senior monks were the inheritors of the lie. He said the Refugee Father was the only Tibetan in the kingdom who still knew what the original Beyul had been before the Karmapa lineage corrupted it in 1962. He said the Refugee Father had a plan to restore the original Beyul by removing the senior monks one by one over the next five years. He said the plan had begun last month when the Chintamani had been ‘reclaimed for the original sanctum.’ He said the Chintamani was now in the original sanctum and the senior monks did not know where the original sanctum was. He said the original sanctum was ‘at the south camp, under the dawn-prayer-wheel.’ He said I must come to the next sub-council on Tuesday or I would be considered a collaborator with the senior monks.” She pauses. She is breathing steadily. She has rehearsed the recitation. She says: “He said this in the voice the Refugee Father had taught him. The voice was not Tashi’s voice. Tashi’s voice was higher and slower. The voice that spoke through Tashi was lower and faster, with the Refugee Father’s exact cadence. I sat across the table from my own cousin and listened to the Refugee Father’s voice come out of his mouth. He could not see me from the inside any more.” Kanchenjunga says: “How do you know the Refugee Father is Krodha.” She says: “In 2018, when I was eight, my mother took me to Pelliang Village to visit a great-uncle. We stayed for nine days. There was a man in the village who had been the village-school’s deputy headmaster. He had been removed from the position the year before because three of the village children had developed a strange silence after their tutoring with him. The village had been arguing about whether to expel him for two years. He was forty at that time. He had a small backpack he carried everywhere. He had a thirty-page autobiography he had written about himself which he showed to my mother on the third evening. The autobiography was about his childhood in a different village in a different region. My mother read three pages of it and told me afterwards that the autobiography was the most carefully-constructed lie she had ever read. She told me to remember his face. She made me draw it from memory the next morning. I drew it. The drawing is in my barrack-locker. The man in the village was Krodha. The Refugee Father is the same man. The face is the same.” She pauses. Kanchenjunga has not moved. She says: “My mother is dead. My father is dead. My older brother is dead. My younger cousin is in the Refugee Father’s sub-council and is not himself any more. I have come to you because no other senior monk would believe a sixteen-year-old refugee girl. You will believe me because the Chintamani is missing from the housing on the altar between us and you have not announced it to anyone outside three people and I am the fourth person to know in this monastery. I know the Chintamani is missing because the housing’s wooden joints make a different sound when the chamber is closed empty than when the jewel is inside. I have heard the chamber closed full at the blessing-ceremony forty-seven days ago and I have heard the chamber closed empty when the senior monk Sonam carried the palanquin past my barrack at the south camp three nights ago when he was searching for the jewel. The empty sound is higher. Anyone who has been listening would know.” Kanchenjunga sits down on the stone bench against the east wall. He has not been told a more devastating thing in his life. He is sitting because his knees have signalled that they are not going to support his weight any longer. He says: “The dawn-prayer-wheel at the south camp. The one beside barrack 17-C. The large one. The black-iron one. The pilgrim-wheel installed by the Karmapa lineage in 1967.” She says: “Yes.” He says: “The wheel is hollow. The Chintamani would fit in the upper chamber. The wheel is turned every dawn by approximately two hundred camp-residents. A jewel placed in the upper chamber would not be discoverable by the wheel-turners because the turning would not open the chamber. Only a specifically-shaped key opens the upper chamber. The key was discontinued from monastery use in 1989. Three keys still exist. One is in the monastery archive. The other two are in Lhasa and Dharamsala.” She says: “The Refugee Father showed Tashi a key on the second night of the sub-council. The key was the same shape as the discontinued key. He told Tashi the key had been ‘recovered from the original sanctum’s sealed cupboard.’” Kanchenjunga is silent for forty seconds. Lobsang stands at the doorway. He has not breathed audibly during the entire recitation. He breathes now. Kanchenjunga says: “Will you walk with me to the south camp at noon.” She says: “Yes.” He says: “The Refugee Father will be at the south camp’s administration office at noon. The Sunday weekly council meeting begins at one. He will see us walk in. He will know what we are.” She says: “Yes.” He says: “The Compassion-Reciprocity Council members at the south camp will outnumber my monastery escort by approximately forty to one.” She says: “The Compassion-Reciprocity Council members are not all loyal to him. About half will go with him when you draw the boundary. The other half will stay.” He says: “How do you know.” She says: “The boys in his sub-council are loyal. The men who are running the older mutual-aid networks are not. My barrack-elder is one of those men. He has been concerned about the sub-council for six weeks. He has been waiting for someone like me to come and say what he could not say. He will speak when I speak. Three of the other barrack-elders will follow him.” Kanchenjunga looks at her for a long time. He says: “Daughter, do you know that you have just become the Beyul-Kshetra’s first refugee to hold what amounts to administrative office in its nine-century history.” She says: “Guardian-la, I have not asked for office. I have asked you to draw the boundary. The boundary is your work, not mine. My work is to walk beside you to the wheel. After that we will see what the kingdom requires.” He says: “The kingdom is going to require both. Sit down. Drink the second tea. Lobsang, bring the breakfast. We will walk to the south camp at eleven.” She sits down at the altar’s edge. She drinks the second tea. The dawn-prayer is missed for the first time in three thousand two hundred and eighty-eight days. The senior monks at the main prayer hall hold the dawn-prayer without Kanchenjunga, in his absence, by his standing instruction (silent in the inner sanctum until he returns) issued through Lobsang at the doorway at five-thirty-three a.m. The Beyul-Kshetra has taken its first action under conditions of internal infiltration. The first action is breakfast. ═══ INTERVAL ═══ THE WALK TO THE SOUTH CAMP Eleven a.m. The monastery’s east-gate path. The four-kilometre walk to the south camp passes the central tea-grove, the small village of Yang-Lhakhang, and the foothills approach where the path opens onto the south-camp plateau. Kanchenjunga walks at the front. He is in the full guardian-robe, the seven-fold sash, the prayer-beads. He carries the Karmapa-line walking-staff he has not used in nine years — a yew-wood shaft one and a half metres long with a copper-cap at the top and a small bronze prayer-wheel at the lower-third. The staff is the senior guardian’s formal walking-implement, used only for cross-kingdom processions and council-summons. He has summoned it from the monastery’s ceremonial cupboard at nine a.m. Pema-Chöden walks at his left shoulder, half a pace behind. She has been given a clean monastery ward-robe to wear over the camp-sweater — a knee-length deep-red over-robe that marks her as a guest of the senior guardian. The over-robe is not a refugee garment. It is the garment a state-government dignitary wears when received at the monastery for tea. She wears it because Kanchenjunga has insisted. The garment is its own boundary — the first boundary of the day, drawn between her and the camp she will return to in two hours’ time. Lobsang and Sonam walk at the rear, with four junior monks behind them. The state-government chief-secretary’s deputy walks beside Sonam — the chief-secretary has been informed by Kanchenjunga at eight a.m. and has sent his deputy in a Sikkim-state-administration plain-clothes capacity. The deputy is unarmed. He has a satellite-phone in his sleeve-pocket and a single sealed envelope (the state-government’s formal exile-of-foreign-national authorisation, pre-signed) in his inside-pocket. The procession is not announced. The four kilometres are walked in approximately one hour and ten minutes — Kanchenjunga’s normal walking pace. Pema-Chöden matches his pace. The other monks adjust to her pace as well. They arrive at the south-camp’s administration-perimeter at twelve-fifteen p.m. The administration-office is the central building of the camp — a single-storey concrete structure with a tin roof at the south-camp’s central plaza. The dawn-prayer-wheel is forty metres east of the administration office, beside barrack 17-C, in the camp’s eastern plaza. The Refugee Father is at the administration-office’s front step. He has been informed by his sub-council messengers (the runners are nine, twelve, and fourteen years old, refugee boys from barracks 14, 17, and 22) of the procession’s approach from the second-kilometre mark onward. He has had forty-five minutes to prepare. He is in his usual plain dark-red Pelliang-style tunic and dark trousers. He has on his wrist the small wooden prayer-bead bracelet that has marked him in the camp since 2024. He has on his face the calm reassuring expression that the camp has known him by for two and a half years. About one thousand four hundred refugees have gathered in the central plaza by twelve-fifteen p.m. The Compassion-Reciprocity Council’s senior delegates — about forty men — are arrayed in a semi-circle behind the Refugee Father. The mid-rank delegates and the sub-council boys — about a hundred and fifty — are arrayed behind the senior delegates. The general camp-population — the remaining twelve hundred — is arrayed in concentric rings further out. The atmosphere is not yet hostile. The Refugee Father has framed the procession in advance to the senior delegates as “the senior guardian’s autumn welfare-visit, accompanied by a young refugee girl whose case has come to the Compassion-Charter’s special attention.” The framing is plausible. The senior delegates are watching with respectful uncertainty. THE BOUNDARY-SPEECH Kanchenjunga stops six metres from the Refugee Father. Pema-Chöden stops one metre behind Kanchenjunga’s left shoulder. The Refugee Father bows in the Pelliang-style. The bow is technically perfect. The senior delegates bow in solidarity. Approximately three hundred of the mid-rank delegates and sub-council boys bow as well. About a thousand of the general camp-population bow, in scattered uneven groups. Kanchenjunga does not bow. He plants the Karmapa-line walking-staff at his right side. He lifts his right palm. He says, in Tibetan, in the senior-guardian formal-address voice that the camp has heard him use exactly four times in nine years (at the major blessing-ceremonies): “I have come at noon to draw the first boundary of my guardianship. I have welcomed every refugee for nine years. I will continue to welcome every refugee who is here as a refugee. The Compassion-Charter is unchanged for them. The Beyul-Kshetra remains open. But I will not continue to welcome one of you. The one I will not welcome is the man standing in front of me. His name is Krodha. He arrived at this camp in 2024 under a forged Tibetan identity. He has organised a sub-council that has been recruiting boys to a doctrine of monastery-replacement and senior-monk-removal over the past three months. He has stolen the Chintamani from the inner sanctum at the autumn blessing-ceremony forty-seven days ago. He has hidden it in the upper chamber of the dawn-prayer-wheel beside barrack 17-C. He will leave the Beyul-Kshetra by sunset. He will cross the Lachen pass into the Bhutan-Tibet border region by sunset of the third day. The refugees who choose to follow him are also welcome to leave with him — the boundary is the man, not the followers, and any follower who wishes to remain in the Beyul-Kshetra without him is welcome to remain. The followers who choose to leave with him will not be barred from leaving and will be permitted to take their personal effects and a one-month ration allocation. The state-government has authorised the formal exile under the cross-border emergency-residence clause. The deputy chief-secretary will issue the exile-order to the Refugee Father in person at the conclusion of this address. The Chintamani will be recovered from the dawn-prayer-wheel by the camp’s newly-appointed Beyul-Kshetra co-administrator, Pema-Chöden of the autumn surge, who will turn the wheel three full revolutions and open the upper chamber with the recovered Karmapa-line key. The Compassion-Charter is now amended for the first time in nine centuries: a refugee who has violated the inner sanctum is no longer welcome. The amendment is limited. The amendment is necessary. The amendment is mine to draw and I am drawing it.” The address takes seven minutes. The translation into the camp’s ambient mix of Tibetan-Hindi-Sikkimese is held in the listeners’ own minds; Kanchenjunga’s Tibetan is the original, and the bilingual listeners translate for the non-Tibetan-speakers in real-time, whispering into neighbours’ ears. The Refugee Father’s face changes through the seven minutes. The change is in stages. At minute one (the announcement of the boundary), the face shows the calm reassuring expression with an added quarter-smile — the smile of a man who recognises a rhetorical move and is preparing his counter-rhetoric. At minute two (the naming of the forged identity), the smile narrows. The calm-reassuring is replaced by a thin focused listening. At minute three (the description of the sub-council), the focused listening contracts. The skin around the eyes tightens. The thin lips fold inward. At minute four (the accusation of the Chintamani theft, with specific location), the face is no longer composed. The Refugee Father’s right hand — the one holding the wooden prayer-bead bracelet — trembles for the first time. The senior delegates behind him register the tremble. Their faces begin to change. At minute five (the offered exit, the freedom for followers to choose), the Refugee Father’s face composes itself again — deliberately. He is recovering. He is preparing to speak. At minute six (the announcement of Pema-Chöden as co-administrator), the Refugee Father turns his head ten degrees to the right. He looks at Pema-Chöden directly for the first time. The look lasts about two seconds. In that two seconds his face becomes the face of the village deputy headmaster from Pelliang in 2018 that Pema-Chöden drew from memory the next morning. The mask slips for two seconds, then resets. At minute seven (the closing “the amendment is mine to draw and I am drawing it”), the Refugee Father makes the choice that decides the rest of his Chapter Three arc. He chooses to leave. He bows. The bow is again Pelliang-style. The bow is shallower than the opening bow. It is the bow of a man who has accepted a defeat and is signalling, to those who will follow him, that they should also accept it for the time being. He says, in his even camp-voice: “Senior guardian. I accept the boundary. I will leave by sunset. Those who choose to follow me will be welcome to follow. She has the recovered key in her right hand. The wheel turns easily on its central axle. The turning takes about forty seconds. She finds the upper chamber’s key-slot at the top of the wheel’s north face — a small triangular indentation in the brass cap. She inserts the key. She turns the key one full revolution counter-clockwise. The upper chamber opens. The chamber is a small lined cavity in the top of the iron drum, about ten centimetres deep, lined with red velvet that has been undisturbed for thirty-seven years (the chamber was last opened in 1989 when the discontinuation-of-use was logged). It has the same exact appearance it has had for sixty-seven years. Pema-Chöden lifts the jewel out of the chamber with both hands. She has never touched it before. The jewel is warm — warmer than the surrounding air, warmer than her hands. The fur-pattern at the back of the paw is rendered in the standard Chapter-Two ink-density. The mark forms in approximately eleven seconds beneath the guardian-robe. It is not visible to the assembled refugees. Kanchenjunga feels the formation as a slow warm pressure across the pectoral — not painful, not uncomfortable, but unmistakable. He has been waiting for this sensation for fourteen years. The waiting is finally over. He does not show the moment on his face. He signs the acknowledgement. He keeps the duplicate copy. The followers are packing their personal effects. The state-government ration-allocation officers have begun issuing the one-month rations from the camp-storeroom. The general followers walk in a long staggered file behind. The column stretches approximately one and a half kilometres along the foothill trail leading toward the Lachen pass. THE LACHEN PASS The Lachen pass is the northeastern crossing-point between Sikkim and the Bhutan-Tibet border zone. The pass is at four thousand seven hundred metres altitude. It is open to foot-traffic in October but closes to motor-vehicles by mid-November. The crossing on foot takes approximately three days from the south-camp’s exit-point. The Refugee Father’s column reaches the pass on the morning of the third day. By the eleven a.m. checkpoint at the Lachen pass entry-station, the column has been reduced to approximately five hundred and ten followers — ninety more refugees have detached over the three-day walk and begun the return-trip to the south camp. The detached followers are mostly the mid-rank delegates and the general camp-population followers. The sub-council boys remain. The senior delegates remain. At eleven-fifteen a.m. on the third day, at the Lachen pass entry-station, the Refugee Father signs his column out of the Beyul-Kshetra. The pass-station officers stamp each follower’s border-crossing certificate. The stamping takes about two hours. The Refugee Father is the first to cross. His sub-council senior delegates follow. The column then proceeds in single-file order across the pass. By two p.m. the last of the column has crossed. The Lachen pass is closed behind them. The Beyul-Kshetra’s boundary has been drawn. Kanchenjunga has not walked to the Lachen pass himself. He has remained at the monastery to perform the forty-eighth-day dawn-prayer with the Chintamani restored to the inner sanctum at five-thirty a.m. on the second day after the boundary-speech. The prayer takes one hour. Pema-Chöden stands at the doorway of the inner sanctum throughout the prayer. She watches the senior guardian perform the prayer she has heard him perform from outside the sanctum-walls four hundred and seventy times in three months. From inside the sanctum-doorway the prayer sounds different. The Chintamani in its housing is two metres in front of her. The senior guardian is at the altar between her and the jewel. When the prayer concludes at six-thirty a.m. Kanchenjunga turns to face her. He gestures for her to come forward. She walks the two metres to the altar. He says: “Daughter. I will write the amendment. The first line will read: The Compassion-Charter remains open to all who suffer; the Charter closes to one who has violated the inner sanctum. Begin.” ═══ SECOND HALF ═══ THE TEN DAYS OF ORGANISATION The next ten days are an administrative re-organisation that the Beyul-Kshetra has not seen in sixty-seven years. The Compassion-Charter amendment is drafted by Pema-Chöden in a single thirty-eight-hour session at the south-camp administration-office, with Lobsang at her left and Sonam at her right and the Sikkim state-government chief-secretary’s deputy at her right-second. The amendment is three pages long. The first page contains the four-clause limit. The second page contains the procedural-test for invoking the limit. The third page contains the appeals procedure. The four-clause limit reads, in the final ratified text: I. The Compassion-Charter is reaffirmed for all who arrive seeking refuge. II. The Charter closes only to a refugee who has knowingly violated the inner sanctum — defined as removing a sanctum-relic, deliberately deceiving the intake about identity-of-record, or organising a covert sub-council aimed at the removal of senior monks. III. The procedural-test for invoking Clause II is the senior guardian’s public boundary-speech delivered in the central plaza of the affected camp with the state-government chief-secretary present and the affected refugee given the right of response. IV. The closure under Clause II is exile across the nearest international border-crossing with one month’s ration-allocation. The exiled refugee’s followers retain the right to choose to remain or to leave; followers who remain are reabsorbed into the camp without prejudice; followers who leave receive ration-allocation under the same terms as the exiled principal. The amendment is ratified by the monastery’s senior monks council on the seventh day after the boundary-speech. The vote is twenty-three to one. The single dissenting vote is cast by an elderly senior monk named Tenzin-Norbu (sixty-eight, the monastery’s second-most-senior after Kanchenjunga) who has held the Compassion-Charter as unamendable for forty years. His dissent is recorded; he affirms his continued loyalty to the senior guardian and accepts the amendment as the majority will. He resigns from active senior-monks-council duties two days later in protest and takes up a meditation-cave residence in the central forest — a long-anticipated retirement that the chapter’s events have accelerated by approximately two years. The amendment text is published in the south, central, and west camps’ bulletin-boards on the eighth day. The text is translated into Tibetan, Hindi, Sikkimese-Bhutia, Nepali, English, and Limbu (the six camp-spoken languages) by a six-translator team that Pema-Chöden assembles from the camp-population in a single afternoon. The text is read aloud at the south camp’s central plaza on the ninth day by Pema-Chöden herself, in Tibetan, in the late afternoon, with about eight hundred returned camp-residents in attendance. The reading takes nineteen minutes. The reading is followed by a senior monk’s response-prayer led by Lobsang. The prayer is the Sixth Karmapa’s compassion-prayer — the same prayer the Refugee Father had led at the Compassion-Reciprocity Council’s closing nine months earlier when Kanchenjunga had walked back to the monastery reassured. The reabsorption of the seven-hundred refugees who had remained begins on the tenth day. The reabsorption is administered through individual interviews with each adult refugee, conducted by the camp’s newly-appointed Re-Integration Committee — eight Compassion-Reciprocity Council senior delegates who had stayed with Kanchenjunga at the boundary-speech, plus three monastery monks, plus Pema-Chöden as chair. The interviews are not interrogations. They are conversations about the refugee’s pre-arrival history, the refugee’s reasons for joining or not joining the sub-council, and the refugee’s current intentions. The committee’s standard is forgiveness-with-record: each refugee’s account is written down, the account is filed in the camp-archive, no further action is taken unless the account itself indicates a violation under Clause II. By the end of the second month, the Beyul-Kshetra has fully reabsorbed the seven-hundred remaining refugees. The seven-hundred have re-joined the camp-population’s daily life. The Compassion-Reciprocity Council has been restructured under new leadership — a senior delegate named Karma-Topgyal (forty-seven, a Tibetan-Sikkimese mixed-line refugee from the 2008 Lhasa wave, a man Kanchenjunga had personally vetted for the new chair-position) leads the council, with two deputies drawn from the long-standing 1959-wave residents. The western face of Khangchendzonga — the third-highest mountain in the world, the Beyul-Kshetra’s deity-peak, the kingdom’s sacred wall. A Khangchendzonga National Park ranger named Tashi-Bhutia (no relation to Pema-Chöden’s cousin Tashi — the name is a common Bhutia given-name; the ranger is forty-three, an eleven-year park-service veteran, the senior ranger of the western-face patrol-station) is conducting the standard December high-altitude wildlife-census on the morning of the second of December. He is at the western-face base camp at four thousand five hundred metres. He has a high-power binocular and a Park Service satellite-telemetry receiver. He is scanning the western face for the December baseline-count of high-altitude mammals — snow leopards (expected count: 3-4 in the western-face range), Tibetan blue sheep (40-60), Himalayan tahr (15-25), and the occasional opportunistic red panda (0-2, normally in the lower-elevation rhododendron belt). At seven-forty-three a.m. he sees the red panda on the snowline edge at approximately five thousand four hundred metres altitude. The panda is on the western face. The panda is grazing on a small patch of rhododendron shrub that has somehow survived above the rhododendron belt’s normal altitude limit of four thousand eight hundred metres — the shrub itself is anomalous; he logs the anomaly in his field-book as a secondary observation. The panda is the primary observation. Red pandas live at two thousand two hundred to four thousand eight hundred metres altitude. Above five thousand metres the atmospheric oxygen-content (approximately fifty percent of sea-level oxygen at five thousand four hundred metres) is physiologically lethal to the species. Red pandas have not been documented above five thousand metres in the species’ recorded biological history. The biological reason is straightforward: red panda haemoglobin lacks the high-altitude oxygen-affinity mutation that yak haemoglobin and Tibetan-antelope haemoglobin have evolved over the past five million years. A red panda at five thousand four hundred metres should be dead within thirty minutes from acute mountain-sickness combined with cold-stress. The panda Tashi-Bhutia is observing is not dead. The panda is grazing. The panda’s breathing rate is, through the binocular, observable: approximately fourteen breaths per minute, which is the normal red-panda resting rate. The panda is not in distress. The panda is, simply, at five thousand four hundred metres, on a Khangchendzonga rhododendron-patch, eating. Tashi-Bhutia calls in the observation by satellite-radio to the Park Service’s Gangtok central-office at eight a.m. The Gangtok central-office routes the call to the Sikkim state-government wildlife-affairs department by eight-thirty a.m. The wildlife-affairs department routes the call to the Rumtek Monastery’s ward-coordination office at nine-fifteen a.m. (the monastery has been the standing recipient of all anomalous-wildlife observations on Khangchendzonga since 1967, by an inter-departmental memorandum-of-understanding the chief-secretary’s office had set up after a similar anomalous observation in the 1962 Karmapa-lineage re-ratification year). Pema-Chöden takes the call at the ward-coordination office at nine-sixteen a.m. She writes down the observation in her co-administrator log-book. She walks the observation across the monastery courtyard to the inner sanctum at nine-twenty a.m. She does not enter the sanctum; she stands at the doorway and waits for Kanchenjunga to finish his morning-prayer. The panda is not in distress.” Kanchenjunga sits down on the stone bench against the east wall. The red-panda-foreleg mark on his right pectoral pulses. The pulse is not pain.
