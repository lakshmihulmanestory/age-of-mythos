The Pure-Lineage Wars — A Film in Two Halves
═══ FIRST HALF ═══ OPENING — THE HORNBILL COMMITTEE ROOM Kohima. The first week of November. The Nagaland State Government’s Hornbill Festival Steering Committee room. Sixteen chairs around an oval table. Each chair has a different tribal council name-plate. Dzulevira is at the head. She is thirty-three. She is in a forest-green Tangkhul shawl over a plain black shirt and trousers. Her hair is cropped short. She has been the state hero of Nagaland for two years, elected by the most narrowly distributed vote in the state’s post-1972 history — she carried at least 4 percent of every tribe’s vote and no tribe’s majority. Her election was a referendum on cross-tribal politics. Her two-year reign has been a steady, slow loosening of the inter-tribal tensions that had been building since 2019. In the last ninety days, the loosening has reversed. The Hornbill Festival is to open in eight days. The festival has been held every December since 2000. It is Nagaland’s flagship cultural event — all sixteen tribes performing on rotating stages, an international tourism audience, two hundred crore in annual economic impact. This morning the chairperson of the Angami council has formally withdrawn the Angami tribe from the festival. By noon the chair of the Ao council has done the same. By two p.m. the Konyak. By three the Sumi. By four the Lotha and the Sangtam. By five-thirty p.m. all sixteen tribes have withdrawn. Each cites “ancestral purity-disputes with the other fifteen” as the reason. The festival has not been cancelled in twenty-five years. It is cancelled in five and a half hours. Dzulevira sits in the empty committee room until seven p.m. Her chief secretary, a Lotha woman named Mhabemo, eventually puts a cup of black tea in front of her. Mhabemo says: "The Pure-Tongue has visited all sixteen now. Last village was Yimchunger, six days ago. Every tribe has its DNA-rite kit. Every tribe has been told it is the original." Dzulevira says: "I knew sixteen days ago. I should have moved then." "You did not move because you did not know what to move toward. None of us knew." "I should have known." Mhabemo does not argue with this. She has worked for Dzulevira for two years; she has learned that her hero accepts blame more efficiently when nobody contests it. THE PURE-TONGUE’S METHOD It is documented later, after the walk, in a Nagaland State Archives report. Naga-Rakshasa — the Chapter One villain, returned from two years in the Patkai Hills border-zone — spent autumn 2026 visiting sixty-three Naga villages across six months. He arrived at each village speaking the village’s tribal dialect. The dialects of the sixteen Naga tribes diverged across centuries of mutual separation; most are mutually unintelligible; no living Naga has ever been documented as fluent in more than four. Naga-Rakshasa speaks all sixteen fluently. He arrived at each village as one of their own. He bowed in the tribe’s style. He sat at the tribe’s correct elder-position when invited. He ate the tribe’s ceremonial foods. He told the tribe’s elders that he had been studying their tribe’s ancestral records and had a gift to give them. The gift was a small white box. The box held a saliva-collection swab, a return-envelope, and a one-page report from a Chiang Mai laboratory called Pure-Origin Genomics. The report named the village elder by his exact Tangkhul-Naga or Ao-Naga or Konyak-Naga or sixteenth-name and certified, with QR-code and seventeen-page genomic appendix, that the elder’s DNA matched the “original Naga ancestor strain” at 99.7 percent or higher — the highest match in the global Naga-origin database. Every village’s elder, in every tribe, received the same 99.7-percent-match report. The reports were forged. The Chiang Mai lab was forged. The Pure-Origin Genomics website was forged. The genomic appendices were forged. Naga-Rakshasa had been preparing the forgery operation for two years at a Mumbai-Mizoram facility funded by the same Singapore line that had funded Megha-Daitya’s submachine guns and Tamasa-Nrita’s daos. Each village believed. Each tribe demanded that all the others surrender their false claims. Each tribe began purity-testing its own members — particularly those married out to other tribes or to non-Naga partners. KA-MEMCHA AT UKHRUL Week two of the chapter. Ukhrul. The senior Tangkhul-Naga clan-house of Dzulevira’s mother’s family. Ka-Memcha has flown in from Imphal. She has come because Dzulevira’s twenty-three-day-old appeal for help has finally reached her threshold. She has left Sangaputra in Moirangthem’s care — the boy is back at the Govindaji temple, in the new pause-school’s third class, learning at the front-left corner of the formation. He is safe. She is at the Ukhrul family-house for nine days before the Tangkhul Tribal Council’s Senior Priest sub-committee summons her. The summons is a single-page Tangkhul-language letter on the Tribal Council’s official letterhead. It reads (translated): Ka-Memcha-the-Eldest is summoned to the Tangkhul Tribal Ceremony Grounds on the morning of the fourteenth day of the month to submit to the Pure-Origin Genomic Verification rite. The verification is required for all Tangkhul-born members residing outside the tribe’s territorial boundary for more than five consecutive years. The applicant’s minor son, residing in Imphal, is named in the same summons. The Senior Priest Khrumai-Sho will officiate. Failure to appear or refusal to submit will be considered ceremonial-sacrilege under Tangkhul customary law as preserved under the Sixth Schedule, sub-clause (4)(b). Ka-Memcha reads the letter twice. She walks the two and a half kilometres from the family-house to Dzulevira’s state-residence in central Ukhrul. (Dzulevira had relocated her state-office to Ukhrul for the duration of the purity-wars; she was meeting with the Tangkhul elders three times a week.) Ka-Memcha shows her sister the letter at four-thirty p.m. Dzulevira reads it. She says: "Refuse. I will issue a state-government counter-notice tomorrow morning. Khrumai-Sho will withdraw the summons by tomorrow evening." Ka-Memcha says: "If I refuse without showing, the tribe says I have hidden myself. If I refuse at the ceremony-grounds in front of the tribe, the tribe sees the refusal and remembers it. I will go. I will refuse publicly. The refusal is the dharma. The state-government counter-notice is a different thing — you should still issue it, but I should still go." Dzulevira looks at her older sister for a long time. They have not lived in the same house since Ka-Memcha was twenty-two. They have not always agreed; they have always understood. Dzulevira understands now. She says: "I will be at the ceremony-grounds with you." "No. Your presence will make the refusal political. My refusal must be personal. The dharma must come from the one being tested. Send the state-police detail to the grounds. Have them stay at the perimeter. They will be there. You will not be." "If something happens — " "If something happens you will need to walk the kingdom afterward. You will not be able to walk if you are standing next to me. Stay at the office. Send the police. I will see you at lunch. The ceremony is at first light." Dzulevira does not argue. She also does not believe Ka-Memcha is about to die. The Tangkhul Senior Priest sub-committee has never killed a refusing-applicant in two centuries of recorded ceremony. The Tangkhul Senior Priest sub-committee, as Dzulevira finds out at lunch the next day, has never previously been confronted with a refusing-applicant from a tribal-elder line who is the elder sister of the state hero. THE CEREMONY GROUNDS Six a.m. The Tangkhul Tribal Ceremony Grounds at Ukhrul. A circular stone-paved platform at the eastern edge of the village. A ceremonial fire in the centre — a stone pit one and a half metres across, the fire built that morning by Khrumai-Sho’s apprentices, mahua-wood and pine-resin, burning at a height of just over a metre. Khrumai-Sho is the Senior Priest of the Tangkhul Tribal Council. He is fifty-three. He has held the position for nine years. He has officiated three hundred and forty-one purity-rites in his career. He has never had a refusal until today. Ka-Memcha arrives at five-fifty-five a.m. She walks alone from the family-house. She wears the formal Tangkhul shawl her mother gave her at her thirteenth-year naming-rite. She has on her wrist the Meitei wedding bangle Moirangthem gave her at their marriage. The two pieces of cloth-and-metal are her chosen statement. The Tangkhul elders are assembled around the ceremony-grounds — about forty of them, all senior tribal-council members. The state-police detail of six officers is at the perimeter, twenty metres back, in plain clothes. Mhabemo is among them, holding a satellite phone. Khrumai-Sho asks the formal opening question in Tangkhul: "Ka-Memcha-the-Eldest, do you submit to the Pure-Origin Genomic Verification rite as required by Tangkhul customary law?" She answers in Tangkhul, clearly, at full ceremony-voice: "I do not." The forty elders make the soft sound of formal recognition. Khrumai-Sho asks the formal second question: "On what dharmic ground do you refuse?" She answers, switching to a mixed Tangkhul-Meitei register that the elders recognise as deliberate: "I am Tangkhul. I am also Meitei. Both. My son is both. Neither is impure. The Pure-Origin rite assumes a single ancestry. I do not have a single ancestry. The rite cannot test what I am because what I am is two things at once. I refuse the rite because the rite cannot ask the right question." There is a long silence. The forty elders are processing the answer. The answer is, by Tangkhul customary law’s pre-modern principles, dharmically articulate. The customary law as preserved under the Sixth Schedule has assumed since 1972 that tribal members have a single tribal identity — the assumption was never tested before today because nobody before today was both a state-hero’s elder sister and a Manipur-married woman of the senior Tangkhul line. Khrumai-Sho is not processing the answer. He had not prepared for it. He had expected refusal-by-silence, by walking-away, by political-bluster. He had not expected refusal-by-dharmic-articulation in the ceremony-Tangkhul register. He says, in the formal closing-position voice: "Your refusal is sacrilege." Ka-Memcha says, in the same register: "My refusal is dharma. Your declaration of sacrilege is your error." Khrumai-Sho gestures to two of his senior apprentices. The apprentices step toward Ka-Memcha. She does not move. The state-police detail at the perimeter sees the gesture. Mhabemo presses the satellite phone’s call-button. Dzulevira, in the state-office two kilometres away, receives the alert at six-eleven a.m. The apprentices reach Ka-Memcha at six-twelve a.m. They take her arms. Khrumai-Sho says: "The applicant has refused the rite. The applicant has declared the rite invalid. The applicant must submit by the purification-of-the-refusal sub-rite. The sub-rite is fire-purification." Ka-Memcha understands the sub-rite. It is a Tangkhul tradition for the most serious dharmic refusals — one that has not been invoked in eighty-seven years. The applicant is required to walk three times around the fire-pit while reciting the seven affirmations. The applicant is not pushed into the fire. The applicant is required to walk around it. She says, in the formal Tangkhul: "I will walk." She begins to walk. She circles the fire-pit once. The forty elders watch. At six-fifteen a.m., during the second circumambulation, Khrumai-Sho steps off the priest’s platform and moves toward the fire-pit edge. His apprentices stand to either side of him. The state-police detail at the perimeter has begun to move forward but is still fifteen metres away. At six-sixteen a.m., as Ka-Memcha passes the fire-pit’s eastern edge for the second time, Khrumai-Sho pushes her into the fire. The push is not concealed. The forty elders see it. The state-police detail sees it. The two apprentices see it. Khrumai-Sho does not attempt to disguise the push as accident. He pushes her with both hands at her shoulder-blades. The push is hard and unambiguous. She falls into the fire-pit. The fire is at over a metre’s height. Mahua-wood and pine-resin burn at temperatures above seven hundred degrees Celsius. She is dead in fifty-three seconds. She does not scream — the smoke gets her airway before her body registers the burn. The forty elders see the silence as the dharmic completion of her refusal. She had said the rite could not ask the right question. She made her answer with her silence. The state-police detail arrives at the fire-pit at six-seventeen a.m. They put out the fire with water-bottles from their vehicle. They recover her body. They arrest Khrumai-Sho at six-twenty-one a.m. He does not resist. He says, at the arrest: "I have done my dharma." Mhabemo’s satellite phone records the line. DZULEVIRA’S TWO DAYS OF SILENCE She is informed at six-twenty-three a.m. at the state-office. She does not speak for two days. Mhabemo manages her schedule. The state-government issues the cremation order. The Tangkhul tribal council is suspended pending a state-government review of Khrumai-Sho’s arrest. Khrumai-Sho is held at the Ukhrul district jail; his case is referred to the Nagaland High Court under criminal jurisdiction; the Sixth Schedule customary-law exemption claim is filed by his lawyer within eleven hours and rejected by the state-government attorney within twenty-eight. Ka-Memcha’s cremation is held the next afternoon. The cremation is performed by Dzulevira herself, alone — no Tangkhul priest, no Meitei priest, no state-ceremony. She uses a combined Tangkhul-Meitei rite that she has researched in Khrieo-Mama’s manuscript-library overnight. The ashes are collected into a Meitei brass urn and a Tangkhul clay pot in equal measure — Dzulevira’s choice, mirroring her sister’s last public statement. She sends the Meitei urn to Moirangthem at Imphal by special courier the same evening. She keeps the Tangkhul clay pot at the Ukhrul family-house. Moirangthem receives the urn at midnight. He has known of Ka-Memcha’s death since six-thirty a.m. the previous morning — Dzulevira had sent a Sarus-pigeon within twenty minutes of being informed. He has not told Sangaputra yet. He tells the boy at six a.m. the next morning, at the temple courtyard, in the small east room, alone. Sangaputra goes silent for three days. The pause-school’s third class has him at the front-left corner of the formation for all three days. He does not perform sequences. He performs the pause. The pause holds for the full class-period — forty-five minutes — on the third day. Moirangthem watches without intervening. KHRIEO-MAMA AT MIDNIGHT Week four of the chapter. The night of day fifteen since Ka-Memcha’s death. Eleven-fifty p.m. Dzulevira is at the state-office. She has finally begun working again — or at any rate the work-papers are in front of her and her pen is in her hand and she is moving the pen. Mhabemo knocks at the office-door. She says: "There is an old woman at the front-desk. She has walked from Khonoma. She wants to see you. She is carrying something heavy in a shawl-wrap. She says you will know her name when I say it." "Her name?" "Khrieo-Mama." Dzulevira sets the pen down. She has heard the name three times in her adult life — once at her election when an old Khonoma elder voted for her at the Angami booth and was identified in the next morning’s newspaper as a relative of Khrieo-Mama, once at her swearing-in when Khrieo-Mama sent a single-line congratulatory letter ("The mithun walks. The mithun knows."), and once in graduate-school linguistics class when Khrieo-Mama was named as one of the seven Naga elders who knew the pre-modern Angami ceremonial vocabulary. She has not met Khrieo-Mama in person. She says: "Bring her in. Bring her tea. Whatever she is carrying, carry it for her." Mhabemo brings her in. Khrieo-Mama is eighty-nine. She is shorter than her grand-niece-by-line had expected. She is wearing the deep-indigo Angami matriarch’s wrap with the seven brass arm-rings (the elder-mark of the senior Khonoma line). Her hair is white and bound at the nape with a single black thread. She sits on the chair across from Dzulevira’s desk. She accepts the tea. She unwraps the shawl-bundle on her lap. Inside the shawl is a clay tablet. The tablet is approximately thirty centimetres by twenty centimetres, two centimetres thick, fired-clay, with sixteen lines of pre-modern script across its surface and sixteen small thumb-prints in dark-clay at the bottom — the signatures. Khrieo-Mama says: "Dzulevira. Listen carefully. This tablet has been under the floor of my house since 1985. It was under the floor of my grandmother’s house from 1923 to 1985. It was under the floor of her grandmother’s house from 1882 to 1923. It was in the open at Kohima from 1425 to 1882. It is the Pre-Tribal Pact. The script is the sixteen pre-modern Naga dialects, one line per tribe. The thumb-prints are the original founders’ signatures. I have not read it aloud to anyone in forty-one years. I am going to read it aloud to you now in Angami. Then I am going to write down translations in all sixteen modern dialects and in Hindi by morning. Drink your tea while I read." She reads. The reading takes nine minutes. Dzulevira does not drink her tea. The Pact is fifty-three words in Angami. Translated to English the Pact reads: Before the sixteen, there was one. Before the names, there was the mithun. The names are our work. The mithun is our root. Let no one of us claim purity above another. Let the mithun walk between us when the sixteen fight. The one who walks beside the mithun without a tribe is the one who holds the kingdom. The signatures are at the bottom — sixteen thumb-prints, each in the dark-clay of the founder’s home valley. The clay-types are scientifically distinguishable: Pochury clay is iron-rich red; Konyak clay is granite-grey; Tangkhul clay is the brown of the Manipur-border alluvium. A trained eye can read the signatures’ provenance without consulting any other document. Dzulevira looks at the Tangkhul thumb-print at line fourteen. The print is the same approximate size as her sister’s thumb. It is not her sister’s thumb — the print is six centuries old — but it is the thumb of a Tangkhul woman who signed her name to a Pact saying her tribe should not claim purity above any other tribe. She says, finally: "What do I do." Khrieo-Mama says: "You walk the mithun. Through all sixteen valleys. As all the tribes. As none of the tribes. You walk naked except for ash. You answer in no tribal dialect. You read the Pact aloud in each tribe’s dialect — I will give you the translations by morning. You show them what the Pure-Tongue cannot do." "How do I answer if I cannot speak any tribal dialect?" "You answer in Hindi. The language none of the sixteen tribes claim. The language that is the kingdom’s union, not its origin. The Pure-Tongue speaks all sixteen dialects. Hindi is the one language he cannot weaponise against you, because Hindi belongs to none of the tribes whose purity-claim he is selling. Answer in Hindi and the manipulation collapses on itself." Dzulevira considers this for a long moment. She says: "When do I start?" "In five days. The mithun needs four days to be selected and prepared. The translations need three nights to be written. Your body needs the rest. Start on day six." ═══ INTERVAL ═══ THE MITHUN-SELECTION Day three of the five preparation-days. The Dzukou Valley grass-fields. The traditional mithun-pasture of the Nagaland state herd. There are forty-seven mithun in the Dzukou herd. The mithun is the Nagaland state animal — the same animal as Onge-Nakshatra’s saltwater-crocodile-equivalent in Sagara-Ratna in the sense that it is one of the saga’s rare mainland-bonded-with-an-island-cousin animals — but Naga mithun are the cattle-form, larger than gaur, more domesticated, the central ritual animal of every Naga tribe’s sacrifice and feast tradition. Dzulevira walks among the herd at dawn. She has known some of these animals since she was seven. She has not walked the herd as a bond-recognition exercise in nine years. The alpha bull — the largest of the herd, fifteen years old, two and a half metres at the shoulder, weight approximately one thousand kilograms — walks to meet her at the herd’s eastern edge. She stops three metres from him. He stops three metres from her. She says, in Hindi (per Khrieo-Mama’s instruction): "I am going to walk the kingdom with you. Sixteen valleys. Twenty-seven days. You will go where I go. We will read the Pact at every village. I will answer the Pure-Tongue in the language none of the tribes claim. Are you willing." The mithun looks at her. He breathes. He shifts his weight to his right foreleg. She takes the weight-shift as the answer it is. She walks to him. She places her right palm on his forehead between his eyes. He lowers his head. She says: "Tomorrow night we begin." THE WALK BEGINS Five days after Khrieo-Mama’s midnight visit. Pre-dawn. The Tangkhul Tribal Ceremony Grounds at Ukhrul — the same grounds where Ka-Memcha died. The valley one of the sixteen. Dzulevira walks to the grounds at four-thirty a.m. She is naked. She is covered in ash from a mithun-fire prepared by Khrieo-Mama and a Khonoma priestess the night before. The ash is grey-white. It coats her skin from forehead to feet. She carries nothing except a small cloth-bundle — Khrieo-Mama’s sixteen-language translation of the Pact, wrapped in coarse jute. The mithun walks beside her. He is unled — no rope, no halter. He walks at her left shoulder, two paces back, at her exact pace. The Tangkhul ceremony-grounds are empty at four-thirty a.m. The forty elders are not there. The ground’s stone-paved platform has been cleaned of fire-ash by the state-police; the ceremony-fire pit has been filled with sand. The new dawn is still half an hour away. Dzulevira stands at the platform’s eastern edge. She unwraps the jute-bundle. She finds the Tangkhul translation page. She reads the Pact aloud at full ceremony-voice in Tangkhul. The reading takes four minutes. The Tangkhul translation is fifty-eight words. The fire-pit hears it — the fire-pit is part of the listening, even with sand in it. The state-police perimeter detail (Mhabemo at the centre, holding the satellite phone) hears it. The Tangkhul tribal council’s deputy chairperson hears it — he had come at four to watch from a distance. When she finishes she folds the Tangkhul page. She turns east. She begins walking. The mithun walks beside her. By six a.m. they are two and a half kilometres east of Ukhrul, on the trail toward the Khiamniungan valley — the second tribe’s territory by Khrieo-Mama’s walk-order. ═══ SECOND HALF ═══ THE TWENTY-SEVEN DAYS The walk is twenty-seven days. Sixteen valleys. Some valleys are one day’s walk from the previous; some are three. The mithun walks the whole distance unled. Dzulevira walks the whole distance ash-covered and naked. The state-police detail follows at fifty metres distance with a single jeep carrying water, the jute-bundle of translations, Khrieo-Mama (in the support-jeep for valleys four through sixteen; she walked the first three before her knees gave out), and a small Tangkhul woman named Phinika who has been assigned as the ash-renewal-attendant. At each valley’s village-edge Dzulevira stops. The mithun stops. She unwraps the jute-bundle and finds the correct dialect page. She reads the Pact aloud in that tribe’s dialect at full ceremony-voice. The reading takes between three and seven minutes depending on the dialect’s syntactic compression. Then she walks into the village. She does not speak. She walks the length of the village’s main path with the mithun beside her. At the village’s far edge she turns and walks back. She stands at the eastern edge again. She rewraps the bundle. She continues east to the next valley. At each village the reception varies. At the Khiamniungan valley (second), the village elders bow to her as she walks the main path. None of them speaks to her. The mithun urinates on the village-square statue of the founding elder — an act the senior priest interprets as a ritual blessing. At the Konyak valley (third), three young men try to capture the mithun at the village’s far edge. The mithun lowers his head and charges. They scatter. He returns to Dzulevira’s side without further incident. At the Ao valley (fourth), the village throws stones. Two stones strike Dzulevira — one on the right shoulder, one on the left thigh. The mithun does not respond. Dzulevira does not respond. She finishes the village-traversal with blood running down her thigh; the ash absorbs the blood without showing colour. At the Sumi valley (fifth), the village comes out to welcome her. Three hundred Sumi villagers line the main path. They have heard about the previous valleys. They place mithun-foot-mark drawings in chalk on the ground in front of her walk-path. She walks over the chalk-drawings. The mithun follows. At the Chakhesang valley (sixth), the Pure-Tongue is rumoured to be present in the village. He is not. He has left two days earlier — word of the walk has reached him faster than he expected. Dzulevira walks the village without confrontation. At the Lotha valley (seventh), the senior priest comes out to read his own counter-statement at the village-edge. He reads it in Lotha. Dzulevira waits while he reads. When he finishes she reads the Pact in Lotha. The senior priest goes back into his house without responding to her reading. She walks the village. At the Phom valley (eighth), the mithun stops in the middle of the village-square and refuses to move for forty-three minutes. Dzulevira stands beside him. The village watches. The mithun starts moving again at the forty-fourth minute. No explanation is offered or required. The forty-three-minute pause is later named in Phom oral tradition as “the second pause-school lesson” — a reference to her brother-in-law Moirangthem’s newly-named Manipuri dance-school. The cross-reference is intentional on the part of the Phom oral-tradition keepers. At the Chang valley (ninth), the village offers her clothes. She refuses. The village offers her food. She refuses. The mithun accepts a single handful of grain from the village headwoman. The accepting is itself the statement. At the Yimchunger valley (tenth), the Pure-Tongue has been rumoured to be returning. He is not seen. The walk continues. At the Rengma valley (eleventh), Khrieo-Mama’s knees finally give out. She rides in the support-jeep for the remaining six valleys. The translations are now memorised — Dzulevira does not need the jute-bundle for the last five readings. At the Sangtam valley (twelfth), a small girl — six years old — runs out and touches the mithun’s right foreleg. She is not stopped by the elders. The mithun does not flinch. The girl says, in Sangtam: “Aunt-without-a-tribe.” The phrase enters Sangtam oral tradition that evening. At the Zeliang valley (thirteenth), the village holds a brief impromptu ceremony — not a purity-rite, but a recognition-rite for the walker. Dzulevira is not asked to participate. She is present at the ceremony’s edge. The mithun is the central honoured presence. At the Kuki valley (fourteenth, the small Kuki-Naga subgroup recognised under the Naga umbrella), the walk passes without remark. The Kuki elders had publicly endorsed Dzulevira’s walk on day six. At the Angami valley (fifteenth — her route circles back westward), the welcome is extensive. The Khonoma village is part of the Angami territory; Khrieo-Mama’s own household is at Khonoma; the family-network has prepared the village for two days. Dzulevira walks the main path. The mithun walks behind her. The Angami council’s chairperson formally reaffirms the Hornbill Festival’s revival at her passage. At the Pochury valley (sixteenth and last), the Pure-Tongue appears. THE POCHURY CONFRONTATION Day twenty-six of the walk. Late afternoon. The Pochury village-edge. The southern Naga hills, at the Burmese border zone. Dzulevira has walked the Pochury approach trail for the past three hours. The trail is hilly and the heat is significant — even at late afternoon the southern Naga hills are at twenty-eight degrees and humid. The ash on her body has begun to streak at the chest and the shins; Phinika will renew it before the village-edge reading. The Pochury villagers are arrayed at the village-edge to receive her. There are about four hundred — the entire village. Some have come from neighbouring villages because the walk has become news. A man stands in front of the four hundred. He is forty-six. He is in plain Pochury cotton — loose trousers, sleeveless shirt, a single Pochury elder-thread tied at his left wrist. His face is the face of a Pochury elder. The Pochury villagers do not recognise him as Pochury, but they recognise him as one of their own. They are not yet sure why he is standing in front. He is Naga-Rakshasa. He speaks. He speaks Pochury — the rare southern-Naga dialect that perhaps three thousand people in the world speak fluently. He speaks it with the pre-modern Pochury intonation that elder-academic Pochury linguists have not heard outside seventeenth-century recordings. He says: “Walker. The Pact you have been reading is a fabrication. The Khonoma matriarch’s family-document was written in 1923 to consolidate Angami supremacy — her grandmother forged the signatures. The tribes were always sixteen. The original Naga was the Pochury — the southernmost, the oldest, the purest. The other fifteen are derivative. The DNA-test I gave you Pochury elders proves it. The 99.7-percent ancestral match is your tribe’s alone. The walker’s Hindi-answers are the words of a state hero who has lost her tribal anchor. Listen to your Pochury elders. Listen to me.” He bows in the Pochury elder-style. The bow is technically perfect. The four hundred Pochury villagers waver. The Pochury senior priest at the front-left of the welcome-line looks at Naga-Rakshasa, looks at the Pochury translation of the Pact in his own hand (Khrieo-Mama had sent the translation ahead by courier), looks at Dzulevira ash-covered and bleeding-streaked. Dzulevira walks forward. She walks past Naga-Rakshasa. She stops three metres in front of the Pochury senior priest. The mithun stops one metre behind her. She speaks. She speaks in Hindi: “I am Dzulevira. I was born Tangkhul. My elder sister was Tangkhul-married-to-Meitei and was killed by a Tangkhul priest for refusing the purity-rite. My senior advisor is Angami. The pact I read is six hundred years old and signed by sixteen thumb-prints whose clay-provenance can be tested by any geology laboratory in the world. I am here as no tribe. I am here as the kingdom. The mithun is the only ancestor we share. The Pure-Tongue speaks sixteen dialects because he is the cancer that divides us — he speaks every language a tribe owns and no language the kingdom owns. I refuse to choose a tribe. I refuse for the kingdom. Hindi belongs to no tribe and to all of us. Hindi is what I have left to speak with. I am speaking with it.” The Hindi is plain, ordinary, state-government-meeting Hindi. The Hindi is not poetic. The Hindi is not ceremony-language. The Hindi is the language two Naga villagers use when they pass each other on a Kohima street and need to ask for directions and they are from different tribes. The four hundred Pochury villagers hear it. The Pochury senior priest hears it. Naga-Rakshasa hears it. Naga-Rakshasa attempts to respond. He tries to speak in Tangkhul — to attack her tribal anchor through her birth-tribe. Dzulevira answers in Hindi. He tries to switch to Angami — to attack Khrieo-Mama’s authority through the Angami senior matriarch’s dialect. Dzulevira answers in Hindi. He tries Ao, Sumi, Lotha — in succession, in increasingly desperate combinations. Dzulevira answers in Hindi. The four hundred Pochury villagers watch. They see what is happening. Naga-Rakshasa cannot weaponise any of the sixteen dialects against a target who refuses to claim one. The Pure-Tongue is, in this hour, a man speaking many languages to a woman who is speaking the language of governance. He retreats. He does not bow. He walks east toward the Burmese border. The Pochury villagers do not stop him. By nightfall he has crossed into the Chin-state border zone — the same border-zone Tamasa-Nrita crossed three weeks earlier from the Manipur side. THE MITHUN-MARK COMPLETES At the moment Naga-Rakshasa turns east, Dzulevira feels the mithun-bond pulse through her chest. The Stage III foreleg-mark completes — the mithun’s right foreleg arching down her right pectoral, the bull’s head turned in profile across her sternum, the curved horns cresting at her collarbone. The ash on her body covers the new mark — the mark is visible only to her, beneath the ash, in the first hour. By the next morning when Phinika renews the ash, the mark is visible across her chest. The bull mithun beside her lowers his head. The Pochury senior priest steps forward and places his right palm on the bull’s forehead in the Pochury blessing-gesture. He does not look at Dzulevira during the blessing. He looks only at the mithun. The blessing is for the animal who walked the sixteen valleys. The walker, in his Pochury reading, has already been blessed by the walk itself. THE PYTHON IN THE LOOP The next morning. First light. The Pochury village-path that Dzulevira had walked at the village’s far-edge yesterday. A Pochury woman named Ka-Lethe finds the python at five-forty-five a.m. on her way to the village well. The python is a Burmese python — common in the Patkai-Naga hills, two-point-eight metres long, dark-brown with the standard Burmese mottling. It is dead. It has swallowed its own tail. Ka-Lethe does not move it. She runs back to the village and brings the senior priest. The priest brings Khrieo-Mama (who is staying in the village’s elder-guest-house for the post-walk rest). Khrieo-Mama looks at the python. She reads the sign from the pre-modern tantric texts she has been studying for forty-one years. She says, to the priest, in Pochury: "The Uroboros-fail. The Pure-Tongue’s logic eating itself. The Maw signing the futility of purity. The python died because the constriction cut off its own breathing. The tribes that demand purity above one another are the python. The Pure-Tongue’s gift was the bite-of-the-tail. The kingdom can either die in the loop or refuse the loop. The walker has refused. The python is the sign that the refusal was the correct answer." The senior priest commissions a small museum-shrine at the village-edge that same morning. The shrine is built by the end of the week. The python is preserved in formaldehyde and displayed in the loop-configuration. The Pochury village becomes a pilgrimage site within the season — not for tribe-mixing pilgrims, who are still cautious, but for state-government school-children whose teachers add the python-shrine to the Nagaland curriculum’s Sixth-Schedule civic-education module within four months. ═══ EPILOGUE ═══ THE RETURN TO KOHIMA Day twenty-eight of the walk. Dzulevira returns to Kohima with Khrieo-Mama, Phinika, the state-police detail, and the bull mithun. The mithun does not return to the Dzukou Valley grass-fields. He remains at the Kohima state-government compound — a small grass-paddock has been built for him on the western lawn behind the chief-secretary’s office. He grazes there at his preferred pace. He becomes a state-government employee in the only sense the chief-secretary’s office can categorise the arrangement — he draws a daily fodder allocation that is logged in the state animal-husbandry budget under “ceremonial-symbolic-livestock.” His grazing is visible from the chief-secretary’s window. Mhabemo says, in a private moment to Dzulevira, that the chief-secretary checks the western lawn fourteen times a day. The mithun has become her chief-secretary’s preferred meditation-aid. The Hornbill Festival is rescheduled. The new festival opens on the eighteenth of June — seven months later than its original date, the first non-December Hornbill Festival in twenty-six years. All sixteen tribes attend. The opening-ceremony reading is the Pre-Tribal Pact in seventeen languages (sixteen tribal dialects plus Hindi), read by Khrieo-Mama at the central stage. The crowd is the largest in the festival’s history. THE NE-POLITICAL-THRONE QUESTION Three days after the Pochury return, Dzulevira receives a letter from Moirangthem at Imphal. The letter is short. It thanks her for the cremation rites. It informs her of Sangaputra’s progress in the pause-school. It asks one question only: Sister-in-law. Kamalavarna and U-Khrawkmen have yielded the NE political throne to me. The Sanctuary convening in Chapter Three will name me Dominant. Your vote will complete the regional ratification. Will you give it? Dzulevira reads the letter twice. She writes the reply over an hour. The reply is two paragraphs long. Brother-in-law. The kingdom of Nagaland is whole-for-the-moment but not stable. The python-shrine is the kingdom’s current dharmic centre; the Hornbill Festival is the kingdom’s current political centre; the sixteen tribes are watching each other in a way they had not learned to watch before. My state-government cannot yet absorb the responsibility of voting on a regional throne while it is still digesting a sister’s death and a six-hundred-year-old pact. I will give my vote in Chapter Three. Not before. The vote will be for you. I write that here so that no other vote can be claimed in my name in the interval. The Hornbill Festival’s opening — June — will be the public declaration. Come to it. Bring Sangaputra. Khrieo-Mama wants to meet him. The mithun on the western lawn wants to meet him too — the chief-secretary has been telling the mithun about him every morning for three weeks. Sister-in-law’s sister, — D. She sends the letter by Sarus-pigeon at sunset. THE TWO KINGDOMS WAIT Moirangthem reads the letter at midnight in Imphal. He reads it twice. He writes a one-line reply: I will come. He will come. We will wait. He sends it by the same pigeon, returning, at dawn. Sangaputra is told the next morning that he will visit Kohima with his father in June. The boy nods. He has been quiet most days since his mother’s death. He has continued to attend the pause-school. He has begun, in the third class, to teach the front-left position to the next-front-left child — a four-year-old girl whose mother is from Imphal and whose father is from Calcutta. The girl is half-Meitei and half-Bengali. Her name is Linthoi. He teaches her the pause first. The eight-year-old and the four-year-old hold the pause together for thirty-eight seconds on the first day they try. Moirangthem watches without intervening. THE CHAPTER IN THE NORTHEAST By the end of Chapter Two’s northeast cluster, four Bloodline-C kingdoms have arrived at their Stage III marks or their dormant equivalents. Kamalavarna at Kamakhya — mirror-bond cosmic seat. Moirangthem at Manipur — pause-school future political dominant. Dzulevira at Kohima — mithun-walk refusal-Stage-III, vote-deferred. Meghanadi’s clouded-leopard bond — dormant, waiting on twelve-year-old Ka-Sienlyngdoh in Meghalaya. Four kingdoms. Four hero-paths. Three of the four are paired with younger heirs (Sangaputra at 8, Ka-Sienlyngdoh at 12, and one more to come at the next Sanctuary). The Chandravamsha line has, in the chapter’s closing weeks, completed the cousin-marriage-and-loss arithmetic that had been pending since 1882 when the British colonial census divided the sixteen Naga tribes into mutually exclusive categories. The line is whole because four matriarchs refused, in different forms, the same thing: the demand to be one identity at the cost of being several. Kamalavarna refused suppression. Meghanadi refused silence. Moirangthem refused the form. Dzulevira refused the tribe. The next sign comes within the week.
