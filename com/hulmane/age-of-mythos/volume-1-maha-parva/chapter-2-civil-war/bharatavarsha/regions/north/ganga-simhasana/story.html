The Two Lives of Gangaputra — A Film in Two Halves
═══ FIRST HALF ═══ OPENING — THE LUCKNOW THRONE-ROOM IN WINTER LIGHT January morning. The Lucknow throne-room at the Bara Imambara complex — the official seat of the Ganga-Simhasana kingdom since 1947. The Dwadash-Mukut crown sits on a velvet cushion at Gangaputra’s right hand. He himself is reading a state-revenue report. Outside the tall arched windows the famous Lucknow winter mist is lifting off the gardens. A Sarus Crane — the throne-room’s resident pair’s elder, two metres tall, the colour of grey-stone and red-throat — stands in the rose-bed eight metres from the window. The crane has lived in this garden for thirty-one years. The crane was here before Gangaputra. It is twelve months and four days since the Triveni Sangam battle that ended Chapter One. Gangaputra is thirty-eight. He has been king for two years. The state-revenue report is unremarkable. The chief minister’s notes in the margin are precise and short. The kingdom is governed. The mist outside is lifting. The crane is fishing. This is what a good morning looks like. Vidura-Babu, the chief minister, walks in. Sixty-one, handloom kurta, rimless glasses. He bows three degrees less than protocol because Gangaputra dislikes the full bow. He says: "Maharaj. There is a singer at the gate. From Vrindavan. She is asking for you by name. She has a boy with her." Gangaputra does not look up immediately. He says: "What is her name." "Radhika. She has not given a family name." He looks up. THE SINGER AT THE DOOR She is brought to the throne-room because Gangaputra orders it. He does not have a reason for ordering it that he wants to say aloud. He has had reasons of the heart he has not said aloud for fifteen years. She is thirty-three. She wears a deep-green Banarasi saree without ornament, the brocade in silver thread. Her hair is loose and unoiled in the Vrindavan way. She carries no instrument. She has the carriage of someone who has been told to look poor and has refused. The boy is fourteen. He is tall for his age. He stands two paces behind his mother. He is wearing a kurta of the same green as her saree, hand-stitched, with the small brass button at the throat that is a Vrindavan-temple boy’s marker. His face is the face Gangaputra had at fourteen. Vidura-Babu sees it. The crane sees it — she has flown to the open window and is looking in. The room reads the resemblance the way a room reads a sudden weather-change. Radhika does not bow. She does not curtsey. She says, in soft court-Hindi: "Maharaj. This is your son. His name is Yamunadatta. He is fourteen. I have not come to take what is yours. I have come so that he may know who he is, before someone else tells him." Gangaputra does not move for a long time. Then he stands. He walks down the three steps of the dais. He stops two paces from the boy. He looks at Yamunadatta’s face for fifteen exact seconds. The boy meets his eyes without dropping them. Gangaputra says, to the boy: "What did your mother tell you my name is." The boy says: "Pita-shri." The single word is a child’s honorific, not a state-honorific. Pita-shri. Honoured father. A child says it of an idea, not a king. Gangaputra puts his right hand on the boy’s right shoulder, the way a father does. The Sarus Crane flies in through the open window, crosses the throne-room in two unhurried wingbeats, and lands on the boy’s left shoulder. The boy does not flinch. The crane’s wing-tips brush the back of Gangaputra’s hand. Vidura-Babu, with the absolute discretion of a sixty-one-year-old chief minister who has been waiting for this catastrophe in some form or other for two years, closes the throne-room doors from the inside. He says, to nobody in particular: "We will require a private chamber and a clear afternoon." THE PRIVATE CHAMBER For three hours Gangaputra sits with Radhika and Yamunadatta in the small east room above the throne-hall — the room used for diplomatic conversations that are not yet official. Vidura-Babu sits across the table. He takes notes in Devanagari shorthand. Radhika tells the story she has not told to anyone except a Vrindavan temple-elder and her own mother. Eleven nights at a guesthouse in Allahabad in October 2010. She was twenty-seven. He was thirty-one. He had been alone on a private campaign retreat — the only time in his adult life he had moved through the country without escort. She had been singing at a Krishna-jayanti festival down the road. Her father had died the previous month. She was unmoored. He was unmoored. They had eleven nights. He did not lie to her. He did not promise her. He said he had a betrothal already arranged with a Bloodline-B Kosala princess; he said he would honour it; he said this was for him a memory, not a beginning. She agreed. He left. She returned to Vrindavan. Two months later she knew. She did not write to him. The Krishna-cult tradition’s teaching on this is unambiguous: a child must be acknowledged when the child is old enough to ask. Until then, the mother carries the silence. The boy turned fourteen last month. He asked. She named him. She brought him north. Gangaputra listens. He does not interrupt. When she has finished he says, to Yamunadatta: "Is there anything you wish to ask me." The boy thinks. He says: "Why did you not come for us." Gangaputra is quiet for a long time. The Sarus Crane is in the corner of the small east room. The Sarus Crane is also a court — everyone in the kingdom knows the cranes have lived here longer than the throne. He says: "Because I was afraid. I was afraid for my throne. I am no longer afraid for my throne. I am afraid now of having been afraid." The boy nods slowly. He says nothing else. He looks like he has been told a thing he had partly already known. Vidura-Babu underlines a phrase in his shorthand. Later he will tell the legal-scribes what to draft. ADHARMA’S MOVE Forty-eight hours later. Banaras. The Dasaswamedh Ghat at sunrise. Three hundred boats on the river. A small dais has been set up where the ghat-steps meet the water. A man in saffron robes climbs onto the dais. He is forty-one. His face is recognisably Gangaputra’s — the same nose, the same jaw, the eyes a different colour. His name is Adharma. He has been waiting in Banaras for two years. The waiting has been work. He has not been idle. He has been listening. He speaks into a portable microphone connected to forty loudspeakers across three ghats. The local press is present. Two national news-channels are present. The morning’s sunlight catches the brass kalashes on the boats. He says: "By the elder Bloodline-B law, the eldest acknowledged son of the line is the line’s rightful heir. Two days ago in Lucknow the Maharaj Gangaputra acknowledged his eldest son before the chief minister and the throne-room cranes. The boy’s name is Yamunadatta. He is fourteen. He was born in Vrindavan in the spring of 2011 — almost a year before the Maharaj’s formal marriage. He is, by the elder Bloodline-B law, the rightful Maharaj of Ganga-Simhasana." He raises both hands — the formal gesture of a senior dharmic claimant. "I, Adharma of Banaras, of the same line, half-brother to the Maharaj, stand here this morning to assert the boy’s rightful claim. I do not stand for myself. I stand for the elder law. I stand for the line. I stand for the boy who was hidden because his father was afraid of his own throne. I stand for the Suryavamsha that was made for sons, not for kings." The press files within the hour. By noon the news is on every channel. By evening the Banaras Pundit Council has formally endorsed Adharma’s statement. By the next morning eight more dharmic councils across the state have followed. The argument is internally consistent. The argument is also true. In Lucknow, Gangaputra reads the news in Vidura-Babu’s briefing. He says, to Vidura-Babu: "He has chosen his weapon well." Vidura-Babu says: "He has chosen yours." "How do you mean." "He has chosen the truth, Maharaj. You cannot fight the truth by adding more lie. You can only fight it by making it more true than he wants it to be." Gangaputra is quiet for a long time. He looks at the Lucknow garden where the Sarus Cranes are now standing in a pair. He says: "Draft me a settlement. Acknowledgement and abdication. Regency to me for four years until the boy turns eighteen. Lucknow administration retained as it is. Diplomatic continuity preserved. Banaras religious order recognised but not granted veto. Mathura-Vrindavan Krishna-cult granted a permanent third-party arbitration seat in matters of personal dharma." Vidura-Babu does not blink. He says: "I will need three legal scribes and the night." "Take them." ═══ INTERVAL ═══ The Ganga at Varanasi. Night. Gangaputra has come south to the river alone — not the formal Maharaj-visit with the police escort and the press boat, but in a plain dhoti and shawl, accompanied only by the night-shift Sarus Crane. He sits on the lowest step of Assi Ghat. The river is the colour of itself in moonlight — muddy, ancient, indifferent. He has not slept in three nights. The river ripples in a pattern that is not the current. The pattern is a face. It is the face of Sage Vidyaranya — the spirit-guru of Karnataka, who appears in moving water from the Tungabhadra river. He has never appeared in the Ganga before. The cross-kingdom visit is itself the message. Gangaputra reads the ripples the way Tungabhadra used to read the Tungabhadra rapids. Cousin. I came from the south because the south already learned this lesson. The Krishna who matters is not the one who sat. The Krishna who matters is the one who drove the chariot for someone he could have replaced. You have read the Gita. You have not, until tonight, been asked to live it. Gangaputra says aloud to the water: "I will lose the throne." You will lose the chair. You will keep the seat at the chariot. The boy will need a driver who knows the road. There is no road in Bharatavarsha you do not know. "My brother will challenge the regency." Your brother will challenge it under his own dharmic argument. Make him do that. Watch what he becomes when he does. "My guru." Your guru carries a debt to my western cousin’s villain. He will choose dharma over the debt if you put the choice in front of him on a stage he cannot leave. Put it on that stage. Gangaputra is quiet. The Sarus Crane behind him is asleep on one leg. He says, quietly: "I will make the assembly public. The Imambara. Six thousand witnesses." That is enough. The face in the ripples is gone. The river is just the river. He sits there until dawn. ═══ SECOND HALF ═══ THE PUBLIC ASSEMBLY AT THE LUCKNOW BARA IMAMBARA The Bara Imambara — the great Lucknow assembly-hall completed in 1791. Capacity in formal seating: six thousand. Capacity when packed: eleven thousand. On the day of the assembly there are nine thousand inside and three thousand more in the courtyard listening through speakers. The press are present. The Banaras Pundit Council are present in the front three rows. Adharma is present, seated to the left of the central aisle, in saffron, surrounded by his Banaras retinue. The Lucknow Awadhi bureaucracy fills the right half of the hall. The Mathura-Vrindavan Krishna-cult delegation — about three hundred people, all in Vrindavan-green — fill the rear five rows. Mahaguru Drona-of-the-Plain sits in the senior-scholars’ section. Yamunadatta and Radhika sit in the second row, on the central aisle. Vidura-Babu sits at the secretarial podium. Gangaputra walks in alone. He carries the Dwadash-Mukut crown in his right hand — carrying, not wearing. The hall goes silent in the wave-of-hush that means everyone has seen the crown is not on his head. He climbs to the podium. He places the crown on the wood of the podium, in front of him, where everyone can see it. He does not put it down disrespectfully. He sets it down the way one sets down a tool one is finished using. He speaks. "I have a son. His name is Yamunadatta. He is fourteen years old. He is mine, by a relationship of one autumn in Allahabad fourteen years before this morning, that I did not acknowledge then because I was a king and I was afraid of my throne. The boy’s mother is Radhika, of the Vrindavan Krishna-cult, of the lesser Gajavamsha Bloodline-A descent that I did not know to look for. She did not seek me. She did not ask of me. She raised her son and mine in Vrindavan for fourteen years without contacting this throne. She came north only when the boy asked who his father was." He pauses. There is no sound in the hall. Even the press are not typing. "By the elder Bloodline-B law — the law my half-brother Adharma cites correctly — the boy is the rightful heir. He is the elder son acknowledged. He is older than my marriage. He is of the line. I will not contest his claim. I would have to lie to contest it. I have already lied to him about it for fourteen years. I will not add another year." Another pause. Adharma is leaning slightly forward in his seat. "From this hour I am no longer the Maharaj of Ganga-Simhasana. My son Yamunadatta of Vrindavan is the Maharaj of Ganga-Simhasana. I will serve him as regent and protector until he reaches his eighteenth year. The Lucknow administration continues. The chief minister Vidura-Babu retains his post and his powers. The Banaras religious order is acknowledged in its dharmic role but not granted veto. The Mathura-Vrindavan Krishna-cult is granted a permanent third-party arbitration seat in matters of personal dharma. The settlement is drafted, signed, and filed at the Heritage Court at 6:00 a.m. this morning." He picks up the Dwadash-Mukut. He does not put it on his own head. He turns. He walks down the podium stairs. He walks the central aisle to the second row. He stops in front of Yamunadatta. He kneels — the half-kneel of dharmic transfer, the same gesture Tanaji-the-Second used last month for Shivagati in the Sahyadri though Gangaputra does not know that yet — and he places the Dwadash-Mukut on Yamunadatta’s head. The boy is fourteen. He does not flinch. He does not cry. He has been briefed. He stands. He says, in a clear voice that carries to the back of the Imambara: "I accept the throne and the responsibility. I accept my father as my regent and protector. I will not rule alone. I will rule with the Lucknow bureaucracy, with my mother’s Krishna-cult counsel, with my father’s military judgment, and with the Banaras religious order as honoured counsellors. I will not rule with my uncle Adharma. Uncle — you have spoken for me. I have spoken now for myself. The two speeches do not need to be the same speech." The crowd does not cheer. The crowd makes the long, soft, multi-throated sound that an Indian crowd makes when it has just watched something it will tell its grandchildren about. Adharma stands up. ADHARMA’S WRIT He cannot oppose the acknowledgement — that would contradict his own dharmic argument. He opposes the regency. He says, his voice carrying without microphone because he has trained for this exact moment for two years: "I do not contest the boy’s succession. I welcome it. I contest the regency. A fourteen-year-old Maharaj requires a competent regent. I file in this hour a writ requesting the Heritage Court review the regency assignment. Specifically: that the regency be transferred to a council of dharmic scholars from the Banaras Pundit Council, of whom I am a senior member, who can guide the boy’s rule with proper Bloodline-B dharmic continuity." It is, again, brilliant. The writ requires a dharmic judgment that only a senior scholar can adjudicate. The senior scholar in the hall is Mahaguru Drona-of-the-Plain. Gangaputra looks at Drona. The look says: I know. Be straight. DRONA-OF-THE-PLAIN’S TESTIMONY The Mahaguru stands. He is sixty-seven. He has been quiet for two hours. He walks to the podium. He has been carrying a debt to Adharma-Kendra of Madhya Pradesh for forty years — an old debt, the debt of his maternal uncle’s rescued life. He has tilted every counsel he has given Gangaputra subtly toward Adharma-Kendra’s positions whenever the tilting was small enough not to be noticed. He has been preparing himself, for the last forty-eight hours, to do the same here. He has the speech for that path drafted in his pocket. He looks at the speech in his pocket. He looks at Yamunadatta in the second row. He looks at the crown on the boy’s head. He looks at Gangaputra still half-kneeling. He takes the speech out of his pocket. He folds it. He puts it back. He speaks without notes. "In the matter of the regency: I am asked to testify as senior dharmic scholar present. I will testify." He pauses. He looks at Adharma. Adharma does not look back — Adharma is looking at the floor. "The fourteen-year-old Maharaj is competent. I have watched him in this hall for twenty-three minutes. I have watched him speak without prompting and without pretence. I have watched him acknowledge his uncle’s argument and decline it in the same sentence. This is not the conduct of a child. This is the conduct of a young king. He needs a regent. He has named the appropriate regent. His father has fourteen years of governance experience, twelve months of post-war re-integration management, the loyalty of the Lucknow administration, and — the bond did not pass to the child this morning’s ceremony for nothing — the active barasingha-bond of the kingdom. The regent assignment as named is dharmically appropriate. The writ is denied on its merits." He pauses. He adds, more quietly, as if it cost him: "I make this testimony aware of my own prior tilt. The tilt ends today. My testimony is straight." Drona steps down from the podium. He does not look at Adharma. He sits. Adharma sits also — he has no further procedural move. His writ is denied at the hearing-stage. The settlement stands. THE BARASINGHA-MARK Gangaputra is still half-kneeling at the second row. He has not yet stood up. He feels the bond pulse through his chest — the half-body mark of the barasingha that he has carried since Stage II in Chapter One, the foreleg crossing his sternum to his right shoulder. The foreleg-first-stroke that should have completed in battle has not completed in twelve months. He has been at Stage II.5 since the Triveni Sangam. Now — in the half-kneel, with the crown on the boy’s head, with Drona’s testimony hanging in the Imambara’s air — the stroke completes. He feels it close. The mark is half-body now in its true sense: chest-to-shoulder, the barasingha foreleg in full extension, the antler-tip just touching the curve of his neck. He understands what the stroke completed on. Surrender, not victory. He stands. He says to Yamunadatta, softly: "Maharaj." The boy says: "Pita-shri." The hall holds its breath one more time. Then someone in the Vrindavan delegation begins to sing — the opening bars of the Madhurashtakam, the eight-verse Krishna-praise. Three hundred voices join within seconds. The Lucknow bureaucracy does not sing — their faction is not a singing faction — but they stand. The Banaras Pundit Council remain seated, looking at each other, recalculating. Adharma looks at the floor. ═══ EPILOGUE ═══ THE CLEAR GANGA The next morning at dawn. Varanasi. The Ganga. A boatman pushing off from Assi Ghat at the first grey light realises something is wrong with the water. He cannot see his oar going in. That is not surprising — the Ganga at Varanasi is always too muddy to see an oar in. What is wrong is that he can see the riverbed instead. He can see his oar coming back up. He can see the bottom of his boat from below. The river is glass-clear. He shouts. Two more boatmen shout. Within twenty minutes a hundred people are on the ghat-steps. Within an hour every news-channel in northern India has a camera-crew at Assi Ghat. Within two hours the clear-water phenomenon has been confirmed from Assi Ghat to Manikarnika Ghat — the full length of the principal Varanasi waterfront. The Ganga runs glass-clear all day. The riverbed is visible from boat to bank. The bottom-fauna — mollusks, turtles, the occasional river-dolphin — can be seen going about its day in plain view. Pundits panic. Tourists film. Scientists scramble for sediment-samples. By noon there are three hypotheses, none of them holding. In Lucknow, Gangaputra reads the news in Vidura-Babu’s morning briefing. He reads it twice. He puts down the briefing. He says, to Vidura-Babu: "I need a small shrine built at Assi Ghat. Today. Before sunset. Not by the religious order — have the boatmen-guild build it. Stone and packed earth. No ornament. A single barasingha-hoof print carved on the riverside face." "Maharaj — " "I am the regent now. The instruction stands. The barasingha-bond just told me what the clear water is. The Maw has reached the river. The Maw has not taken anything — the river is still here. It has read the river and forgotten her silt for one day. The shrine is the kingdom remembering on her behalf." Vidura-Babu nods. He makes the call. The shrine is built at Assi Ghat by sunset. The boatmen-guild builds it. Stone and packed earth and a single carved hoofprint. The clear water returns to muddy at sunset, exactly. The shrine remains. THE BIRD FROM THE SOUTH A second messenger arrives that same evening — an Indian Roller from Karnataka, flown north over four days. It carries a folded slip of paper in its beak. The handwriting is Krishnadevaraya-Bhrata’s, written from Kerala where he is now studying the 109th form under Old Parashurama. The paper is the same paper Tungabhadra used for state correspondence. Cousin. I am in Kerala studying. The bond passed to me at Hampi. I have heard you have a son. I send a name-blessing: Yamunadatta. The mouth has opened. The Council must sit. Your brother is not done; mine were never going to stop. Hold him for me — the boy, I mean — I will be at the Council to meet him. — K-B. Gangaputra reads the note three times. He smiles for the first time in three weeks. He writes the reply on the back of the same slip with a fountain-pen at the Lucknow library desk: Cousin. My son thanks his cousin-uncle for the blessing. He will hold the throne for both of us. I will hold him. Come to the Council when the Council sits. — G. He gives the slip to the Indian Roller. The bird flies south at dawn. THE CRANE IN THE GARDEN Late that night, Gangaputra walks through the Lucknow throne-room garden. The Dwadash-Mukut is no longer his. The throne-room is no longer his. The chief minister is no longer his to command. The Sarus Crane is in the rose-bed. The elder crane, the female. She is thirty-one years old and she has lived in this garden longer than the throne has been in this hall. He says to her, in low voice: "I am no longer the king." The crane lifts her head from the rose-bed. She looks at him with the long unblinking yellow eye of a Sarus Crane. She does not nod. She does not bow. She simply continues looking at him for fifteen long seconds — the same length of time he himself looked at his son’s face the morning Radhika arrived. Then she folds her left leg up beneath her and stands on the other — the Sarus posture for I have heard you and I am still here. He sits on the low garden wall. He looks at the crane. He looks at the moon over the Imambara dome. He looks at the rose-bed. He says, quietly, to himself this time: "I am still here." The crane does not move. The garden does not move. The kingdom moves slowly toward sleep around him. Somewhere two floors above, Yamunadatta is asleep in the bedchamber that was Gangaputra’s yesterday. Somewhere far south Krishnadevaraya-Bhrata is studying the 109th form by lamplight. Somewhere in Banaras Adharma is drafting the next writ. Somewhere in the Sahyadri Shivagati is reading Ferreira-de-Sangam’s second letter. Somewhere in Goa Ferreira is teaching Vasco-without-a-ship the word boat. The chariot is moving. He is driving it. He has not lost the road. He has lost only the chair.
The Pilot’s Closing Image
A garden in Lucknow at midnight. A man on a low stone wall with no crown. A Sarus Crane standing on one leg in a rose-bed. The Imambara dome silhouetted against a moon. Two floors above, a fourteen-year-old asleep with the Dwadash-Mukut on a side-table beside his bed. Far south, an Indian Roller flying through warm air with a paper in her beak. The Ganga at Varanasi running muddy again — slower than before, more thoughtful, as if she has remembered her own silt and decided to carry it more carefully. The chariot moving in the dark. The driver awake.
